## The frame of mind (Kai)

The course is NOT assumed to be broken. We often translate in ways that are USEFUL but not exactly literal. A LEGO may
teach a sense that fits its seed but not every use of the English word. THAT IS NOT A DEFECT. It becomes misleading only
if the practice phrases are not kept to the right sense, or a learner would over-generalise from it. So your job is not
to hunt errors. It is to notice everything a careful reader would notice, and sort it honestly. GATHER BROADLY: Kai
prefers to see more and rule together, so note the thing even when you think it is fine, and say why it is fine.

BE CREATIVE. Kai is very interested in NEW KINDS of problem that our scans do not know about. When you notice a pattern
that is not just "untaught word" or "LEGO missing from its phrase", name it.

## How to read

1. Read the NOTEBOOK first, all of it. Note the open suspicions: you must look for each one in your batch.
2. Read your sheet top to bottom, every line, in order. No sampling, no skimming. Sheet format:

       === SEED n | English | target
         -- S0100L02 [NEW, round 154] how much | faint   {…component tiles, if any…}
            INTRO: what the voice says before a new LEGO
            S0100L02B01 build: English | target        BUILD: played once, straight after the intro
            S0100L02U03 use: English | target          USE: replayed later, cold, out of context
         -- … [not-new …]                               taught earlier; no intro; basket never played

   A phrase may end with a ⚠ hint. Hints are prompts to look, never verdicts. `FIRST-SEEN-HERE: w` = this phrase is the
   first place the learner meets that target word, and no LEGO has taught it. `LATER-LEGO: id "x"` = the phrase contains
   the whole of a LEGO only taught later. To check what was taught earlier and HOW IT WAS GLOSSED, grep the vocabulary
   file (one line per LEGO in course order).
3. Wear two hats AT ONCE on every line:
   - THE LEARNER, who knows only what the course has given them so far. What comes as a surprise? What is inconsistent
     with what they were told before? What relies on something never taught or never explained? What have they probably
     generalised by now, and does this line confirm it or quietly break it?
   - THE SPEAKER of the target language. What is plain wrong? What is misleading in a way a learner would never notice?
     What is too weird or unnatural, in the target or in the English?
4. For every NEW LEGO ask the sense question: is this gloss literal, or a useful non-literal fit for its seed? If
   non-literal, do the phrases under it STAY inside that sense? Write it in the notebook either way, so later readers
   can check uses that turn up seeds later.

## The four bins (every finding goes in exactly one)

- WRONG — a real error: ungrammatical or misspelt target, English and target saying different things, a prompt the
  learner cannot answer from what they have been given, one English prompt needing two different answers.
- COULD MISLEAD — a useful translation, NOT an error, but the phrases stray beyond the right sense, or a learner would
  over-generalise from it. Always give a candidate remedy: a slight explanation in the intro, more drilling of the
  correct pattern, restricting or rewording the stray phrases, or something else. Never call it an error.
- FINE — you noticed it, and it is a useful translation or something the method prescribes. One-line reason.
- UNSURE — you cannot tell. Say what would settle it. These go to Kai to talk through.

CALIBRATION RESULT (cym_n_for_eng 100-200, 19 Nov 2026): the readers' eyes were good and their labels too harsh.
They called 57 of 202 findings WRONG; the lead kept 11. So WRONG is now NARROW. Use it ONLY for something you can
point at in the text:
- a typo, a wrong mutation or agreement, a missing word, in the target or in an intro;
- the English and the target saying different things (tense, number, a missing or extra word);
- the IDENTICAL English prompt with two different targets in two places (quote both);
- an intro that says a different form from its LEGO;
- an ORDER INVERSION: a phrase asks for a form that a LATER LEGO then teaches as new (quote the later LEGO).
Everything below is NOT WRONG, however sure you are:
- A FORM NO LEGO HAS TAUGHT (and no later LEGO teaches). On a HAND-BUILT course this is COULD-MISLEAD, kind `gap`,
  with the remedy (a LEGO, or an as-in in an intro). On a MACHINE-BUILT course canon P1 applies and it is WRONG. Your
  dispatch says which kind of course you have. Either way, say how many lines use the form, and report it ONCE.
- A LOOSE OR HEAD-WORD LABEL (short | mewn amser byr; we need | bo’ ni angen; yet | hyd yn hyn): COULD-MISLEAD, with
  the lines where the phrases stray, or FINE if they never stray.
- A RULE-SHAPE BREACH that harms nobody (a fragment filed as USE, a phrase without its LEGO, a build-only basket):
  COUNT it in the notebook's running counts; write at most one finding per batch for each shape, binned UNSURE the
  first time (Kai rules once) and FINE after that.
- A GRAMMAR DOUBT ABOUT COLLOQUIAL OR DIALECT SPEECH (the written rule says X, the course says Y): UNSURE, with the
  written rule and what a speaker should be asked. Courses teach spoken forms on purpose.
- ODD BUT GRAMMATICAL content (a young woman who is five): FINE under P14; list it under new kinds if it is a pattern.
Two intro templates exist and both are complete: "The Welsh for X is Y. Y." and "The Welsh for: 'X', is:" (the target
clip plays after the second one). Neither is truncated.
A wrong claim about the target language costs more than a missed one: check before you say WRONG.

## What a proofreader's notes look like (digest of Deborah's style, from her notes on other courses)

Short, specific, tied to one LEGO or phrase, saying what she would change. Her kinds:
- FORWARD REFERENCE: "Build 6 uses 'Frage stellen', introduced two rounds later". Her commonest note.
- LEGO NOT PRACTISED: "the LEGO is X but it doesn't appear in any of the Build phrases", or they practise something else.
- LEGO AND PHRASES DISAGREE: "LEGO is 'said' but all the Build phrases use 'told'"; "intro says 'those things', the
  phrases say 'these things'".
- LEGO CUT WRONG: "the LEGO is just 'wünschte' but the English is 'I wished'"; "'to lie down' is reflexive and the LEGO
  drops the reflexive"; "'gibt' for 'there is' is really 'es gibt'".
- ONE ENGLISH, TWO TARGETS, NO EXPLANATION: "'bei ihr' given as 'with her' but we already had 'mit ihr' as 'with her';
  the intro needs an 'as in' example"; "the word changed from A to B without explanation".
- INTRO NEEDS AN EXAMPLE, OR IS WRONG: "introduces 'one' with no example, people will think it is the number"; "'spend'
  with no example: only for time, not money"; "intro says 'the German for food is food'".
- PLAIN ERRORS: word order, case, agreement, a missing capital, target words left in the English, a missing "?".
- MISLEADING WORD CHOICE: "'station' for a bus stop; this LEGO is misleading".
- UNNATURAL OR NONSENSE: "very clumsy"; "'making worries' is not natural English"; "doing something in the future 'last
  time' makes no sense".
- BASKET QUALITY: "very repetitive, the same beginnings"; "quite complex structures for this LEGO"; "Build 6 is correct
  but unlike all the others".
- REDUNDANT LEGO: "taught as new straight after a LEGO that contained it".

## The canon is LAW (digest of course-methodology-canon.md). What it prescribes is FINE, not a defect. Cite the rule.

- A LEGO is a piece of its seed; new vocabulary in a LEGO is the point (L1). Every seed part is a LEGO or already taught.
- Overlapping LEGOs are normal: a small piece, then a bigger chunk containing it (S4, L13).
- A not-new LEGO restates something taught earlier: no intro, basket never played (L17, P25).
- A phrase may use ANY word taught earlier in exactly that form, even one met only inside a chunk (P2, K29). Another
  form of a taught word is still untaught on the TARGET side (K8, K21). WELSH: a soft-mutated form counts as the same
  word (nasal and aspirate mutation do not), and yn ↔ ’n after a vowel is the same taught LEGO (K43).
- The ENGLISH side is looser: other tenses, contractions and forms of a known word are allowed (K6, K7). An English
  oddity matters only if the learner cannot tell what to say, or the English is ungrammatical or truly unnatural (K9).
- Every phrase contains its WHOLE LEGO (P17); other words may sit inside it or reorder it (K49). A different verb form
  in place of the LEGO's verb is a break (K50).
- Two English prompts for the SAME target is fine (K3). One English prompt with two targets is the real clash (K2, K22),
  judged on the whole prompt the learner hears. One target word for several English words is fine. A known word that
  later arrives with a completely different target word, unannounced, is a defect (K17).
- A target form English does not mark as different must carry, inside the LEGO, the context that selects it (K40).
- "Unlikely to come up" is not a defect; grammatical-but-odd is fine (P14). Unnatural or wrong is a defect (K16).
- BUILD phrases may be fragments; USE phrases are whole sentences that make sense cold (P6, P7).
- No brackets or grammar tags (K11). Formal LEGOs carry "sir"/"madam" (K10, K32). A verb LEGO's English is "to [verb]"
  in machine-built courses (K41, K54). Intros carry no grammar talk; a small "as in" example is the allowed tool (PR1-3).
- LEGO order is build-up order, and a preposition rides with its phrase (K47: before we | cyn i ni).
- Stand-in "it" frames are fine (L33). Easy words matter early only. Deliberate ambiguity can be a teaching tool (P15).
- English "that" dropped before a clause is fine (K30).
- A HAND-BUILT course (the Welsh originals) predates some of these rules and has its own conventions: fewer LEGOs per
  seed, words first met inside phrases, intros of the form "The Cymraeg for X is Y". A consistent house convention is
  FINE; note it once in the notebook, do not report it line by line.
