# The 78 misfiled lessons, read one seed at a time

*What was changed, what was left alone, and why. 28 August 2026. Every line below was read against live database rows, not against any earlier document.*

## What I was sent to do, and what was actually there

The brief said 78 lessons were structurally wrong in one of two mechanical ways — either two lessons in the wrong order, or a single practice sentence filed under the wrong lesson — and that both could be repaired without touching a word of text.

Reading all 78 seeds whole, **that is not what is in the database.** Two cases are genuinely a misfiled sentence and I repaired both. Eight are not faults at all — the checker could not see a difference a learner would never hear. The remaining seventy are one single fault, and it is neither of the two I was sent to fix: **the lesson's own word is too long.**

### The shape of it, in one German seed

Seed 445 teaches *diesen Koffer* ("that suitcase") at lesson 1. Its practice ladder runs *diesen → diesen Ansatz → sie brauchen diesen Ansatz*. That is a correct, complete ladder — for **diesen**. Lesson 2 then teaches *Koffer*, and its ladder runs *Koffer → diesen Koffer → wir brauchen diesen Koffer*. Also correct.

Nothing is misfiled and nothing is out of order. The sentences are right where they belong. The **label on lesson 1 is wrong**: it names the phrase the learner will have finished building by the end of lesson 2, instead of the chunk lesson 1 actually teaches. Seventy of the seventy-eight are this, in seven languages.

### Why the order can never simply be swapped

This is the part that decides the whole job. In every one of the seventy, the sentence that contains the over-long lesson word sits in a **later** lesson — and it contains the later lesson's new word too. That is exactly why it matches. So moving it back, or swapping the two lessons round, would put a word in front of the learner before it has been introduced. Vocabulary accumulates in strict order and never runs ahead, so both of the mechanical fixes are ruled out by construction, not by taste.

Only three of the eighty had the sentence sitting in an **earlier** lesson, which is the one direction where a re-attach is legal. Two of those were real, and I repaired them. The third turned out to be a duplicated lesson label.

## The two repairs

Both in German. No text was altered, so no audio was invalidated — every moved row kept its own recording.

**Seed 465.** Lesson 1 teaches *nächstes Mal*; lesson 2 teaches *Nächstes Mal werde ich*. Two of lesson 1's build sentences — *nächstes Mal werde ich* and *nächstes Mal werde ich fragen* — used *werde*, which lesson 2 has not introduced yet. They were lesson 2's sentences sitting in lesson 1. **Moved to lesson 2**, where they now finish its ladder: werde → ich werde → ich werde fragen → nächstes Mal werde ich → nächstes Mal werde ich fragen. Lesson 1 is left with a clean two-word ladder and its own six sentences.

**Seed 568.** Lesson 2 teaches *nächsten*; lesson 3 teaches *beim nächsten Mal*. The sentence *beim nächsten Mal* — word for word lesson 3's own phrase — was filed under lesson 2, where it ran ahead to *Mal*. **Moved to lesson 3**, completing its ladder: mal → nächsten Mal → ich erwarte jedes Mal → beim nächsten Mal.

|---|---|---|
| nächstes Mal werde ich | German seed 465, lesson 1 | lesson 2 |
| nächstes Mal werde ich fragen | German seed 465, lesson 1 | lesson 2 |
| beim nächsten Mal | German seed 568, lesson 2 | lesson 3 |

## The eight that were never faults

Seven Arabic and one Mexican-Spanish case are the checker reading a difference the rule expressly tolerates. Arabic drills the shirt as القَميصُ and القَميصَ while the lesson is written القَميصِ — identical letters, a case ending grammar forces and speech drops. And Spanish seed 33 teaches *llevas aprendiendo* in sentences that say *llevas todo el día aprendiendo*: the chunk is interrupted, which is allowed, not absent.

## One thing worth more than the fixes

In **fourteen** of the seventy, the only lesson in the seed that ever practises the taught phrase is marked as not-new — and the word it carries was never introduced anywhere earlier in that course. Lessons in that state are real rows in the database and silent in delivery. So for those fourteen the learner is not merely being taught a phrase the sentences do not use; the one lesson that would have fixed it never reaches them. German 537 (*ich hatte unrecht*), Chinese 198 (市政府), Korean 643 and 647, Italian 308, and nine others are in this state.

There is also a small cluster of **duplicated lesson labels** — two lessons in one seed carrying the identical word, one of them with a ladder that plainly teaches something else. French 395 and 408, Arabic 419, English-for-Korean 61.

## What the seventy need, and why I did not do it

The repair for the seventy is to rename the lesson word to the chunk its ladder already teaches: *diesen Koffer* → *diesen*, *ein gutes Beispiel* → *gutes*, *نَبْقى هادِئينَ* → *هادِئينَ*. That is a change of text. Changing a lesson's text invalidates the recording of that lesson word, which means new audio — and audio spend is a separate approval that was not part of this job. It is also a content decision, not a mechanical one: the alternative repair is to keep the label and rewrite the sentences, which is a much larger job. That choice belongs to a human.

## How the eighty were found

The published analysis gave a count, not a list, so the set was rebuilt from live rows: every lesson in the priority courses whose own build sentences never contain its taught phrase, where another lesson in the same seed does. That reproduces 275 flagged lessons against the published 277, and 80 of them over 78 seeds against the published 78 — the small differences are punctuation and case-vowel handling in the matcher. Each of the 78 seeds was then read whole, every lesson and every sentence in order, before anything was decided.
