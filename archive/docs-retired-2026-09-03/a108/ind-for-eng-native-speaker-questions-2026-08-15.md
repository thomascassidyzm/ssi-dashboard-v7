# Indonesian for English speakers — questions for an Indonesian speaker

**2026-08-15.** Nobody who built this course speaks Indonesian. Every question below is
written so that an Indonesian speaker with **no knowledge of how our system works** can answer
it. You do not need to read any code, open any tool, or know what a "LEGO" is.

**BLOCKING** means the course currently teaches a guess, and learners are being taught that
guess right now. Those come first.

Where a question says "N phrases depend on this", that is the number of sentences already
written into the course that would have to be rewritten if the answer is "no".

---

## A. BLOCKING — Does *sebisa mungkin* work with "you", or only with "I"?

We teach **`sebisa mungkin`** to mean **"as hard as I can"**.

> *Saya mau coba sebisa mungkin hari ini.* — "I want to try as hard as I can today."

We think this literally says something like "as-able-as possible", which is why we have
deliberately kept it **out of every sentence with "you" as the subject** — we were afraid
that *"kamu coba sebisa mungkin"* would sound wrong or would mean "as hard as **I** can"
even with "you" in the sentence.

**Question:** Can you say *"Kamu coba sebisa mungkin"* to mean "you try as hard as **you**
can"? Or does *sebisa mungkin* always point back to the speaker?

**Why it matters:** if it works with "you", we can open up a whole class of sentences we are
currently avoiding. **9 phrases depend on this.**

## B. BLOCKING — Where does *sedikit* ("a little") go?

The existing translations put it **after** the thing, not before:

> *Saya bicara bahasa Indonesia **sedikit** sekarang.* — "I speak a little Indonesian now."

In English "a little" comes before ("a little Indonesian"). We have taught the Indonesian
order as-is, and built sentences on it.

**Question:** Is *"Saya bicara bahasa Indonesia sedikit"* what a person actually says? Or
would you say *"Saya bicara sedikit bahasa Indonesia"*? If both are fine, which is more
natural in relaxed speech?

**Why it matters:** **22 phrases depend on this**, and if it is wrong the word order is wrong
in every one of them.

## C. BLOCKING — Is *dengan* needed in front of adverbs?

Three separate "how" expressions in the course all use **`dengan`**:

| Course teaches | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *dengan sangat baik* | very well |
| *dengan cepat* | quickly |
| *dengan mudah* | easily |

**Question:** Would a person actually say *dengan* each time, or would they just say
*"sangat baik"*, *"cepat"*, *"mudah"* on their own? For example — is *"Kamu bicara bahasa
Indonesia dengan sangat baik"* natural, or would you just say *"Kamu bicara bahasa Indonesia
sangat baik"*?

**Why it matters:** **35 phrases** across three lessons use this pattern.

## D. BLOCKING — *kita* for "we": is it always right?

Every "we" in the whole 668-sentence course is **`kita`**. The word **`kami`** appears
**zero** times.

We understand there may be two words for "we" in Indonesian — one that includes the person
you are talking to, one that excludes them.

**Question:** In a course where the learner is always talking **to** one other person, is
*kita* always the right choice? Is there any sentence where *kami* is what a person would
really say? For example: *"Kita mau ketemu jam enam malam ini"* — "We want to meet at six
o'clock this evening", said to the person you are meeting.

**Why it matters:** 42 of the 668 sentences use *kita*; 24 phrases are already built on it.

## E. BLOCKING — Is *jawaban* a real word on its own?

The course sentence is *"Dia mau cari tahu apa **jawabannya**."* — "She wants to find out what
the answer is."

We also teach the shorter piece **`jawaban`** on its own, meaning "answer", so that learners
can see how *jawabannya* is built up.

**Question:** Does anybody say *jawaban* by itself? Is it a normal word, or does it only ever
appear with something attached to it?

**Why it matters:** if nobody says it alone, we should delete that piece — it would be
teaching a word that does not exist in speech.

## F. Is the bare-verb style right throughout?

The whole course deliberately uses short, bare verb forms rather than the longer prefixed
ones. We checked all 668 sentences and the corpus is completely consistent — the longer forms
appear **zero** times:

| Course always uses | Never uses | How often |
|---|---|---|
| *bicara* | *berbicara* | 33 sentences |
| *coba* | *mencoba* | 22 |
| *bilang* | *mengatakan* | 70 |
| *ketemu* | *bertemu* | 16 |
| *latihan* | *berlatih* | 3 |
| *pikir* | *berpikir* | 51 |
| *gimana* | *bagaimana* | 10 |
| *kamu* | *anda* | 139 |

**Question:** Go down that list and mark any that sound **wrong** in relaxed conversation
between two people who know each other. We are not asking which is more correct in writing —
we are asking which one a person actually says out loud.

**Also:** *`jelaskan`* ("explain"), *`tebak`* ("guess"), *`bantu`* ("help"), *`tanya`*
("ask") are used the same bare way. Do any of those sound wrong without a prefix?

## G. A word we invented a "piece" for: *nama* / *namanya*

The course sentence is *"Kamu mau belajar **namanya** dengan cepat."* — "You want to learn his
name quickly."

To help learners see how *namanya* is put together, we also teach **`nama`** on its own,
meaning "name" — even though *nama* alone does not appear in that sentence.

**Question:** Is *nama* on its own a normal, everyday word? Is *"Saya mau belajar nama"* a
sentence a person would say, or does it sound incomplete?

## I. *apakah* at the start of questions

The course opens every yes/no question with **`apakah`** — *"Apakah kamu bicara bahasa
Indonesia sepanjang hari?"* This was a deliberate choice so learners have one predictable way
to ask a question instead of relying on tone of voice.

**Question:** Do people actually say *apakah* out loud in ordinary conversation, or is it
mostly a written/formal thing? If it sounds stiff, how bad is it — mildly formal, or
odd-sounding?

We also use *apakah* in the middle of a sentence to mean "whether": *"Saya tidak yakin
**apakah** saya bisa ingat seluruh kalimat itu"* — "I'm not sure whether I can remember the
whole sentence." **Question:** is that second use natural?

## M. A genuine inconsistency in the existing translations — "better"

This one is not about anything we built; it is about the 668 translations that already
existed. The English word **"better"** is translated **two different ways**:

| Seed | English | Indonesian |
|---|---|---|
| **29** | "…speaking **better** as soon as I can" | *bicara lebih **bagus** secepat mungkin* |
| **291** | "I hope I'll be able to speak **better** soon" | *bicara lebih **baik** segera* |
| 42, 117, 118, 401 | "feel better", "doing better", "it would be better" | *lebih **baik*** |

Six sentences use *lebih baik*; **one** (seed 29) uses *lebih bagus*, and it is the one that
is closest in meaning to seed 291.

**Question:** Should seed 29 be changed to *lebih baik* so the course is consistent, or is
*lebih bagus* genuinely better for "speaking better"? If they mean different things, please
say what the difference is.

**Status:** seed 29 is **not yet built**, so nothing has to be rewritten if you change it —
this is the cheapest possible moment to decide.

## N. He and she are the same word — is that ever a problem?

Indonesian *dia* covers both "he" and "she", so the course teaches:

> "He wants to come back…" → *Dia mau kembali…*
> "She wants to find out…" → *Dia mau cari tahu…*

We know this is a real feature of the language and we are not trying to change it.

**Question:** Is there anywhere in ordinary conversation where losing the he/she distinction
would actually confuse someone, and where a speaker would add something to make it clear?
