# The "try" ruling is applied to Donegal, and the coinage is gone

**2026-08-21. `gle_ul_for_eng` (Ulster / Donegal). 16 seed translations rewritten, 6 deliberately
left alone. No teaching units, no practice phrases and no audio existed on this course, so seeds
were the entire surface. £0.00 spent — zero TTS, zero clips, zero audio rows before and after.**

---

## What was decided, and by whom

The ruling was already made and I did not reopen it. Donegal says:

| English frame | Donegal form |
|---|---|
| **"trying to …"** | **`ag iarraidh`** + verbal noun |
| **"to try" / "tried" / no complement** | **`iarracht`** with a light verb — `rinne … iarracht`, `iarracht a dhéanamh` |
| **"going to try to …"** | **`ag gabháil a dhéanamh iarracht`** — nominative, never genitive `iarrachta` |
| ~~`ag iarracht`~~ | **banned everywhere — a coinage, not a form** |

The evidence, restated only so the numbers travel with the change: in transcribed Donegal speech
`ag iarracht` occurs **zero** times against **1,165** for `ag iarraidh`; it is zero in Connemara
speech (against 1,888) and zero in Kerry speech (against 290), with a nonsense negative control
also returning zero, so the zeros are real rather than a silently failed query. It appears in no
dictionary, and the grammar forbids it: `ag` plus a plain noun cannot make a progressive, and
`iarracht` has no verbal-noun function. The verbal noun of `iarr` is `iarraidh`.

**The provenance correction, carried as instructed.** `ag iarracht` was written into this course as
"Kai's ruling". **It is not one.** It was a preference expressed while commissioning the question —
that `iarracht a dhéanamh` is "a bit long" — and the worker who first called the result "a genuine
Ulster form" has formally retracted that. Its presence in the course was never evidence for itself.
That retraction is now recorded in the course's dialect spec, where the old "OPEN, NOT RULED"
section used to be.

## What I found before touching anything

Measured directly, not assumed: **668 seeds, all 668 translated, 0 teaching units, 0 practice
phrases, 0 audio rows.** Kai's figures held.

**22 seeds carry `iarracht` in any shape** — 14 with the coined `ag iarracht`, 8 with the noun
under a light verb. Nothing else in the course reaches for a "try" word: I checked for seeds whose
**English** says try / tried / trying but whose Irish uses neither `iarracht` nor `iarraidh`, and
there are **none**. The banned `ag triail` and `traíáil` are both at zero. So these 22 are the
whole surface, and the sweep set is closed rather than sampled.

---

## Keyed on the English, every row read individually

This was the instruction that mattered most, and it is not a formality. **`ag iarraidh` already
appears in 111 seeds of this course and mostly means "want", not "trying".** A sweep keyed on the
Irish would have rewritten "want" rows into try-rows, and because both readings are perfectly
grammatical Irish, nothing downstream would ever have flagged it. Every one of the 22 was decided
from its English gloss. No string was replaced blind.

### The 14 that carried the coinage — all plain progressive "trying", all now `ag iarraidh`

Seeds **2, 6, 50, 102, 103, 140, 159, 195, 205, 213, 222, 226, 372, 638**. Every one of these has
*trying* in its English and takes the progressive, so each is a straight substitution of the
banned form for the attested one, with the preposition and mutations unchanged — `ag` governs both
words identically and lenites neither.

Two are worth showing because they are now word-for-word what Donegal speakers were recorded
saying:

> **seed 2** — *I'm trying to learn* → **Tá mé ag iarraidh foghlaim**
> *(the Donegal desk has "ag iarraidh foghlaim corrfhocal thall is abhus")*
>
> **seed 226** — *the man is trying to help me* → **tá an fear ag iarraidh cuidiú liom**
> *(`ag iarraidh cuidiú` — 7 hits in Donegal speech)*

And one shows why keying on the English was not optional:

> **seed 222** — *he's trying to tell me what he wants* → **tá sé ag iarraidh inse domh goidé atá
> uaidh**

Both senses are in that single English sentence. The Irish renders *trying* with `ag iarraidh` and
*wants* with `atá uaidh`, so the seed stays unambiguous — but only because the decision was made
from the English.

### The 2 that needed the going-to frame repaired

These did not carry the coinage; they carried a construction the evidence does not license. Donegal
speech says `ag gabháil a`, not `ag dul a`, and takes **nominative** `iarracht` — the genitive
`ag déanamh iarrachta` that the Donegal translators originally proposed is itself **zero** in
Donegal speech, and the whole construction is attested in a recorded Donegal sentence,
*"tá mé ag gabháil a dhéanamh iarracht arís"*.

> **seed 8** — *I'm going to try to explain what I mean*
> was *Tá mé ag gabháil iarracht a dhéanamh **ar** a mhíniú…*
> now **Tá mé ag gabháil a dhéanamh iarracht a mhíniú goidé atá i gceist agam**
>
> **seed 236** — *…she was going to try to help*
> was *…go raibh sí ag gabháil a dhéanamh **iarrachta ar** chuidiú*
> now **…go raibh sí ag gabháil a dhéanamh iarracht cuidiú**

Seed 8's "explain" half was deliberately not touched: this course was already checked for the
defective *"explain what I mean with someone else"* shape that appears at seed 8 in eight other
courses on the estate, and it is clean here. I repaired only the going-to frame.

### The 6 I left alone, on purpose

Seeds **7, 146, 407, 491, 541, 579**. **`iarracht` the noun is not banned — only the fake
progressive `ag iarracht` is**, and these six all use the noun with a light verb, which is exactly
what the ruling says Donegal does for the non-progressive frames. Seed 579 is the one that proves
the point: *"we've often tried"* has no complement at all, so no progressive can render it, and it
already read **`is minic a rinne muid iarracht`** — the ruling's own answer, already in place.

Leaving these is a decision, not an omission. A sweep that had "cleaned" them would have removed
the only form capable of carrying a bare past.

## The clash check, before and after

ZUT is one known prompt to exactly one target. This course had **zero** known prompts pointing at
two different targets before I started — I re-measured rather than taking it on trust — and it has
**zero** after. The sweep introduced no regression.

The point worth stating plainly, because it is the price of the ruling rather than a defect in it:
**want and trying are now the same Irish string in Donegal, permanently.** That passes the rule —
one prompt still has exactly one answer, and *I want* and *I'm trying* simply share an answer,
which is what the language does. What is banned is one prompt with two answers, and that is the
condition the course was in before, when "try" had three competing renderings.

## Gaps — what I could not settle

**One open gap, carried forward rather than papered over.**

**Seed 638, *"I'm trying to think"*, now reads `tá mé ag iarraidh smaoineamh`. The construction is
evidenced; the verb is not.** Donegal commonly says **smaointiú / smaoitiú** where the standard has
`smaoineamh`, and *both* are zero in the transcribed Donegal sample, so the corpus does not choose
between them. The frame itself is sound — the identical shape `ag iarraidh cuidiú` has 7 Donegal
hits — so the sentence is not wrong, but its verb is unverified. **This wants a lexical check
before that seed is decomposed.** It is recorded in the course's dialect spec as well as here, so
it cannot be lost.

**Two limits inherited from the evidence base, neither of which I could close and neither of which
affects the ban.** No native speaker of Donegal has reviewed any of this — it is dictionaries,
corpora and existing courses throughout. And the Donegal speech corpus is small at roughly 65,000
tokens: `ag iarraidh` at 1,165 hits is far too common to be affected by that, but a form appearing
once or twice in it would not be safe to build on. Nothing in this sweep rests on a one-hit claim.

**Nothing else was blocked.** I had full database access, every count in this document was measured
directly, and there is no category of check I attempted and could not complete.
