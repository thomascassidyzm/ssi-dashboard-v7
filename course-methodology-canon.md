## How to add to this

**If you learn a durable rule, it goes here.** Not in a skill, not in a brief, not in a commit message that nobody will read again.

- **Add it with all five fields** (below). A rule without a source is not a rule, it is an opinion, and the next agent cannot tell the difference.
- **If you are not sure whether you understood it correctly, ask Kai** rather than writing your best guess. A canon with an invented rule in it is more dangerous than a canon with a hole, because agents act on it.
- **If your new rule contradicts something already here, do not resolve it yourself.** Add it to the CLASHES list at the top and ask Kai. He rules in one word; you would spend an hour and be wrong.
- **If you cannot tell whether a rule is HARD or SOFT, mark it UNCLEAR** and list it. Guessing wrong in the HARD direction blocks good work; guessing wrong in the SOFT direction ships defects.
- **The methodology rules in this document are KAI'S to change, and a ruling he gives you does NOT need Tom's authorisation to be written in.** "It's my canon document, you don't need to ask Tom for permission to override it" (Kai, 2026-09-10). **R0.11 has been narrowed to match:** builder prompts and shared tooling documents are genuinely joint with Tom, because they are tools built together; the teaching rules on this page are not. If Kai rules, write it in.
- **This is Kai's own instruction, not a house convention.** "You can add to it without me prompting, just make sure that whenever you learn something it goes in there (if you're not sure, ask me to make sure you understood correctly, or if something clashes)" (Kai, 2026-08-18) and "it'd be good for you to update it with my decisions as we go as well" (Kai, 2026-08-27). Keeping it current is the job. Resolving a clash inside it is not.

Every rule below carries: **the rule in one plain sentence · HARD or SOFT · where it came from · what breaks if you get it wrong · how to check it.**

**HARD** = violating it breaks the course or teaches the learner something wrong.
**SOFT** = judgement, taste, or a default you may depart from with a stated reason.
**UNCLEAR** = nobody has ruled yet. Listed for Kai, not guessed at.

## 1. What a SEED is, and what makes one valid

A **seed** is a sentence from the course curriculum — the thing the learner will eventually be able to say. It is delivered to the learner by being broken into LEGOs.

**S1 — A seed is a vehicle for delivering LEGOs, not the point in itself.** · **SOFT** · `ralph-methodology.md:405–409` · You optimise the sentence instead of the teaching units and produce one opaque blob per seed · Human judgement.

**S2 — Every seed must rebuild perfectly, in BOTH languages, from its own decomposition plus already-taught vocabulary.** · **HARD — this is the master rule; most other failures are special cases of breaking it** · `ralph-methodology.md:21` · The learner is asked to produce something they were never given the parts for; the round is unanswerable · Automatic on the target side (tiling + vocabulary gates in `POST /api/seed/complete`). **The known-side half is human judgement unless a pair-contract exists.**

**S3 — Every part of the seed sentence must be COVERED by a LEGO. Coverage is HARD and blocks; overlap is a separate question and never blocks (S4).** · **HARD** · **Kai's ruling, 2026-08-27, closing clash C8**; `ralph-methodology.md:411–418`; `course-audit.md:33–42` · A piece of the seed is never taught · **Automatic — tiling gate, hard-blocks, runs even for golden seeds.**

> "They don't have to tile perfectly, as in there migth be some overlap (for example we might introduce a bit, before introducing an m-lego including that bit - in order to avoid introducing a large m-lego all on one go) however **all parts of the seed sentence should be covered by the legos (or previously introduced legos)**" — Kai, 2026-08-27

**What changed:** this entry read "no words missed, no words added", carrying overlap as an aside. `ralph:411` ("a sanity check, not a rigid constraint") and `course-audit:35` ("CRITICAL") were arguing about two different properties — *no gaps* and *no overlap* — as though they were one. **S15 — A seed sentence is not rewritten unless it is actually wrong. "Better phrasing", "more natural", "reads nicer" are never reasons.** · **HARD** · **Kai, 2026-09-08**, approving this wording. He approved it as a single statement drawn together from the fragments this rule had been living in — **S6**, **S7**, **R0.3**, "Editing translated seed text in a course he owns" under what is Kai's call, and `ralph-methodology.md:399`'s "you cannot quietly reword the seed" — rather than composing it cold; the assembly is this canon's, the approval is his · A seed edit's blast radius is the whole course (**S11**) and nothing checks it (**O1**), so a rewrite made for taste spends every LEGO, phrase and clip downstream of the old wording and buys nothing back · **How to check: name which of the three senses of WRONG applies, in writing, before you touch the row. If you cannot name one, there is no edit.**

**WRONG means exactly three things, and the test is that you can say which one:**

1. **Ungrammatical** — the sentence is not well formed in its own language.
2. **Teaches something false** — a learner who takes it at face value ends up believing something about the language that is not true.
3. **Fails a rule in this canon** — and you can cite the entry. Coverage (**S3**), a piece acquired by accident (**S5**), changed canonical meaning (**S7**), an untaught form (**K14**, **K15**), a register marker arriving after the phrases that need it (**S13**).

There is no fourth item. "I would have said it differently", "this reads better", "a native speaker would more naturally say…" — where the existing sentence is grammatical, true and canon-compliant — are all the same non-reason wearing different clothes, and none of them opens the row.

**The one other circumstance in which a seed may change is a mapping failure that survives re-chunking, and that goes to Kai, never taken by an agent.** That is **L25 rung 2**, which already carries the ladder, the bounds and the escalation; follow it there rather than reasoning from this entry. It is an exception to *when a seed may change*, not to the three senses of wrong — the seed in that case is not defective, the chunking is, and the seed change is the remedy Kai may choose.

**Where the gates are.** The canonical-mismatch 400 (**S6**) catches a *build agent* submitting a reworded seed, and that is the only automatic hold. An edit made directly against an existing seed row does not pass that gate at all, so on the fix path this rule is human judgement end to end — which is why the naming step above is the check.

**S16 — IRISH *try*: prefer the FINITE and INFINITIVAL shapes over the progressive wherever the known side allows it.** · **SOFT (the preference) / HARD (the genitive inside the progressive, which is grammar and not taste)** · **Kai, 2026-09-10** · The progressive is grammatical, so nothing rejects it and no check flags it; a course built on it drills the least-attested vehicle for a very common intention, and the learner meets `rinne mé iarracht` for the first time in the wild · **How to check: read the seed's target text. No gate covers this and none could — both shapes are well formed.**

**The preferred shapes:** `rinne mé iarracht…`, `ní dhearna sí iarracht ar bith…`, `iarracht a dhéanamh`. **The dispreferred shape:** `tá mé ag déanamh iarrachta…`.

**The evidence is an absence, and the absence IS the finding.** None of Ó Dónaill, focloir.ie or de Bhaldraithe illustrates the progressive **at all**: every dictionary example of this construction, in all three, is finite or infinitival. Three independent lexicographic sources reaching for the same two shapes and never once for the third is what this rule rests on. **It is not a claim that the progressive is wrong** — it is well formed, and a learner who hears it should understand it. It is the less-travelled vehicle for *I am trying to*, and a course teaches the travelled one first.

**Four points settled in the same pass, recorded with it because they are the same decision:**

**(a) THE GENITIVE STAYS.** `ag déanamh iarrachta`, never `ag déanamh iarracht`. *iarracht* is feminine and the object of `ag` + verbal noun goes genitive, exactly as in `ag déanamh oibre`. Connacht speech erodes it, and **the asymmetry is the argument**: a learner taught the full form is understood everywhere and will hear the erosion for themselves, while a learner taught the erosion has to be re-taught. Teaching the full form is safe in both directions; teaching the eroded one is not.

**(b) THE BARE VERBAL NOUN IS FINE where the verbal noun has no object of its own** — `iarracht a dhéanamh cabhrú`. **With an object, the object goes FIRST**, with `a` + lenition: `rinne sí iarracht Everest a dhreapadh`. The shape breaks only when an object is left dangling *after* the verbal noun.

**(c) NO PREPOSITION in modern usage.** The older `ar`/`faoi` frame pairs with `iarracht a thabhairt`; modern `iarracht a dhéanamh` takes none.

**(d) SCOPE — CONNACHT-LEANING, NOT CONFINED TO CONNACHT.** The base course leans Connacht but is not confined to it, so **wherever the Caighdeán and Connacht speech differ, the standard form wins**. Only an Ulster-only form is excluded.

**THE EVIDENCE GAP, stated because this canon's value is that it does not overstate.** The research behind this ruling carries **no corpus counts**: corpas.ie could not be rendered and Pota Focal refused the connection. It rests on **four dictionaries** — three of them named above — **plus one attested prose text**, and **two of its paragraphs were explicitly self-labelled by their author as feel rather than source**. So the frequency claim is a lexicographic argument, not a measured one. If a future reader can reach a corpus, this is the entry to re-check first (**GAPS 24**).

**What this entry does NOT do.** It does not make an existing progressive rendering *wrong*: **S15** opens a seed only for text that is ungrammatical, teaches something false, or fails a rule here, and the progressive is none of the three. **Whether the preference reaches backwards into rows already built on the progressive is now RULED — as a question about who decides, not about the rows** (**R0.18**, closing **C26**): an agent never rewrites correct live rows on the strength of a soft preference, and **Kai** may open any live seed he likes on the strength of this one, whenever he says so · So this entry is not a work order against live Irish content, and it is not a bar on him giving one · **Read with S14**, Kai's own Irish instance of moving a pattern (*ag triail*) later in the course, and **R0.15**, where a form learners have already been taught may outweigh the methodological point and the call is his.

**THE COMPANION RULING — THE SPLIT APPLIES TO BOTH IRISH COURSES.** Kai ruled the same day that the `iarracht` / `ag iarraidh` split holds in **`gle_for_eng` and in the Connemara course `gle_cn_for_eng` alike**: **`iarracht` carries *try*, `ag iarraidh` carries *want*, one known sense to one target form, in both courses.** He ruled it against a recommendation to exempt the Connemara course:

> "Stick to the original ruling - it's harmless in terms of clashes, but such a basic word so early, will throw off learners." — Kai, 2026-09-10

**The recommendation he overturned is recorded so it is not re-made, because it was a strong one.** de Bhaldraithe — the foundational Cois Fharraige lexicographer, and the nearest thing the Connemara course has to a source of record — glosses `ag iarraidh` as *trying to* throughout his own dictionary, as do Ó Dónaill and Bedell's 1685 Bible, and **no dedicated alternative exists that Connemara natives reach for instead**. So the dialect attestation for letting `ag iarraidh` carry both senses in the Connemara course is genuine, and the clash it would create is in the direction this canon itself calls harmless (**K3**) — two known senses landing on one target word. Kai granted both of those points in the same sentence and still ruled the other way · **His reason is a rule about clashes rather than a fact about Irish, so it is written where it can be reused: K24.**

## 2. What a LEGO is, and what makes one valid

A **LEGO** is a chunk the learner is taught as one unit and can then recombine. **A LEGO introduces new vocabulary — that is what a LEGO IS.**

**L1 — New vocabulary in a LEGO is REQUIRED, not a defect. Never contort a fix to avoid a new word.** · **HARD** · Kai, 2026-08-18: "that's the definition of being in the course, a lego by definition will have something new. Just need to make sure it fits the course (the word is in the seed, affected phrases are fixed if needed)." · Applying the practice-phrase rule ("never use an untaught word") to a LEGO produces mangled sentences dodging words the card was entitled to teach · **The two tests are FIT:** (a) the word belongs in its seed, (b) every downstream practice phrase using that LEGO still works afterwards — and where it does not, those phrases are fixed **in the same pass**, never left dangling.

**L2 — A LEGO is one bit that is not too small to be meaningless and not too big to be inflexible. The balance is judged per language pair, not by a rule.** · **HARD (the test), SOFT (where any particular chunk lands)** · **Kai, 2026-08-27, closing the general half of clash C17**; `synonym-choice-architecture.md:82–87` · You mint a card whose whole content is "this is a question marker" and the learner can produce nothing from it · Human judgement; partial proxy in `course-audit.md:44–59` (red-flag words in component known_text).

> "A lego is one bit that is not too small to be meaningless, not too big to be inflexible. A perfect lego can be combined with other legos easily without thinking about grammar rules and stuff. It's a balance. **Not many hard rules - it depends on the language pair what makes a good lego.**… Or look at the Welsh courses - they have the gold standard of legos. Some are one word, some are bigger." — Kai, 2026-08-27

**What changed:** this entry stated only the meaningfulness half. Kai's test is **meaningfulness plus flexibility**, and there is **no general over-chunking rule** — `synonym-choice-architecture.md:108`'s claim that a two-morpheme chunk fails because each part is itself a meaningful unit is not his test. Where one side of the pair cannot be broken down, **the chunk grows on both sides rather than being split**: "obviously 'can' doesn't work as a lego, it needs to be bigger", and on the objection that merging hides a productive pattern, "This is not a cost, this is the course working as it should. When one side can't be broken down, the other side follows and grows as well. Works both ways." (Kai, 2026-08-27; see **L22**) · **When the question is open, read Welsh (R0.12).**

**Which way the balance tips when it is genuinely close: toward the bigger chunk, because the cost of going smaller is paid by the learner.** Kai closed the 我想 case — and clash C17 with it — on 2026-09-08:

> "to me, **'I want' is a good chunk for almost all language pairs**. If we go smaller, it can feel like you're **juggling too many small pieces as a learner**. It gives the phrases more flexibility, but **that makes the learning harder**." — Kai, 2026-09-08

**The reasoning is the durable part, not the verdict on one chunk.** Smaller chunks are genuinely more flexible — the phrases can be built more freely — and that flexibility is bought with learner load. **The trade goes to the learner.** So "this chunk could be split and the pieces would still be meaningful" is not an argument for splitting it; the argument for splitting has to be that the whole is inflexible enough to block phrases the course needs. This does not turn L2 into a count — the balance is still judged per language pair, and **L3**'s 8-syllable cap is still the only size limit that rejects. It says which side of a close call to come down on · **L25** is the other direction of the same move: where a chunk cannot be glossed to match its seed, it grows.

**L3 — A LEGO's target is capped at 8 syllables, and this cap runs even under skip_validation.** · **HARD** · `language-config.cjs:61` (`MAX_LEGO_SYLLABLES = 8`) · Rejection; pedagogically, an oversized LEGO exceeds what the learner can hold as one chunk · **Automatic, always-on. This is the one size limit that genuinely rejects.**

**L4 — A-LEGO is a single meaningful word; M-LEGO is a multi-word chunk. Type is author-declared, never computed.** · **HARD (structural)** · `ralph-methodology.md:94–116` · An M with no components[] is rejected; a mistyped LEGO passes silently and misleads every later reader · Automatic type/components check.

**L5 — When an atom fails ZUT, chunk it up with the context that resolves the ambiguity.** · **HARD** · `calibrate.md:39–48` · The learner hesitates between je/moi/me and confidence collapses · Automatic only after the fact; choosing the chunk size up front is judgement.

**L6 — Pronouns, articles, prepositions and conjunctions are near-always ZUT failures and should not be bare A-LEGOs.** · **SOFT** (explicitly "usually") · `calibrate.md:268–287` · "with"→avec plus "you"→tu assembles to "avec tu" · Human judgement.

**L7 — Grammatical particles are construction-features, never atomic LEGOs.** · **HARD** · `ralph-methodology.md:25,157–177`; `synonym-choice-architecture.md:266–274` · A category error: the card asks the learner to *mean* something that is not a unit of meaning, so it never reads naturally · The learner never forms an intention to "say 吗" — only to mean a whole thought the particle helps build · **But a particle MAY be carried as a COMPONENT of an M-LEGO** — Kai ruled clash C13 that way on 2026-08-27, and that is the designed route by which it later becomes combinable. `jpn-analysis-example.md:168–217`, which says it must be omitted entirely and calls including it "BULLSHIT", is the losing side. **See L23 and L22 step 5.** The rule here is only that it is never an *atomic LEGO*.

**L8 — A particle is consolidated, not introduced: made salient only once enough whole-thought carriers already exist.** · **HARD** · `ralph-methodology.md:166` · Placement is **pull, not push** — the signal that the moment has come is that carrier phrases already exist in earlier baskets · Human judgement.

**L9 — A particle's first exposure is a contrastive twin debut — two or three overlapping carriers sharing it, never a bare debut.** · **HARD** · `ralph-methodology.md:168–175` · The learner has one instance and no contrast, so nothing is inferable · Human judgement.

**L10 — A multi-sense particle is introduced once per real usage, and at least one context must reconstruct the seed's own use of it.** · **HARD — a sense the seed uses but you don't teach is an ERROR** · `ralph-methodology.md:25`.

**L11 — A LEGO is glossed by its whole communicative intention; a COMPONENT is glossed literally. Never a grammar label.** · **HARD** · `ralph-methodology.md:27,55,177`; **Kai, 2026-09-08, closing clash C18** · "把 = object marker" costs cognitive action and yields zero confidence · Warning-level check exists (`checkMetadataGloss`).

> "**whole intention for a lego, literal for a component.** Yes, although **we should match the seed translation as much as possible**. If it's not working (the lego can't be translated in a way that matches the seed) we probably need to break the lego up differently, usually that means making it a bigger lego." — Kai, 2026-09-08

**Both instructions were right and neither was general.** `tools/breakdown-flat.cjs:65` — "parts get LITERAL glosses" — is correct **for component rows only** and must not be read as a rule about LEGOs; `ralph:177` is correct for LEGOs. This is the same scoping that **L23** applies to the collision test: a component is held to a different standard because it is never prompted on its own.

**The second half is a constraint on the gloss itself: match the seed's own translation as closely as possible.** A LEGO whose gloss drifts from the wording the seed uses teaches the learner one phrasing and prompts them with another, and the drift is invisible to every gate because both sides are individually fine · **And where the match cannot be made, the gloss is not the thing to fix — see L25.**

**L12 — Idiomatic, non-derivable combinations are taught whole.** · **HARD** · `ralph-methodology.md:31` · The learner composes "prepare + good" and gets something that does not mean "ready".

**L13 — Introduce the A-LEGO before the M-LEGO that contains it (non-greedy introduction).** · **HARD** (pre-submission checklist item; no gate) · `ralph-methodology.md:454–461` · The M-LEGO arrives entirely new; cognitive load doubles and the pattern is not inferable.

**L14 — Use an overlapping LEGO only where something non-obvious happens between the small piece and the big one.** · **SOFT — "the art is knowing the difference"** · `layered-decomposition-brief.md:23–68` · Redundant drilling that bores the learner and buys no inference · The test: "would the learner be surprised by how these pieces combine?" · **Clash C15 is closed (S4): overlap is normal, expected and never blocks.** Kai's wording is looser than this entry's — his examples are about **load** ("to avoid introducing a large m-lego all on one go"), not only about non-obviousness — so read L14 as the strong case for an overlap, not as the only licence for one.

**L15 — Each LEGO must be able to appear naturally in 3+ other sentences; if not, redraw the boundaries.** · **HARD** (checklist item) · `layered-decomposition-brief.md:88–92` · A single-use chunk costs a slot and yields nothing.

**L16 — Decomposition is driven by phrase quality, not tiling logic.** · **HARD** · `ralph-methodology.md:937–947` · Always ask "can this LEGO make meaningful BUILD phrases with existing vocabulary?" — a LEGO that tiles perfectly but cannot be practised (Dutch bare "hoe") is a wrong decomposition · Rebundle.

**L17 — A debut may be suppressed (`is_new:false`) on either of TWO grounds: a pure same-meaning restatement, or a pattern the learner is by now FAMILIAR with.** · **HARD (that those are the two grounds), SOFT (when familiarity has been reached)** · `ralph-methodology.md:57`; **Kai, 2026-09-01, closing clash C25 on 2026-09-10** · Announcing a pattern as new for the tenth time tells the learner they are meeting something they have been using for fifty seeds, and the debut machinery — presentation, BUILD basket — is spent on nothing.

> "After the first couple legos with 'I'm going to x' we can just start treating it as taught (is new false)." — Kai, 2026-09-01

**What changed, and why.** This entry used to permit `is_new:false` **only** for a pure same-meaning restatement, and called the lever minor by design (~15 of ~1,100 rounds in the zho audit). Kai's words are later and they win: **familiarity is a ground in its own right**, which this entry did not recognise at all. A pattern that has had its debut and a couple of LEGOs' worth of drilling is taught, and the next LEGO built on it is not new · **The measured rarity was a description of one audit, never a quota** — do not read "~15 of ~1,100" as a ceiling.

**What survives from the old entry, and this is a judgement call flagged as such.** Kai ruled on the *pattern* case — "I'm going to x" — and said nothing about the other three. Read as compatible, and marked here as **this canon's reading rather than his words**: a **distinct word**, an **idiomatic chunk** (**L12**) and an **overlap-ladder rung** (**L14**) may still genuinely keep their debut, because none of them is the learner meeting a familiar pattern again — each carries something the learner has not had. **A deliberate nuance re-debut likewise keeps its debut**, since its whole purpose is to announce a difference · **If a case does not fit either ground cleanly, keep the debut and ask Kai.** Suppressing one wrongly silently removes a teaching moment nothing will flag.

**L18 — LEGO 1 of seed 1 cannot carry content, because the learner knows only that one word.** · **HARD** · Kai, from first principles, overturning a completed worker chain (Finnish) · There is nothing to recombine with; a LEGO 1 that carries content is unanswerable.

**L19 — Needing to add a tag or a slash is a DIAGNOSIS that the LEGO is under-contexted.** · **HARD on scope, SOFT on the diagnosis** · Kai, 2026-08-18: "the need to add a tag and/or slash in the item does suggest the lego could possibly do with more context (merging to a relevant sister lego)… Even if a lego isn't going to actually clash, it could cause confusion." · You ship the tag (violating the no-parentheticals rule) or strip it and leave the cause untouched · **Non-clashing items in the same class stay in scope** — confusion is broader than collision.

**L20 — Where one target word carries two known meanings, separate the senses by a distinct KNOWN word or by context, then drill both.** · **HARD (procedure)** · Kai, 2026-08-17 (Sinhala අහනවා = ask/hear), corrected by him the same day, extended 2026-08-26 — **closes clash C16** · The learner cannot know which answer is wanted — the one-prompt-two-answers defect in reverse · **The procedure, in order:**

1. **Do both senses actually come up in seeds?** If only one, stick to it and align the outliers — done.
2. **If both, separate them by a distinct KNOWN-language word per sense**, where the known language has natural ones. His own correction of the write-up is the substance: *"It's about a distinct KNOWN word per sense that fixes it, not a distinct target word."*
3. **Otherwise — and this is just as valuable, sometimes better even where (2) is possible — separate them by CONTEXT built into the LEGO itself, expanded on BOTH sides**, by merging to a sister LEGO so the seed's pattern still holds. *"the other option which is just as valuable as the distinct words, is distinct context… it might be a better option sometimes even if [distinct words] is possible."*
4. **Never with a tag or a slash.** *"Not almost never - it should NEVER be fixed with a tag or a slash. The ones that have them have just not been fixed yet."* Where a marker word such as "sir" carries the sense, **the target side carries the translated marker too**; the known side is the only place it may look like a bare tag (K11, L19).
5. **Every practice phrase stays consistent with the split — across other seeds too**, not only the one that raised it.
6. **Enough phrases for BOTH senses** that the distinction actually gets drilled.

**What changed:** steps 2 and 3 were previously written as having *equal standing with no order*. Kai's own correction gives them an order — distinct known words first, context second — while keeping context's standing explicitly. The never-a-tag rule, the both-sides-carry-the-marker clause, and the known-word-not-target-word correction are all new here · **See L24** — the "as in" context is a third route and is *preferred* where the sister LEGO is long · **And see K23, which is the STANDARD the result of any of these routes is judged against** (Kai, 2026-09-10): keep the clash rather than merging it away, put the sense in the presentation and never name the sister, and make sure the marked sense carries its context in every prompt in the course. **Route 3 of this procedure — putting the sense into the LEGO's own text as context — is bounded by L26: the added context must be words the seed actually says.**

**L21 — A word that appears in a seed must be drilled as part of a pattern, not left as a one-off.** · **HARD (the test), SOFT (the remedy)** · Kai, 2026-08-17: "If 'nimi' comes up in a seed then we should make sure it's not just a one-off. we need to make sure we drill a pattern… **Sometimes the answer is to add more, not less.**" · **The two-part availability test:** is the word available at this stage, *and* is it clear enough? Every gate we own answers only the first · An agent's reflexive fix (delete the offending phrase) is the opposite of what is wanted.

**L22 — A bound element that cannot stand alone grows to the verb IN ITS OWN SEED — not to the commonest verb, and never to a placeholder.** · **HARD (which verb), SOFT (edit-versus-delete on each phrase)** · Kai, 2026-08-27 · Six courses hit this independently and invented four different answers · The standing rule is that when one side cannot be broken down, the other side grows to match — but a bound element attaches to a *different* verb in every phrase (吧 appeared with roughly eight verbs across eight prompts), so "merge with its neighbour" looked to have no single answer. **This rule ends that.**

> "Grow it to whatever is in the seed. What does the seed say? Then the learner will just practice different phrases with that specific one - the phrases should be removed or edited as a result, of course. Same for phrases that include it, that are under future seeds. If it comes up again in a seed, we teach it with the new verb that seed uses - now the learner has two verbs to use it with. They might see the pattern and start using it in other places. Technically, if the 吧 stays a component in the M-lego, it will be available to mix with other verbs in the future. But in the beginning, when it is first introduced, it is better to keep it simple and only use it with one thing. But there is nothing blocking it being used with other verbs later when the learner is used to it. We should make sure it is drilled enough and not just dropped, anyway, and then gradually introduce new verbs, if we want to do it properly." — Kai, 2026-08-27

**The procedure, in order:**
1. **The seed decides.** Merge the bound element — a Japanese bound ending like the potential inside 覚えられる, a Chinese particle like 吧, a stranded inflection — with the verb **in its own seed sentence**. Not the commonest verb across the phrases, not a representative one.
2. **Practice phrases under that LEGO that use a different verb are EDITED to the taught combination, or DELETED.** Prefer the edit where the sentence still teaches something; delete where an edit would produce a sentence nobody would say. **Variety at first introduction is a DEFECT, not a virtue.**
3. **The same applies to phrases under LATER seeds** that use the element with another verb.
4. **Exception, and the designed path:** if a later **SEED** itself uses the element with a different verb, that seed is where combination two is legitimately taught. **Do not flatten it.** This is how generality is built — deliberately and gradually, not by accident at the debut.
5. **Keep the bound element as a COMPONENT of the merged M-LEGO.** That is what leaves it free to recombine later; merging does not close the door.
6. **Drill it enough.** "We should make sure it is drilled enough and not just dropped." If editing and deleting drop the LEGO below the phrase floor, **author new phrases using the taught combination** rather than leaving it thin (L21 — sometimes the answer is to add more, not less).

**Ruled out explicitly:** a label containing a gap, tilde, bracket or placeholder standing in for "whatever verb comes here". Kai on that option: **"yeah, this one is a no-go."** It produces a lesson the learner cannot say aloud. **See K11 and L19** — the same never-invent-notation rail, reached from a different direction.

**Two constraints on the merge itself, added by Kai on 2026-09-03:**
- **A merge expands BOTH sides of the LEGO, never only the problem side.** "remember merging means both sides of the lego expand, not just the problem side. Otherwise we create another problem as they wouldn't match anymore."
- **A sister merge is only worth doing if the sister actually carries the distinguishing context.** "does the sister merge you were planning involve a sister lego that actually holds the past/future context? Because if they don't, it's pointless as a teaching opportunity! And whether it's 'cheap' is not relevant, **I don't care about losing a few phrases, just make new ones.**" — the cost of lost phrases is not an argument (P18, L21).

**Obligations:** this is a LEGO edit, so O2 and O6 both fire; edits and deletions of phrases are reported rather than applied silently (R0.3, and see "What is Kai's call").

**L23 — Components are held to a LOOSER standard than LEGOs and are scanned separately. (The "cannot fail the collision test" half is SUPERSEDED by K22.)** · **HARD (separate scan, looser standard); SUPERSEDED (collision exemption — see K22)** · **Kai, 2026-09-04, closing clashes C13 and C14** · Judging a component by the LEGO standard generates a queue of non-defects; treating it as a LEGO for collision purposes blocks a construction that by design is never prompted again · A component may read oddly on its own without that being a defect.

> "Oh, you should have said those were components! **Components can be a bit weird, we have to accept some weirdness sometimes** - and **they never play again after that first introduction so there's no risk of a clash.** We should scan them separately from everything else." — Kai, 2026-09-04

So `glossary.md:54` is right about clash exposure and `phase1-translation/PROMPT.md:229` is the losing side; and a grammatical particle carried with `introduce:false` is the **designed route** by which it later becomes combinable (**L7**, **L22** step 5) · **Permitted is not encouraged.** A component earns its place: on componentising the Hindi copula, in the same thread — "doesn't sound like a valuable contribution to me… Something like that should be done mindfully."

> **Reconciled, 2026-09-08 — this entry used to say "ask Kai", and Kai no longer needs to be asked.** Tom put his own 2026-08-28 ruling and Kai's 2026-09-04 one side by side and ruled over both: the test is on the **delivered prompt**, not on the component (**K22**). So Kai's *reason* for the exemption — "they never play again after that first introduction so there's no risk of a clash" — is no longer the grounds, and a component is not exempt *by construction*; what protects it is that the prompts it appears in each have one answer. **His words above stand and his other two rulings here are untouched:** components are scanned separately, held to a looser standard, and are allowed to be a bit weird.

**L24 — The "as in" context is a first-class fix for a two-sense chunk, and is preferred to merging when the sister LEGO is long.** · **HARD (that it is legitimate), SOFT (which route a given case takes)** · Kai, 2026-09-03 · Merging into a long sister produces an unwieldy LEGO, and the two-sense chunk stays unfixed while the merge is argued about.

> "can they ba attached to a sister lego with context easily? Otherwise, we can use the 'as in' context… And then keep using it in only that sense… When it comes up in a seed sentence next, introduce it again (won't matter that it causes a ZUT - because we're handling it), make sure the 'as in' context is in the presentation, this time with the other sense, and drill the new one with phrases it works in. Soon after, you can start drilling both versions." — Kai, 2026-09-03

**Be consistent within a course:** "that sister is really long, prefer the as in method. Probably best to be consistent also - the merging method didn't work for all, so we should use the as in method for all." · **Note the striking part: a DELIBERATE, HANDLED ZUT is acceptable** when the second sense is introduced with contrastive context in the presentation. That is a named exception to **K2**, it is Kai's, and it does not extend to any collision that is merely inconvenient to fix · **L20** is the parent procedure; this is its third route.

## 2a. What a PRESENTATION is, and what may go in one

A **presentation** is the narration that introduces a LEGO — the line the learner hears before the BUILD phrases. It is spoken aloud, so everything in it is heard, and nothing in it can be skimmed past.

**PR1 — Tiny explanations ARE allowed in a presentation. Authored BY HAND, during a FIXES pass, and the builder is not changed to emit them.** · **HARD (the scope), SOFT (when one is worth adding)** · **Kai, 2026-09-10, overriding Tom's standing "no explanations, ever" rule with Aran's authority** · The method's default is still inference from contrast — a presentation is not a grammar lesson — but a learner meeting a marked sense with no way to know it is marked is not inferring anything, they are guessing.

> "I've talked to Aran and he's given me the power to override Tom's 'no explanations, ever' rule. From now we can include tiny explanations (manually, during the fixes phase! No changes to the builder for now)." — Kai, 2026-09-10

**Three scope conditions, all of them load-bearing:**

1. **TINY.** "We should keep the explanations minimal." One clause naming the sense. Not a rule, not a paradigm, not a contrast.
2. **BY HAND, IN A FIXES PASS.** A person authors it, on a case they have read. It is not generated at volume.
3. **THE BUILDER IS NOT CHANGED.** *"No changes to the builder for now."* Making the builder emit explanations is a separate conversation with Tom and is **R0.11** territory — do not propose it as a follow-on to a fix.

**Where the old rule still lives, and it is not being swept.** "Grammar is NEVER explained" is stated in `ralph-methodology.md:75` and in the builder brief. Those are a doctrine file and a builder prompt, so **R0.11** governs them and this canon does not edit them. **The rule on this page is the one that governs a fixes pass**; if you are authoring content by hand, PR1 is your authority, and the divergence with those files is recorded here rather than papered over · **PR2** is the shape a good one takes.

**PR2 — An explanation describes ONLY the sense it is introducing, and NEVER names or contrasts its sister sense.** · **HARD** · **Kai, 2026-09-10** · Naming the sister teaches the learner that there is a choice to make, at exactly the moment the method is trying to give them one deterministic answer — it manufactures the hesitation ZUT exists to prevent.

> "Only ever describe the sense it's introducing and never mention its sister… We should keep the explanations minimal." — Kai, 2026-09-10

**The template, in his own words:** *"The Italian for 'to know', when you're talking about people, is: …"* · **No "not sapere". No "as opposed to". No "there are two words for this".** The other sense is not mentioned, not implied, and not gestured at · **K23 is the whole standard this serves**, and the explanation is only one of its six parts — a presentation line does **not** on its own make a two-sense chunk safe, because **K23(d)** obliges the marked sense to carry its context in every prompt, everywhere in the course.

**PR3 — A LEGO whose target form belongs to a SPECIFIC FRAME gets a heads-up presentation: vague, no grammar, a short clear example that shows the frame. The template is Kai's, and each line is marked human-authored.** · **HARD (the shape: no grammar, no rule, one short example), SOFT (when a LEGO needs one)** · **Kai, 2026-09-28** (`630a12be`), applied by job #546·I · A learner meeting *sarebbe stato* glossed "it would be" with no hint that it is the after-*said* form will produce it standalone, where it is wrong (**P24**) — and a presentation that *explains* the rule is a grammar lesson, which **PR1** keeps tiny and **`ralph-methodology.md:75`** forbids outright.

## 3. What a PRACTICE PHRASE is, and what makes one valid

A **practice phrase** is a sentence the learner is asked to produce. There are two kinds, and **the rules differ**.

**P1 — A practice phrase must be buildable from material already taught by that point. No untaught words.** · **HARD — this is the constraint that pairs with L1** · Kai, 2026-08-18; severity in his own word, 2026-09-03: **"Yes, critical fail to have stuff that isn't taught."** · A correct sentence built from untaught LEGOs is **not an acceptable fix** · Automatic on the target side (vocabulary/tiling gate). Known side is gated only where a pair-contract exists — see the enforcement table.

**P2 — Phrases tile from WHOLE already-introduced chunks, never re-split into words.** · **HARD** · `ralph-methodology.md:516–524` · This is what blocks untaught conjugations, inversions and contractions: if a wording needs a form you have not introduced as a whole chunk, it fails · Vocabulary accumulates strictly in seed/index order — **never a later sibling, no forward references** · Automatic.

### BUILD phrases

**P3 — A BUILD phrase shows the new LEGO plugging into what the learner already knows.** · **HARD** · `ralph-methodology.md:184–219` · BUILD phrases are NOT random extensions and NOT the bare LEGO in isolation ("how → hoe" is not a BUILD phrase) · Automatic — the BUILD anti-template gate rejects the LEGO plus a short filler tag.

**P4 — BUILD phrases play once, in context, and are never replayed.** · **HARD** · Kai, 2026-08-17: "Some short bits can be confusing when not in the context of having just heard the presentation… So we only play build phrases in context, and never again." · **Fragments are fine here** — the presentation just framed them · Not eternal-eligible; never enters spaced repetition.

**P5 — BUILD quantity is governed by cognitive load, not an arbitrary count.** · **SOFT** · `ralph-methodology.md:196` · If the LEGO is already long, you add almost nothing · **But note the enforced floor is 3 from seed 4 onward** — that is a floor on the *number* of BUILD phrases, and it is real. **How long each one is is settled: P19, and it never blocks (P21).** Clashes C11 and C12 are closed.

### USE phrases

**P6 — A USE phrase is replayed later, out of context, so it must never be confusing or misleading when met cold.** · **HARD** · Kai, 2026-08-17 · This is the whole reason the two roles exist · All USE phrases are eternal-eligible and enter spaced repetition.

**P7 — Builders are pushed toward FULL SENTENCES. A fragment is a last resort, allowed as a BUILD phrase and NEVER as a USE phrase.** · **HARD (the BUILD/USE asymmetry), SOFT (the push)** · **Kai's ruling, 2026-08-27, closing clash C1** and sharpening his own 2026-08-17 position · A fragment that replays cold, out of context, has two right answers and the learner freezes (P6) · **The order of resort:** try merging the LEGOs; try reordering them within the seed; only if neither works, accept incomplete phrases **as BUILDs**, and practise the first LEGO with a few incomplete phrases until later content arrives that completes it.

> "I think we should push builder agents to use full sentences, which is likely where the documents push for it. I also think that in a very limited amount of cases, where a lego is tricky and can't be combined with enough of the legos that exist at that point to make a full sentence, then I believe **having incomplete sentences as BUILDs and not USE, is acceptable**. But this should only be decided while making fixes if nothing else works, like merging legos or reordering legos in the seed… it might be better to just introduce one first and practise it with a few incomplete phrases before the second part comes in" — Kai, 2026-08-27

**Generation time versus fix time — the load-bearing half.** "This should only be decided **while making fixes**." **Generation pushes for full sentences; the fragment allowance is a fix-time remedy, not a licence at the build stage.** He had raised this doubt himself on 2026-08-19 ("It might be a fixes-specific rule to apply occasionally when there is an issue, rather than something to allow at the generation stage") and the 08-27 answer settles it.

**What changed:** this entry carried his 2026-08-17 position — *full phrases preferred but not a hard rule*, a shorter USE phrase passing if it was standalone-sayable. The 2026-08-27 ruling keeps the preference and adds two things that position did not have: the **BUILD/USE asymmetry** and the **exhaust-the-alternatives-first** condition. Where the two differ, the later one governs. The standalone-sayable test survives as the test for a **short but complete** USE phrase — "something that you could say on its own in a conversation" — not as a licence for a fragment · **⚠ The ten live locations that state "NEVER acceptable" are now nearly right for USE and still wrong for BUILD** — check what each one is scoped to before repointing it, and note that editing those files is R0.11's question, not ours alone.

**P8 — A fragment's fate is decided by WHERE its conflict sits in the course.** · **HARD** · Kai, 2026-08-17: "If it's only later in the course, then it can be a build. If it's both earlier and later, then we should avoid it as a fragment, add context to it so it's clear." · Keeping an earlier-and-later ambiguous fragment as a build leaves the learner meeting a cold prompt with two right answers · **Read with P7 as amended 2026-08-27:** a fragment's *only* available home is a BUILD phrase, so this rule is the test for whether even that home is open. An earlier-and-later ambiguous fragment gets context added, or is not used at all.

**P9 — Every USE phrase must vary along the axis the new LEGO actually changes.** · **HARD** · `ralph-methodology.md:33` · Swapping only the subject pronoun (我→你→他) holds the frame constant and teaches nothing · **BUILD may repeat frames** — its job is to automatise the chunk. **USE must buy a new frame per phrase**, because a low-diversity USE basket pollutes the eternal review pool forever · Partial check: `tools/audit-frame-diversity.cjs`; the builder's own frame-coverage warning is blind to topic-swaps.

**P10 — Every newly-taught LEGO needs practice: at minimum a build phrase and about two use phrases.** · **HARD** for empty (0 build + 0 use), **SOFT** for "fewer than 2 use" · scan-course Check 16 · A LEGO introduced and never practised is not taught · **Reported, never blocks.** Deborah checks this by hand and re-tests it across stages · **Kai hardened the content requirement on 2026-08-28 (see P17 and P18), and the remedy for thin practice is always to AUTHOR MORE PHRASES:** "adding more phrases is great - it improves course quality if the phrases are good. We want to add more phrases. **No reason to try to go for solutions that 'mostly need no new text'.**" · Adequacy of practice stays part of the standing review even after a seed is approved: "do we have enough practice phrases in all the buckets, are all the legos used enough in the rest of the course? These are things we should check even if a seed has been approved" (Kai, 2026-08-11) · **Clash C22 is CLOSED, 2026-09-10: both checks stay warnings, and an empty bucket is a DIAGNOSTIC rather than a defect — P23.**

**P11 — A chunk taught once and never reused later gets no spaced repetition.** · **SOFT — explicitly informational, never blocks** · scan-course Check 17 — **the under-spread check, which keeps that number; the other Check 17 is being renumbered (R0.17)** · Hand-crafted Welsh originals sit at 6–9% orphans; machine-built courses left unchecked sit at 20–55% · **Runnable: `node tools/backfill-spread/analyze.cjs {course} --max-uses 1`** — add `--cjk` for unspaced scripts or the numbers are inflated artifacts · **Ruled informational for good, 2026-09-10 — see P23.**

**P12 — Reinforcement is a taper, and a contrast is never opened before both sides exist.** · **SOFT** · Kai, 2026-08-17: "add LOTS of phrases… to the seed and after… Then add a lot of them for the next few seeds wherever they can fit, then get sparser as you continue." And: "Best not to swap the pronoun before the other one is introduced or it might spook the learners too soon." · Spreading phrases evenly gets the volume right and the shape wrong — the drilling never reaches critical density at the point of confusion.

**P13 — When the first item of a new marked category debuts, practise it only with category-compatible material.** · **HARD (standing pattern)** · Kai, 2026-08-17, explicitly asked to be logged as a general pattern: "This is an issue that comes up often, worth logging it as one so I don't need to re-explain the solution." · **Two tests, both checked against the course data:** (1) the LEGO works in that marked setting as it stands; (2) **no marked counterpart is coming later** — if a more-formal version debuts later, using the plain one here teaches a pairing the learner will later be told was informal · Fragments and short builds from compatible material are legitimate here.

**P14 — Unlikely to come up is NOT a defect.** · **HARD (triage rule)** · Kai, 2026-08-17: "It's fine, weird thing to say, but it's grammatically fine and doesn't sound unnatural. Just something that's unlikely to come up, which is fine." · A sweep flagging "would a learner ever say this?" generates a queue of non-defects and spends Kai's scarcest resource disagreeing with it · **Distinguish "nobody has occasion to say that" (fine) from "nobody would phrase it that way" (fix it).** Raise it only when the phrase ALSO fails on grammar or naturalness.

**P15 — Deliberate ambiguity is a teaching tool; do not normalise it away.** · **HARD (per-course)** · Kai, 2026-08-12, on Finnish seeds 20/21 · He ruled to MIX two confusable cases and drill them, accepting the learner will initially misread the trigger — "the misreading is corrected by exposure, and arriving at the real conditioning from context is the learning" · **The ruling is not valid without the volume of practice phrases it requires** · A ZUT-style or consistency sweep that "normalises" this destroys a deliberate design. **The invariant underneath: the grammatical choice is always made by grammar, never by which pronoun appears.**

**P16 — A USE phrase must satisfy ZUT against the WHOLE course. A BUILD phrase only has to satisfy it up to its own position.** · **HARD** · **Kai, 2026-08-27, closing the scope half of clash C10** · A USE phrase replays out of context at any later point, so a collision that only comes into existence 200 seeds later still reaches the learner cold.

> "USE phrases are important here - they can play at any point later in the course, so **a USE phrase has to satisfy ZUT for the whole course, not just up to the point it's introduced.**" — Kai, 2026-08-27

BUILD phrases play once, in context, and never replay (**P4**), so their ZUT horizon ends where they sit · **Do not claim this is enforced.** A collision that does not exist yet cannot be raised by a check that runs at submission time; the forward half is human judgement, and it is the forward-check already named in P7 and P6.

**P17 — Every practice phrase under a LEGO — BUILD and USE — must contain the WHOLE LEGO. Phrases grow OUTWARD from it, never upward to it.** · **HARD** · Kai, 2026-08-28 · A phrase carrying only part of the LEGO drills something the learner was never taught as a unit, and the LEGO goes unpractised in its own basket · **A phrase may be a fragment of a sentence (P7); it may never be a fragment of the LEGO.**

> "The word should be practiced in all the phrases under the lego - build and use. **All of them should include the lego, not just 'at least one'.**" / "building outward is what I've already understood. I don't believe there are cases of building upwards to the lego in the Welsh course for example, it's all building outwards" — Kai, 2026-08-28

391 upward cases were found across the estate when this was checked · **Refines P10**, which counts *whether* practice exists; this rule says what each phrase must *contain* · **O6** is the obligation that fires when a LEGO changes underneath its phrases.

**P18 — Never move a USE phrase to BUILD in order to manufacture BUILD phrases. Write new ones, or accept the gap.** · **HARD** · Kai, 2026-08-28 · Demoting a good USE phrase takes it out of the eternal pool to satisfy a count, and satisfying a count is not what a BUILD basket is for.

> "If a sentence is good enough to be a use phrase, there's no point moving it to build. We'd just write more phrases if we need builds. They're different kinds of phrases, and **if we can't have one of the kinds for some reason, then we accept it**, we don't move phrases from use to build just to have build phrases." — Kai, 2026-08-28

Pairs with **L21** (sometimes the answer is to add more, not less) and **R0.3** · Cost is not a reason to economise here — see **A10**.

**P19 — A BUILD phrase is its LEGO plus one to three other LEGOs, and you aim short. The unit is LEGOS, not syllables and not words.** · **SOFT** · **Kai, 2026-09-08, closing clash C11** · Counting a BUILD phrase in syllables measures the language rather than the teaching — the same recombination is a different syllable count in every target, which is why six estate documents give six different numbers for it · Count the LEGOs. There is no gate (**P21**).

> "doesn't matter that much. a build should be a **lego + 1-3 other legos, aiming for shorter**." — Kai, 2026-09-08

**This supersedes every absolute length spec for BUILD phrases** — the six competing syllable specs and the six phrase-count specs recorded in the redirect map all measure the wrong thing. **P3** still governs what a BUILD phrase must *do*; this rule only says how big it is. **"Aiming for shorter" is a tendency, not a target — a BUILD basket should still range (P22).**

**P20 — A USE phrase aims LONGER, and there is no hard cap. Two constraints only: never more than one sentence, and never ridiculously long.** · **HARD (the one-sentence constraint), SOFT (the aim and the outer limit)** · **Kai, 2026-09-08, closing clash C11** · A cap authored against the wrong unit clips exactly the long recombinations a USE phrase exists to drill · Read the phrase: is it one sentence, and would a person say it in one breath without losing the thread?

> "use phrases should aim to be longer, but **there's no hard cap - just not more than one phrase and it shouldn't get ridiculously long**. Look at Welsh course phrase lengths for an example - not sure how the build and use phrases are divided since it's a pre-popty course, but it's a great example for the general phrase length." — Kai, 2026-09-08

**"Not more than one phrase" means one sentence** — it is the same constraint **P7** states from the other side, where a USE phrase must be a complete sentence that stands on its own in a conversation. A USE phrase is therefore exactly one sentence: never a fragment, never two. **"Longer" is likewise a direction and not a number: USE phrases should range too (P22).**

**P21 — Phrase length never blocks a submission. It is a preference, not a gate.** · **SOFT** · **Kai, 2026-09-08, closing clash C12: "As I said, it's not that important."** · A length spec enforced as a gate rejects good recombination for failing an arithmetic it was never meant to carry · No check may be added that rejects on length; the existing one warns.

**What the code does today, stated plainly rather than left buried.** `checkPhraseComplexity` (`validation.cjs:162`) does apply hard minimums from seed 6 — 1+ SHORT, 1+ MEDIUM, 2+ LONG for seeds 6–20 and 2/2/3 from seed 21 — and returns `valid: false` when a basket misses them. **Its single caller demotes that to a warning:** `seed-complete.cjs:1759` logs `PHRASE TIERS (warning, not blocking)` and continues, and it skips the check entirely for any LEGO submitted in the build/use format (`usesBuildUseFormat`). So the doctrine and the code agree in effect — nothing is rejected for length — while the function itself still carries thresholds nobody ruled on. **That is a divergence to be visible, not to be tidied:** the thresholds are the residue of the superseded absolute specs (**P19**), and removing them is a code change, not a canon change.

**P22 — A range of phrase lengths is a POSITIVE, not something merely tolerated. Do not author to a target length, and do not "normalise" a basket that ranges.** · **SOFT — but it is a preference in favour of variety, not against it** · **Kai, 2026-09-08, closing clash C11 with P19 and P20** · A course whose phrases all sit at one length drills one rhythm: the learner gets fluent at sentences of that shape and stalls on the short reply and the long one alike, and the basket stops sounding like speech · Read a LEGO's basket end to end and ask whether the lengths vary; there is no gate and none should be added (**P21**).

> "**it's good to have different lengths!**" — Kai, 2026-09-08

**This is why P19 and P20 give directions rather than numbers.** "Aiming for shorter" and "aim to be longer" are the tendencies of two populations that both spread; they are not two target values. A phrase that is unusually short or unusually long for its basket is **not** a defect to be regularised — it is the variety working · **Consequences worth stating.** (1) A sweep that flags phrases for being outside a length band is measuring the wrong thing; if one is run at all it reports the *shape* of a course's distribution, never a per-row verdict. (2) The right reading of a length census is "does this course range like Welsh does" — 28 distinct syllable lengths, no band holding more than 28% of the phrases (**P20**) — not "what is this course's average". (3) The superseded tier thresholds in `checkPhraseComplexity` were at least reaching for this: they ask for a mix of SHORT, MEDIUM and LONG rather than one size. **The instinct behind them survives here; their numbers do not** (**P21**).

**P23 — The practice-coverage checks stay WARNINGS. And an empty bucket is a DIAGNOSTIC, not thin authoring.** · **HARD (that they never block), SOFT (what you do about a zero)** · **Kai, 2026-09-10, closing clash C22** · Gating on an empty bucket buys nothing and costs the signal: whoever hits the gate reaches for the escape hatch, the zero disappears from the report, and the thing it was telling you goes with it · Read the zeroes in a scan as a **reading list of chunks the builder could not author for**, and take them one at a time.

**The reframe is the ruling, not the verdict.** The verdict — *warn* — is what C7 and C8 already predicted. What Kai added is what a zero **means**: the builder is normally good at filling both baskets, so a bucket it left empty is a chunk it **could not author for**, and that is a signal about the chunk. It is usually one of the things this canon already names: a LEGO too big or too small to recombine (**L2**), a bound element with nothing to attach to (**L22**), a chunk whose only phrases would be fragments (**P7**), or the deliberate build-only debut of a marked sense (**K23(e)** route 2).

**Two things follow, and both are the point of writing it down.** (1) **Never satisfy the count by moving a USE phrase to BUILD** — **P18**, ruled separately, is exactly the manoeuvre a gate here would provoke. **P24 — When a LEGO's target form is correct only inside a particular frame, KEEP the LEGO and make every phrase under it SUPPLY the frame — then sweep the whole course for the pattern, in every person, and check the other cases too.** · **HARD** · **Kai, 2026-09-28** (`630a12be`), applied by job #546·I · A phrase that drops the frame drills the form where a native would use a different one: the learner practises *sarebbe stato difficile finire* ("it would have been difficult to finish") for a prompt that means "it would be difficult to finish" · For every LEGO of the kind, read each phrase and ask whether the frame the form needs is present in that phrase; then run the same question over every phrase in the course that carries the form, whether or not it sits under such a LEGO.

> "…and then make sure all the phrases under it follow the pattern, and that we follow the pattern everywhere in the course, and do check the other cases as well" — Kai, 2026-09-28

**The worked example — Italian future-in-the-past.** After a past verb of saying, thinking, knowing or promising, English *would* + verb is the Italian **conditional perfect** (*ha detto che sarebbe stato difficile*, "he said it would be difficult"); standing alone it is the **conditional simple** (*sarebbe difficile*); *would have* + participle is always the perfect. `ita_for_eng` seed 544's LEGO "it would be difficult → sarebbe stato difficile" was right in its seed (*chiunque abbia detto che sarebbe stato difficile…*) and right in the two phrases that sat after *said/thought*, and wrong in five that stood alone. **The LEGO was kept** and the five were rewritten to sit after *I thought… / he said…* — *pensavo che sarebbe stato difficile finire* — rather than growing the LEGO to "said it would be difficult", which was heavier and taught nothing extra. The sweep then read every *would / 'd* row and every Italian conditional in the course (15,600 rows, 1,168 matched): 15 phrases were corrected either way, 640 were already right, and four LEGOs (201, 203, 348, 593) came back to Kai one at a time as genuinely unclear · Detector `tools/course-optimization/ita-future-in-past-2026-09-28.cjs`; report at https://watson-1.tail4968cb.ts.net/d/d5b82237.

**Why keep the LEGO rather than grow it.** The seed's own cut was sound and **L2** says a chunk grows only to the size that makes it meaningful and no further. What was wrong was the *phrases*' failure to carry the condition, and phrases are the cheaper, more local thing to fix (**R0.3**) · **This is K23(d) generalised past two-sense words:** a form that is only right in context must carry its context in every prompt, everywhere in the course — and **P16** is why the sweep is course-wide, since a USE phrase replays cold at any later point · **PR3** is the presentation that tells the learner the frame exists.

**The intro gives the past frame when the LEGO's gloss relies on it — worked example, S0544L02.** "it would be difficult → sarebbe stato difficile" is right *only* after a past frame; heard bare, the learner would expect *sarebbe difficile*. Kai's ruling (2026-09-28 19:15Z, job #580·I): the LEGO stays unchanged, and its introduction carries a demonstration sentence that supplies the frame — *The Italian for 'it would be difficult' in phrases like 'he said it would be difficult' is:* — built only from taught words, preferably one of the LEGO's own phrases. That line was already on the LEGO as a human-marked (guarded) presentation written by job #546·I under Kai's template, so #580·I changed nothing and the strict intro-mirror check holds. The rule generalises: whenever a LEGO's gloss is licensed by a frame the LEGO itself does not contain, the intro's "as in / in phrases like" sentence must show that frame.

Phrases worth drilling therefore live under a NEW LEGO — moved there, edited where needed so each contains it — and a not-new LEGO is never flipped back to new to make its basket play.** · **HARD** · **Kai, 2026-09-28** (22:23Z, job #621·I: *"Not-new LEGOs STAY not new (they are not new for a reason). ## 4. The known side versus the target side

**K1 — The known side is a controlled language too, not free natural language.** · **HARD** · `ralph-methodology.md:45–49` · A prompt using unlicensed machinery is *unmappable* — it forks or stalls production exactly like a target-side ZUT violation · Every prompt must compose from: the known-glosses of introduced LEGOs, the **free class** (glue words, -s/-ed/-ing inflection, "any/ever" under negation, do/does/did), and constructions **licensed by a debuted carrier**.

**K2 — ZUT is the ZERO UNCERTAINTY TEST: prompted in the known language, the learner has no uncertainty about what they are meant to say.** · **HARD — the top rail** · **Read K22 with this: Tom sharpened on 2026-09-08 what the test is asked OF — the delivered prompt, never the component words.** · **Kai, 2026-08-27 and 2026-09-02, closing clashes C9 and C10**; `ralph-methodology.md:39`; `CLAUDE.md` · A learner with a deterministic production function interacts fearlessly; one juggling natural variants freezes.

> "Zero uncertainty test, where when the learner is prompted in the known language, there is no uncertainy (in terms of what they've been taught as LEGOs so far) what they're meant to say. That is, **there can't be two or more valid answers to a prompt based on the legos the learner knows** (real life language is more complicated than this of course, there can be more right answers! Just wanted to acknowledge that)." — Kai, 2026-08-27

**The name** is Zero Uncertainty **Test**. "Zero Unprompted Thinking" (`course-methodology-analysis`) and "Zero Uncertainty Translation" (`COURSE_BUILDER_BRIEF`) are both wrong.

**The direction** is always known-side prompt → target-side production: "(remember the ZUT direction btw! multiple knowns to one target is fine, other way is not)" — Kai, 2026-09-02. `course-methodology-analysis.md:29`, which gives fra_for_eng a French→English direction, is simply wrong. The reverse — one target rendered by several natural known-side phrasings — is *reception*, and is **harmless** (**K3**). Never atomise a construction-feature just because its known-side shadow varies.

**The definition** is the **single-item hesitation test**: one prompt, and whether the learner could reasonably answer it two ways given the LEGOs they hold. **The collision test — two rows sharing a known side — is the mechanised approximation of it, not the definition.** `docs/fix-agent-rules.md:162` reconciled the two on its own authority and guessed the other way · **Scope: a USE phrase must satisfy this against the WHOLE course — P16.** · **One named exception, Kai's own: L24**, where a second sense is deliberately introduced with contrastive context and the resulting ZUT is handled rather than avoided.

**K3 — Two different known prompts mapping to the SAME target is not a defect. The reverse IS.** · **HARD** · Kai, 2026-08-06 · It may be redundant, especially when the twins sit next to each other, but it is not an error · Do not spend attention on the harmless direction · **Bounded by K24** (Kai, 2026-09-10): this allowance is about attention, not about permission, and it runs out where the known word is **basic** and arrives **very early** — there, a harmless-direction clash is still split.

**K4 — ZUT is enforced at the level of the thing that collides.** · **HARD** · Tom, 2026-06-14 · A **LEGO's** colliding known→target is the seed's core wiring — hard reject, fix and resubmit. A **practice phrase's** colliding known is held out per-phrase — never inserted, so the guarantee holds, while the seed and every conforming phrase still save · **ZUT is never a reason to lose a whole seed of good work.** · **Kai ruled the phrase-level half — clash C7 — on 2026-08-27: WARN, do not reject.** "Yes, warning is fine. it shouldn't make it into the course, but there's no reason to reject the whole seed. **Also - we don't know which one of the pair is the bad one.**" So `CLAUDE.md`'s "Same known → different target = reject" is the losing side and should be rewritten (R0.11 says whose call that edit is) · **His second sentence is a design constraint nobody had stated:** a collision is a *pair*, and the checker cannot know which half is defective — so the warning must **name both sides and let a person choose**, and must not silently hold out whichever phrase happened to arrive second.

**K5 — Do not widen the ZUT checker to catch soft conflicts.** · **HARD** · Kai, 2026-08-17: "The ZUT checker is fine as it is, it catches the really obvious ones. The others are a bit soft and fuzzy, so they need us to look at them properly to decide." · Widening it floods the queue with technically-true non-defects · **The severity test is the LEARNER'S EXPERIENCE, not formal correctness:** if the learner says one form and hears another, would they notice — and if they noticed, would it derail them? Italian -o/-a fails both tests · **Kai ruled clash C5 on 2026-08-27, and the question was wrong: these are two different animals, not two ends of one dial.** A flagged collision caused by trivial form variation — his example, two endings that also happen to sound almost the same — **may be left where there is no easy way to resolve it**. The other animal is a defect the checker cannot see at all, and it is **K17**.

**K6 — The known side MAY use uninstructed forms of taught words; only genuinely different WORDS are defects.** · **HARD** · Kai, 2026-08-17: "Sometimes grammar in known is more complicated than the target… the known can use a different case, or conjugation, contraction, gender or whatever if it needs, even if it isn't introduced… They'll try with the closest thing they know, and be pleasantly surprised." · **The controlled-language constraint on the known side is about LEXEMES, not surface forms.** · **Restated and bounded by Kai, 2026-08-28:** "Known side - this is the one that can be a little looser… any slightly different forms like endings, mutations, conjugations, contractions are acceptable, **as long as the learner will easily connect the two concepts without having to think about it**." That last clause is the limit — the latitude is measured in the learner's effort, not in the grammar · **The TARGET side has its own, much tighter answer: K8.**

**K7 — A known-side finding is NOT serious by definition. The real question is: can the learner produce the answer yet?** · **HARD — this is the framing rule that kills most known-side findings** · Kai, 2026-08-17/18 · It killed 467 of 486 findings in a German sample · **Three tiers:**
1. **Fine (design):** a contraction, gender, case or conjugation of a word they know.
2. **Mild defect — fix by removing or editing the phrase:** a *distinct lexeme* for a concept whose mapping they were taught differently. Kai's example: "know" taught as *sapere*, then a phrase needs *conosco* — to the learner it feels like a whole new word, "it could scare a learner a bit too much". It becomes legal the moment any form of *conoscere* is introduced. **This is the calibration point: he names it as "the mildest case I can think of something that should be fixed"** (2026-08-17) — anything milder than sapere/conosco is tier 1 · Worse within this tier: "More serious cases are situations where the words are actually not very related, they just happen to be written the same way." (Kai, 2026-08-17)
3. **Serious:** the learner has NO CHANCE of guessing at all — even the reach for the closest word is unavailable. **Even if the pair is technically correct, the SCARE alone makes it serious.**

This adjudication needs language knowledge, not string matching. **A matcher cannot place a finding on this scale.** Run it as a background programme, course by course: tick off tier-1 acceptable findings first, then take the remainder one by one.

**K8 — The TARGET side stays strict, always.** · **HARD** · Kai, 2026-08-17 · Every target sentence tiles from taught chunks, no exceptions. K6 and K7 are known-side latitude only. · **What may and may not vary, in his own words, 2026-08-28:** "Target side - no, infected [inflected] forms don't count, **it should be exactly the form introduced**. Very very slight differences, such as a mild softening mutation or a slightly different vowel ending… is acceptable because it's barely perceptible to a learner, but nothing more than that. **What is acceptable is that if the lego is more than one word, they can be broken up** (have a word put between the words in the lego) if needed. But all the words should be the same and in the same form as the lego." · **The split clause is the part a strict tiling check would read as a defect and must not:** a multi-word LEGO may have a word inserted between its words, provided every word is present and in the taught form.

**K9 — English must be grammatical AND natural; a ZUT compromise is a rare justified exception, not a licence.** · **HARD (grammar), near-HARD (naturalness)** · Kai, 2026-08-06, explicitly settling a documented contradiction between two methodology docs: "It shouldn't sound very weird, especially when English is the target language. **K23 — THE STANDARD FOR ONE KNOWN WORD WITH TWO TARGET WORDS. Keep the clash, explain the sense in the presentation, and never let the marked sense be prompted without its context.** · **HARD** · **Kai, 2026-09-10** · The commonest wrong fix is to merge the LEGO until the clash disappears, which buys a green check and teaches the learner a chunk nobody asked for; the second commonest is to fix the point of introduction and leave forty later prompts ambiguous · **How to check: (d) is a whole-course audit, and there is no gate for any of this.**

This is the *sapere*/*conoscere* shape: one known word, two target words, each right in its own setting. **Six parts, all of them required.**

**(a) KEEP THE ZUT CLASH. Do not merge the LEGO to defeat it.** A merge is legitimate only where there is an **independent** reason for it — the chunk is inflexible, the sister carries the distinguishing context (**L22**), the gloss will not match the seed (**L25**). "It makes the clash go away" is not a reason. Compare **L24**, where a deliberate, *handled* ZUT is likewise accepted rather than avoided.

**(b) EXPLAIN THE SENSE IN THE PRESENTATION.** Kai's own template:

> "The Italian for 'to know', when you're talking about people, is: …" — Kai, 2026-09-10

This is what **PR1** exists for: a tiny explanation, authored by hand in a fixes pass.

**(c) THE EXPLANATION NEVER NAMES THE SISTER WORD.** Describe only the sense being introduced. No *"not sapere"*, no *"as opposed to"*, no *"there are two words for this"*. **PR2** is the rule; Kai's words are *"only ever describe the sense it's introducing and never mention its sister… We should keep the explanations minimal."*

**(d) THE MARKED SENSE MAY NEVER BE PROMPTED WITHOUT ITS CONTEXT — ANYWHERE IN THE COURSE.** This is the part with teeth.

> "the key is making sure we NEVER use it wrong - the people-know always has people-context in a clear way!" — Kai, 2026-09-10

**A fix at the point of introduction is not a fix.** Every prompt in the course that reaches for the marked sense must carry, visibly in the known side, the context that makes it the only possible answer — and a prompt that does not is edited or deleted (**R0.3**, **K15**). **So this rule obliges a whole-course audit as part of the fix**, which is **R0.1** in its most literal form. **USE phrases are the sharp end** (**P16**): they replay cold at any later point, so a USE phrase carrying the marked sense without its context is a live defect however clear it looked in its own round.

**(e) THE CONTEXT MUST EXIST BEFORE THE MARKED SENSE IS INTRODUCED. There are exactly two legal routes.**

1. **Teach the context first.** Bring in the material that makes the setting sayable — people, in the *conoscere* case — before the marked sense debuts, so every prompt from the debut onward can carry it.
2. **Introduce the MARKED sense with BUILD phrases only, and NO USE phrases**, deliberately without disambiguating context. This is legal because a BUILD phrase plays **once, in context, immediately after the presentation, and never again** (**P4**) — *having just been introduced and being drilled straight afterwards IS the context*, and no BUILD prompt can ever resurface later to confuse anyone.

**Read route 2 carefully, because it is easy to get backwards and an earlier agent did.** It is the **MARKED** sense that goes BUILD-only. Not the general one. The general sense is the default answer to a bare prompt and needs no protection; the marked one is the one that would be ambiguous if it replayed cold, so it is the one denied a USE basket until its context exists · This is a **named exception to P10** — a LEGO with builds and no uses — and it is a deliberate design, not thin authoring (**P23**). Record it as such where you use it, or a coverage sweep will "fix" it.

**(f) MARKED versus DEFAULT — and either may come first.**

- Where **one sense is specific and the other general**, only the **specific** one needs context. The general one is the **default**: a bare prompt means the default, and that is what makes the pair deterministic.
- Where **BOTH senses are specific**, **BOTH require context**. There is no default to fall back on, so neither may ever be prompted bare.
- **Teaching order is independent of this.** *"it might be the marked version that comes first!"* (Kai, 2026-09-10). Marked/default says **which one needs context**, never **which one is taught first** — do not infer one from the other.

**The reasoning Kai OVERTURNED, recorded so it is not re-invented.** It is tempting to say the problem is that the two senses sit far apart in the course — that the learner has forgotten the first by the time the second arrives, and the fix is to move them together. **That reasoning is wrong and he rejected it:**

> "the learner should NOT have stopped thinking about the default sense, they should be using it regularly in the practice phrases still and be very familiar with it." — Kai, 2026-09-10

**Distance is not the defect.** If distance appears to be doing damage, what you have actually found is **thin reuse of the first sense** — the default was taught and then dropped — and that is the thing to fix (**P11**, **L21**: sometimes the answer is to add more, not less). Do not propose reordering the curriculum on these grounds (**S14** is for a genuine mapping-too-early problem, not for this).

**Where this sits.** **L20** is the parent procedure for a two-sense chunk and its routes still stand — a distinct known word first, context in the LEGO second, the "as in" context third (**L24**). K23 is the standard the result is judged against, and it is what replaces the narrower-gloss answer **K11** used to give, because **L26** bars putting the sense into the LEGO's own text.

**A19 — Replacing a clip: which route depends on whether the TEXT or the RECORDING is wrong, and neither route deletes anything.** · **HARD** · Kai's C0 ruling, 2026-08-27; verified against the live code and DB the same day · Deleting a `course_audio` row is irreversible and cascades away that clip's repair candidates, QA flags, human sign-offs, revision history and envelope · Two routes:

- **The RECORDING is bad, the text is right** (clipped, silent, wrong voice, hallucinated words) → **flag it and regenerate the flagged clips** (A20 mechanism 1: raise the flag from the dashboard, then `POST /api/audio/regenerate-role/:courseCode` with `flaggedOnly`; phase 8 renders, veracity-gates, uploads to a new key, then swaps in place at the same id with a revision bump, and clears the flag only on success). - **The TEXT changed** → repair **cannot** be used: `accept` asserts the text did not move and throws if it did. ## 6. Obligations — fix X and you must also fix Y

This section exists because most damage in this system comes from fixes that were correct in isolation.

**O1 — Edit a SEED → sweep the whole course.** Which LEGOs derive from this seed; which phrases use its chunks; which audio (including presentations of affected LEGOs) now speaks superseded text. **Nothing checks this automatically.** The seed's own audio link will silently keep pointing at the old clip (A5).

**O2 — Edit a LEGO's text → fix its presentation clip in the same pass** (A2), and fix every downstream practice phrase that used it (L1).

**O3 — Edit any text → the audio link is already mutated** (A5). Check for NULLs before you overwrite anything; "my audio reverted" is usually a NULL pointer.

**O4 — Strip a disambiguating tag → handle the ZUT collisions it was preventing.** Plan the collision handling into the pass; don't discover it after. Stripping the Greek labels naively would have collided *that*, *when* and *time*.

**O5 — Make any edit → unapprove the affected seeds.** · Kai, 2026-08-11: "Edits justify unapproving a seed… **So it only happens if WE decide to do fixes.**" The trigger is the *edit*, not the *finding* · Scope it to courses genuinely in proofreading — most courses have never been proofread, so an estate-wide rule would flag ~98% of approved seeds.

**O6 — Change a LEGO → check the phrase still respects the LEGO it sits under.** If editing breaks that link, re-shape or delete the phrase so the link survives.

**O7 — Apply a human reviewer's suggestion → validate it against the course first.** · Kai, 2026-08-18: "check that none of her suggestions or fixes affect the course validity" — the "as always" marks it as standing · A proofreader editing a known-side prompt for naturalness can silently break the introduced-before-used chain, create a ZUT collision, or orphan an audio link · **This applies to Kai's own correction lists too** — three items on his 303-item pdc list were overridden for course-wide consistency.

**O8 — Finish a content pass → queue an audio-pass request.** Never by running TTS. Keeps text edits from silently accumulating as a missing-audio backlog.

**O9 — Change pod content → migrate learner progress.** Never edit a live pod in place. Progress is filed under a sentence's *slot*, not its text, so an in-place edit silently credits a learner with a sentence they never heard, with no error and no alarm.

**O10 — Normalise spellings → test for genuine lexical distinctions first.** In pdc, `ken`/`kenn`, `hat`/`hatt` and `alle`/`allee` are **different words**, not spelling variants. This must be tested in any language before any normalisation runs.

**O11 — Replace a clip → make before you break, and delete nothing.** · Kai, 2026-08-27 (C0) · Whichever route you are on (A19), the replacement must exist and be verified before the old link moves, and the old `course_audio` row is never deleted — an unlinked row reaches no learner, and deleting it destroys the repair candidates, flags, sign-offs, revision history and envelope that a revert would need · **If a job can die between your delete and your regenerate, it will, and the slot is silent until someone notices.**

**O12 — Grow, shrink or re-gloss a LEGO → rewrite every phrase UNDER it so each still contains the whole LEGO, AND re-check every phrase ANYWHERE in the course that uses the old gloss.** · **HARD** · **Kai, 2026-09-28** (`c73be6f8`, `630a12be`) · This is **P17** and **O6** with the course-wide half that both were missing: a re-gloss changes what the learner is taught a phrase *means*, and that phrase's English appears under other seeds too · A census over every build and use phrase in the course for the old gloss and the new target, hand-checked, before the pass is called done.

> "In all of these cases, we need to check the phrases by the way! Make them match the new gloss" — Kai, 2026-09-28

> "(not just the phrases under the lego but all phrases that use the gloss in the rest of the course should be checked)" — Kai, 2026-09-28

# PART TWO — WHAT THE MACHINE ACTUALLY ENFORCES

**A rule existing is not the same as a rule being enforced.** This table is the honest version. Read it before you claim a course is clean.

## What genuinely blocks a submission

`POST /api/seed/complete` validates everything before any insert; on error it returns 400 and **nothing is written**.

| Rule | What it blocks |
|---|---|
| **ZUT (LEGO level)** | Same known text already mapped to a different target in an earlier seed |
| **Syllable cap** | LEGO target over 8 syllables — *runs even under skip_validation* |
| **Tiling** | Seed's target not covered by its LEGOs — *runs even for golden seeds* |
| **Target vocabulary** | Any phrase not fully tileable from already-introduced chunks |
| **LEGO containment** | A build/use phrase that doesn't actually contain its LEGO |
| **BUILD anti-filler** | The bare LEGO plus a short filler tag instead of real recombination |
| **Known-side vocabulary** | A prompt using a word not yet introduced — *only where a pair-contract exists* |
| **Length ratio** | Known and target more than 2.5× apart in characters |
| **Phrase counts** | Below 3 BUILD / 5 USE per LEGO (graduated for seeds 1–5) |
| **Balance, 3rd strike** | Phrases leaning on the same over-taught vocabulary |

**Bypasses that define the real perimeter:** `skip_validation` is clamped to seeds ≤ 3. `draft=true` — **or any existing draft row for the course** — disables ZUT and duplicate detection entirely. `x-agent-role: creator` gets a 403; only the checker submits.

## What only warns

Phrase-level ZUT (held out, never rejected — K4) · metadata glosses · frame coverage (self-documented as having false positives and blind to topic-swaps) · known-side construction advisories · **phrase complexity tiers** — the function returns a failure and its only caller logs it as a warning and continues (**P21**; clash C12 is closed).

## Check 20 — seed containment: new 2026-09-10, warn-only by ruling

**What it is.** A check that reads every LEGO against its own parent seed and reports any word in the LEGO the seed does not say — on the **known** side and the **target** side separately. Pure string containment, no linguistic knowledge. It enforces **L26**. `node tools/check-seed-containment.cjs <course_code>`; rule and tokenisation in `tools/seed-containment/tokenise.cjs`, with tests that pin Kai's own example, four measured false positives, and planted defects in Arabic and Devanagari so it cannot go inert in a non-Latin script (**WC-F2**).

**It warns. It always exits 0. It is not in the builder and not in the build validator**, deliberately — Kai: *"We shouldn't touch the builder without having something that definitely works, and then only through talking to Tom"* (**R0.11**).

**The calibration, in Kai's own words, and it is the whole instruction for reading its output:**

> "we shouldn't expect the items it flags to necessarily be wrong, but it'll be good to look at them and why they're different from the seed." — Kai, 2026-09-10

**So a hit is a QUESTION, not a defect.** The output is a reading list. Read the tiers rather than the total: `lexical` (a missing word with no relative in the seed at all) first, `markup` (a bracket or a slash) already covered by Checks 1 and 2, `variant` (every missing word looks like an inflection of a seed word) weakest.

**Its first run, reported honestly.** Roughly **17,400 known-side** and **1,000 target-side** rows flagged across the estate. **Kai has PARKED that population for a proper grouping exercise; it is not a defect list and no fix pass is open on it.** Do not quote the totals as a defect count, and do not start repairing rows off it · **Spaceless scripts are EXCLUDED, never reported clean** — Japanese, Chinese, Thai, Khmer, Lao, Burmese and Tibetan return `excluded_spaceless`, so a course with one of those as its target **has not been checked on its target side**.

**⚠ Two honest caveats about the check's status, neither of them papered over.** (1) As of this writing the check is on the unmerged branch `cs/968-seed-containment-check`, not on `main` — the **rule** at **L26** is Kai's and stands regardless, but the tool may not be in your checkout. (2) **The number 20 is used twice.** `tools/check-untaught-forms.cjs`, on `main`, declares itself "scan-course Check 20 — UNTAUGHT FORMS", and the seed-containment check takes the same number in `scan-course.md`. **That is the shape R0.17 already ruled on for the two Check 17s: cite these checks by NAME, never by number**, until somebody renumbers one. Which one moves is not this canon's call · *(Measured on `main`, 2026-09-10: `scan-course.md` runs …18, 19, then 21 — **20 is skipped in the file entirely**, so the number is claimed twice and documented nowhere.)*

## Where the checks are blind — read this before quoting a zero

These are measured, not suspected:

- **The known-side vocabulary gate is INERT on 31 courses.** `validation.cjs:818` tokenises with `split(/[^a-z']+/)`, so every Devanagari, Tamil, Arabic and CJK character is a separator, the token list comes back empty, and the function returns "no problems". 13 language families affected. A fix exists on `fix/known-side-tokenizer-unicode-2026-08-17` (commit `1104a6c1`), **not merged**. Accented Latin is *shredded* rather than skipped — 41 Azerbaijani words become 70 fragments — which is worse, because the gate then compares garbage.
- **The same gate silently skips 60 of 145 courses** — the contract fallback only fires for `*_for_eng`. No warning, no log line. The largest known-side leak population sits in exactly those skipped courses.
- **Check 14 (question marks) is superseded by Check 21 — `node tools/check-question-marks.cjs <course>`, 2026-09-10 — and the word-boundary bullet that stood here was half wrong.** The trap is real: `\b` is a boundary between a word character and a non-word character, so it fails when an alternative *ends* in a non-ASCII letter and is followed by a space. Measured against the literal patterns, **five entries die estate-wide**: `qué` and `por qué` (spa), `où` (fra), `perché` (ita), `você` (por). But **`cómo`, `cuándo`, `dónde` and `quién` fire perfectly well** — the accent is in the middle, so the boundary after the final `o`/`n` exists — and French `qu'` fires too, because what follows the apostrophe is a letter. The earlier text named four words as undetectable that are not, which is worth knowing as a case in its own right: **the mechanism, not the character set, decides**, and a probe that appends a space to every alternative will mis-call the apostrophe forms. The trap was never the biggest hole anyway. Against a 214-defect hand-confirmed set on `ita_for_eng`, Check 14 found **53**, and the accents account for none of that gap on an English-known course: it lost **0 of 48** side-mismatches to its own "both sides must look like questions" rule, and dropped every question under three words. Both are now regression-tested in `tools/check-question-marks.test.js`.
- **Check 6 (unpronounceable) flags EVERY phrase as garbage** on Hindi, Hebrew, Thai and the Indic courses — their scripts are missing from its keep-list.
- **Check 3/4 (wrong language) has detectors for five scripts only.** Cyrillic, Greek, Hebrew, Devanagari, Thai, Tamil, Kannada, Telugu, Georgian and Ge'ez are invisible. Latin Extended-A (Polish ł, Welsh ŵ, Turkish ğ) is misread as "not Latin".
- **Check 13b returns `NaN` on caseless scripts and reads as a pass.**
- **Check 15 (identical known/target) has a five-pair cognate allowlist**, all `eng|X`. Every related-language pair — cat_for_spa, bre_for_fra, nld/deu — reports its legitimate cognates as corruption.
- **Checks 11 and 12 are inert on CJK and Thai**: the tokeniser produces one token for a whole sentence.
- **scan-course as written never fetches `is_new`, `type`, or `presentation_audio_id`**, so Checks 10, 12 and 18 report zero if run literally, and Check 11 crashes. There is **no committed runnable implementation of Checks 1–16** — every agent that has "run scan-course" wrote a throwaway script.
- **Check 18 was fixed 2026-08-18.** The old inline matcher parsed 21,342 of 72,063 clips and silently skipped 50,721; against 229 known drifted rows it flagged 2. The replacement covers 72,058/72,063 and exits non-zero below 99%.
- **The gate has never examined the ~1M rows already in the database.** It runs at submit time only.

## Cases where the FALSE POSITIVE was the lesson

"Why this looked like a defect and was not" is as valuable as "why this was one."

### WC-F1 — 1,170 Catalan hits that were just cognates
**Demonstrates: R0.6, and the checklist below.**
A known-side answer-leak detector — calibrated on 27 real positives, not a naive probe — returned **1,322 hits**. The truth was **142 high-confidence, 42 of them new**. **1,180 of 1,322 (89%) were false**, concentrated in two courses: `cat_for_spa` (1,170) and `bre_for_fra` (10). The detector's identity is *string overlap between the known and target fields*, and Spanish and Catalan simply share words: `recordar una palabra` → `recordar una paraula`. The shared run `recordar una` is cognate vocabulary, not a leak; the known side is proper Spanish throughout.

**Caught by** refusing to hand up a raw count: sorting by course showed 88% in one minority course, which is the shape of an artefact, not a defect population. Real positives look nothing like it — `eng_for_spa` S54 `queríamos give you más tiempo`.

**The rule:** before believing a cross-field string-overlap count, check the genetic distance between the two languages. Precision on unrelated pairs was ~96%; without the exemption, the estate's cleanest minority courses read as its worst.

### WC-F2 — The check that returned a clean pass because it could not read the alphabet
**Demonstrates: A3, K12, and the enforcement table.**
The known-side vocabulary gate — the check the estate treats as catching the most mistakes — reported **zero violations** on Hindi, Tamil, Japanese and Arabic. Not lenient: **inert**. Feeding real `known_text` through the exported function showed **13 language families and 31 courses tokenise to zero tokens**. The cause is one line: `split(/[^a-z']+/)`. Every non-ASCII character is a separator, the violation loop never executes, and the function returns "no problems".

**Caught by** reading the code, then confirming by direct invocation on live text.

**The rule:** a zero from a check is only as wide as its tokeniser. **Feed the check one string you know is bad in the target script.** If it returns clean on a planted defect, you have measured nothing — report "the gate is inert here", never "the course is clean".

### WC-F3 — Check 18 looked at 30% of the estate and reported clean
**Demonstrates: A3 — the coverage principle.**
Check 18 reported clean course after course. Against a known set of **229 drifted rows it flagged 2**. It was parsing **21,342 of 72,063** presentation clips and silently skipping **50,721**. One template with a hardcoded connector — `, as in` — followed by `if (!m) continue`. The estate mostly says `, is:`. A census found **13,762 distinct narration skeletons**: Tamil, Hindi em-dash forms, Kannada, Chinese, Korean; two families carry no quotes at all (legacy Welsh `<src>`/`<tgt>` markup, and a Japanese frame); Dutch SSML actively poisons a quote scanner.

**Caught by** measuring coverage instead of trusting the verdict — running the template against every clip and counting the `continue`s. The replacement parses **delimiters, not sentences**, covers 99.99%, returns an explicit `unparsed` status so nothing is dropped silently, and **exits non-zero below 99%**. It now flags 226 of the 229, and surfaced 2,744 new drift rows across 70 courses.

**The rule:** a detector must report its own coverage next to its verdict, and a coverage shortfall must be a **failure, not a footnote**. Any matcher with `if (!match) continue` is a silent-skip machine.

### WC-F4 — Arabic tokenisation invented 1,126 defects
**Demonstrates: R0.9, and the checklist.**
The estate-wide untaught-word audit read `ara_for_eng` at **1,126 high-confidence defects** — by far the worst course on the estate. After fixing the normaliser the same course reads **0**. Estate-wide residue fell **1,697 → 552** on that single change. The tokeniser stripped Latin punctuation only and folded diacritics via a Unicode range that does not contain Arabic marks, so `هنا؟` never matched the taught `هنا`.

**Caught by** the *vocabulary* of the defect list: a pile of ultra-basic function words (yes, how, there, not) reading "never taught" is an orthography bug, because no released course fails to teach "yes". The worker found it mid-run and re-ran before reporting.

**The rule:** when a script-specific audit names a course as your worst, sanity-check the defect list against core function words, and re-run the identical check on a course you already believe is clean.

### WC-F5 — The zero that could not fail
**Demonstrates: the difference between verifying and re-measuring your own guard.**
"**0 out-of-corpus target tokens across 1,093 generated rows, 0 forward references**" was reported on `pdc_for_eng` as independent verification. It is **0 by construction**. `checkTiling` already validates every LEGO target against the seed at submit time, and phrases are rebuilt from already-introduced chunks — the vocabulary can hardly escape the corpus it was validated against. The zero confirms the guard is working and carries no information about whether the content is right. Note also that `checkTiling` validates the **target side only** — the known side is genuinely unvalidated, which is where independent signal lives.

**The rule:** ask what would have to be true for this check to fail. If the answer is "the builder violated its own validator", the zero measures the validator.

### WC-F6 — A tool whose header claimed a calibration it failed
**Demonstrates: R0.9.**
`filler-build-scan.cjs` defines a filler BUILD phrase structurally — the LEGO plus a residue of 1–3 tokens — with no notion of semantic emptiness. Deborah's actual complaint is padding that adds no new grammar (`here`, `yesterday`, `before`); a residue of `Spanish` or `to help` is genuine recombination and exactly what the method wants. Its RAW→CONFIRMED promotion requires the residue to recur 3+ times, which `my`, `your`, `a` and `the` satisfy trivially — **the promotion adds no evidence while sounding like verification**. ~81% false positives.

**Caught by** running the tool against the five examples its own header claimed it reproduced. It flagged zero of them.

**The rule:** a tool's header is a claim, not evidence. Replay its stated calibration examples before quoting a single number, and check what any RAW→CONFIRMED promotion actually excludes.

### WC-F7 — Two false RECONSIDERs, both the tool's own fault
**Demonstrates: uniformity is the signature of an artefact.**
On its first production use, the edit-impact tool reported that **every** phrase edit had "1 other row carrying the identical old text", and that **every** CJK edit's proposed text was taught "at no point at all". Both artefacts. A display label was compared against an identity, so self-exclusion never fired and the count was always the edited row itself; and `words()` split on a space, so a Japanese known side is one token that can never match a LEGO.

**The rule:** when a detector fires on 100% of a class, suspect the detector, not the class. And never compare a display string to an identity.

### WC-F8 — 27 untaught-vocabulary candidates, 1 real
**Demonstrates: K6, K7.**
The tool reproduced **27** untaught-vocabulary candidates for the day's `eng_for_sin` edits. Exhaustive adjudication found **1 real breach**. **13** were inflectional variants of a word already taught (`ඔයාගෙන්` ← `ඔයා`, seed 1); **13** were inherited, not introduced by the edits — `මිනිස්සු` had been in the course since seed 69. The matcher is unstemmed and exact-surface, **and says so in its own caveat**, which nobody had acted on.

**The rule:** read the tool's own caveat and price it in. For any inflecting language an exact-form "untaught" count is an upper bound, not a finding. And always ask: *did my change introduce this, or did I just become the first person to look?*

### WC-F9 — 15 Spanish mismatches invented by two normalisers that disagree
**Demonstrates: A7, and the checklist.**
During the Spanish `poder` audio-generation run, 2026-08-27, a verification pass comparing each generated clip's text against its database row raised **15 apparent mismatches. All 15 were false.** The JavaScript-side `normalizeForAudio` **keeps** a trailing question mark; the database-side normaliser **strips** it. Two normalisers in the same system disagree, so any comparison routed through both reports differences that do not exist.

**Caught by** the worker noticing the shape of the difference and re-running on a direct text comparison, which came back clean.

The same run also named a genuinely benign class, so nobody re-investigates it: **40 PRE-EXISTING clips differ from their row text by capitalisation or a trailing full stop only** — same words. This predates the work and is not a defect (A7).

**The rule:** **when a check compares text through a normaliser, verify the normaliser against a known-identical pair before trusting any mismatch it reports.** A punctuation-only or case-only difference is almost never a real defect — but a **question mark can change the sound** (A7, A9), so it is not simply noise either. The two cases must be told apart, never filtered wholesale.

## Before you believe a count

1. **Report coverage next to the verdict.** A verdict without a denominator is not a result. Below ~99%, the run failed.
2. **Grep your own matcher for `continue`, `if (!match)` and early returns.** Every one is a silent skip. Make "unparsed" a first-class status.
3. **Plant a known defect and confirm the check fires** — especially in the target script.
4. **Check the tokeniser against the script before the data.** `\b`, `[a-z]`, `split(' ')` and NFD all fail outside ASCII Latin, silently, usually in the direction that reads as clean.
5. **Ask what would have to be true for this check to fail** — and whether an earlier gate already forbids it.
6. **A uniform verdict is an artefact.** 100% of a class firing, or 0%, means suspect the detector first.
7. **Check the relationship between the two things you are comparing.** Related languages break string-overlap detectors; dialect courses make standard spellings miss wholesale.
8. **Look at the vocabulary of your defect list, not just its size.** Basic function words reading "never taught" is an orthography bug.
9. **Replay the tool's stated calibration examples.**
10. **Read the tool's own caveats and price them in.**
11. **Distinguish "my change caused this" from "I am the first to look here."**
12. **Re-run the check on a course you believe is clean.** The known-side noise floor is ~255 per 1,000 even on mature English courses — **judge by distance from the floor, not from zero.**
13. **State the check's population coverage.** "The gate would catch that" is only true where the gate runs.
