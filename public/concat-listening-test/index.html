How far in does the join stop mattering?

Two versions of the same Welsh phrase. One was recorded in a single continuous take, the other built by gluing separately-recorded pieces. Each phrase is labelled with where in the course a learner meets it — early, middle or late. Judge each one on its own terms: a beginner at seed 1 has nothing to compare against, and by seed 300 the ear is a different instrument.

What am I listening to, exactly?

Twenty-four phrases spread across the course — eight early (seeds 1–30), eight middle (93–200), eight late (260–334) — then eleven pod extras at the end that sit outside the course.

Every clip already existed; nothing was recorded or generated for this test. The glued version is made from real recordings of that phrase's own pieces, joined with the same processing the live splicer uses: each piece trimmed to its speech, levelled to the same loudness, then stuck together.

Both versions get identical trimming, levelling and encoding, so loudness or file quality can't give the game away — only the joins can. The position label tells you where you are in the course; it says nothing about which side is which.

The eleven pod extras at the end are the cleanest join test there is: Aran recorded them whole, and a later pass cut those very takes into pieces. Re-gluing them changes nothing but the joins. Eight of the eleven were confirmed against the waveform as genuine excerpts; the other three turned out to be a different take of the same words, and say so when you reach them. They have no course position, which is why they come last.

One honest caveat on the course phrases: their pieces come from separate course recordings rather than one read of the phrase, so a few drag more than a real fast pass would. If a glued one still sounds fine, it will sound at least as good in production.
