Aran's cloned voice: a listening test

Every clip on this page is the voice clone made from Aran's recordings, reading real lines from SaySomethingin courses. There are no human recordings here.

The audio is in the same quality the courses use. Headphones help.

Introductions

Ten introductions from several courses: a welcome, grammar points, encouragement and the longer talks given during breaks.

1WelcomeGerman course

Welcome to this unusual game that will help you to become a German speaker. Here's how it works - I teach you something in German, then I say something in English and give you a chance to say it out loud in German. Then, you listen carefully to the German speakers who will model it for you. It's important that you say something in the gaps, because that's how you learn. Even if you aren't sure, have a go at saying whatever you can, and you'll be starting to speak in German sooner than you think. The more you treat it as a game, the more playful you are with it, the more you laugh when you make mistakes, the better it will go and the faster you will learn. So, let's start playing.

If you got the “I want to speak” right then that’s brilliant—we didn’t teach you that, that’s your own natural ability to learn a language. If you didn’t—don’t worry, most people take a bit of time to get used to the way a SaySomethingin course works. Now, the Welsh for Welsh, as in the Welsh language, is Cymraeg, Cymraeg.

3Encouragement, then a new wordWelsh course (North)

Now you’re doing extraordinarily well—you’ve learnt a huge amount in not much more than a quarter of an hour—well done! Now let’s carry on and just add a few more new words. The Cymraeg for I need is dw i angen. Dw i angen. And like isio, you don’t need the n sound with this.

Did you notice we were saying deud o instead of deud hi? In Cymraeg, it can be masculine or feminine—o is masculine. But don’t worry about this, you will get used to it just by listening, and it doesn’t matter at all if you say it the other way around. Now, to make this a question, we change it into fedri di ddeud? 5A grammar pointWelsh course (South)

You already know how to say I didn’t as in I didn’t understand, wnes i ddim deall. Another way to say I didn’t in Cymraeg is do’n i ddim. Do’n i ddim. We use it with things that last for more time, but don’t worry about that—you’ll get used to it naturally, and everyone will understand if you swap them!

6EncouragementWelsh course (North)

It’s really important to remember that when you feel incredibly frustrated at not quite getting a word right, that means you’re really close to learning it successfully—the frustration is a huge sign of success, so don’t lose heart.

7A break: frustrationWelsh course (North)

Do you get frustrated when you can’t remember a word you’ve been trying to learn? It’s an incredibly common feeling… You’re trying to remember it, you know it’s not far off (you might even say “It’s on the tip of my tongue!”)—and then someone else says it, and you feel like kicking yourself—you were SO close! Now, here’s the thing… some people get so tired of feeling that frustration, they end up losing momentum—and their journey towards their new language gets interrupted (or even, sometimes, just stops). Frustration can stop you from becoming a confident speaker—but if you do a bit more learning for me now, I’ll tell you how to cure the problem of frustration.

8A break: mistakesWelsh course (North)

Now, I’ve been asking you to make mistakes, and if you’re like most people, you don’t like making mistakes. You were probably told at school that mistakes are bad. But when you’re learning a new language, mistakes are good. There are only two kinds of mistakes—the first are ones that you don’t notice, which aren’t a problem because you don’t notice them. The second are ones where you notice that what you said was different to the model you heard. For you to notice that, your brain has to compare what you said to the model, and identify a difference. And we have another word for that process—when your brain gets the input it needs to change—we call it learning. Every time you notice yourself making a mistake, you’re actually noticing your brain learning more. So please, keep on making those mistakes—the more you make, the faster you learn. And in our next break, I’ll help you start to feel better about when you get frustrated.

9A break: speaking out loudWelsh course (North)

You are speaking in the gap, aren’t you? I mean, out loud? Even if it feels embarrassing sometimes? We’ve heard about learners speaking on the bus, on the train, while taking a dog for a walk—it feels weird at first, but you soon get used to it. Why should you feel embarrassed that you’re learning a wonderful new skill? And the speaking is very important—because it adds extra neural circuits to the memories that you’re building, and the more neural circuits we add to a memory, the stronger it becomes. If you speak out loud in every single gap—you don’t have to be perfect, just say whatever words you can remember—then you are building stronger memories, and you’re building them faster, and you’re going to be more successful. So speak out loud in every single gap, even if it surprises the person sitting next to you on the bus! And while you’re speaking out loud, I’ll be getting ready to tell you something about the settings in our next break…

10A break: the brainCroatian course

Have you ever heard of a hemispherectomy? It's quite a rare operation, thank heavens. It's usually performed on children, and in most cases it's an attempt to cure extreme epilepsy, or some other very significant brain damage. It involves entirely removing one half of the brain. Now, if you not being very good at science, or not having a very strong working memory, means that your brain isn't as good as the 'clever' people, then actually physically having half a brain removed must mean that those people can't really function, mustn't it? They should be unable to lead an independent life, shouldn't they? Learn a little more of your new language for me, and I'll tell you the answer in a bit.
