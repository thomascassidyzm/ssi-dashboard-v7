# DL-2026-09-10-03 — The `iarracht` / `ag iarraidh` split holds in BOTH Irish courses

- **Date:** 2026-09-10
- **Decided by:** Kai
- **Status:** STANDING
- **Produced:** **K24** (the reusable half — a clash in the harmless direction still fails when the known word is basic and arrives very early) · **S16** companion paragraph · `lang/gle.md` · cited by **HB-02**

## DECIDED

`iarracht` carries *try* and `ag iarraidh` carries *want* — one known sense to one target form — in `gle_for_eng` **and** in the Connemara course `gle_cn_for_eng` alike. The Connemara course gets no exemption.

## CONTEXT

The Irish *try* work of 2026-09-10 had established a preference for the finite and infinitival shapes of `iarracht` over the progressive (**S16**). A follow-up recommendation proposed exempting the Connemara course `gle_cn_for_eng` from the sense split, on dialect-attestation grounds, and let `ag iarraidh` carry both *try* and *want* there. Kai was asked to rule.

## REASONING

> "Stick to the original ruling - it's harmless in terms of clashes, but such a basic word so early, will throw off learners." — Kai, 2026-09-10

He granted both halves of the case against him in the same sentence — that the clash is harmless in the technical sense this canon itself uses (**K3**: two known senses landing on one target word is the harmless direction), and, implicitly, that the dialect attestation is real. He ruled the other way anyway, and the reason is **not a fact about Irish**: it is that "harmless direction" stops being a safe test when the known word is very basic and the learner meets it very early, because at that point the learner has nothing else to disambiguate against and simply gets thrown. That is a rule about clashes, reusable in any language, and it is why this entry produced **K24** rather than an Irish note.

## EVIDENCE, AND ITS LIMITS

The preference in **S16** rests on an **absence**: none of Ó Dónaill, focloir.ie or de Bhaldraithe illustrates the progressive of this construction at all — every example in all three is finite or infinitival. Three independent lexicographic sources reaching for the same two shapes and never the third is the whole basis.

**Limits, stated because the frequency claim is lexicographic and not measured:**

- **No corpus counts exist.** `corpas.ie` could not be rendered and Pota Focal refused the connection.
- The rule rests on **four dictionaries plus one attested prose text**, nothing more.
- **Two paragraphs of the underlying research were explicitly self-labelled by their author as feel rather than source.**
- Recorded as **GAPS 24**: if a future reader can reach a corpus, this is the first entry to re-check.

## REJECTED, AND WHY

**Rejected: exempting `gle_cn_for_eng` and letting `ag iarraidh` carry both senses there.** Stated at its strongest, because it was a strong case:

- **de Bhaldraithe** — the foundational Cois Fharraige lexicographer, and the nearest thing the Connemara course has to a source of record — glosses `ag iarraidh` as *trying to* throughout his own dictionary.
- **Ó Dónaill** and **Bedell's 1685 Bible** do the same.
- **No dedicated alternative exists that Connemara natives reach for instead**, so the exemption was not proposing a gap, it was proposing to follow the dialect.
- The clash it would create is in the direction this canon calls harmless (**K3**).

**Why it lost:** the learner-facing cost of a very basic word arriving very early with two senses outweighs both the dialect attestation and the technical harmlessness of the clash direction. Source authority does not beat learner confusion at seed 1. **This is the transferable part of the entry** — a recommendation can be better sourced than the ruling that overturns it and still be wrong, because the two are answering different questions.
