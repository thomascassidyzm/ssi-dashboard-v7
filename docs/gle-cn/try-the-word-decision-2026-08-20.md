# The Connemara word for "try" — decided

**Course: Connemara Irish for English speakers. Draft. No learner can reach any of it.
No audio exists for this course at all — not one clip — so nothing in this plan costs money.**

---

## The decision, in one line

**The course teaches ONE word for "try": `iarraidh`.**

It is the word the course already teaches at seed 1 for "want". A learner meets it in their
first minute and never has to learn a second word for trying.

| what the learner sees | what they say | syllables |
|---|---|---|
| trying | **ag iarraidh** | 3 |
| to try / tried | **iarraidh** | 2 |

That is the whole system. It is the same shape as every other verb this course already
teaches — *foghlaim* / *ag foghlaim*, *caint* / *ag caint*, *stopadh* / *ag stopadh*. The
learner is not learning a new pattern. They are learning one more word that behaves exactly
like ones they already have.

**`iarracht a dhéanamh` goes. `mo dhícheall a dhéanamh` stays but stops being a "try" word.
`ag triail` and `traíáil` stay banned — though the second of those is a closer call than
anyone has admitted, and section 4 puts the case against my own recommendation.**

## Kai — you were right, but not for the reason you thought

You said *iarracht a dhéanamh* is too long for a word a beginner meets in the first hour and
then uses constantly. **The length is real. The "uses constantly" is not — and that turns out
to be the more useful half.**

Here is what a learner actually meets in the first ten sentences:

| seed | English | Irish now | which "try" word |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | I want to speak Irish with you now | Tá mé **ag iarraidh** Gaeilge a labhairt leat anois | — (this is "want") |
| 2 | I'm trying to learn | Tá mé **ag iarraidh** foghlaim | **ag iarraidh** |
| 6 | I'm trying to remember a word | Tá mé **ag iarraidh** cuimhneamh ar fhocal | **ag iarraidh** |
| 7 | I want to try as hard as I can today | Tá mé ag iarraidh **mo dhícheall a dhéanamh** inniu | **mo dhícheall a dhéanamh** |
| 8 | I'm going to try to explain what I mean | Tá mé chun **iarracht a dhéanamh** a mhíniú céard atá i gceist agam | **iarracht a dhéanamh** |

**Four of the first eight sentences ask for "try". Three different Irish answers.** That is the
defect. The long one is only one of the three, and it appears exactly once in the first hour.

So the fix is not "find a shorter word". The fix is **stop having three words**. And once you
ask which of the three should survive, the answer stops being a matter of taste: it is the one
already there, already short, and already what Connemara says.

**One thing on the record, because you asked to be corrected rather than agreed with.**
Shortening the *word* barely shortens the *sentence*. Seed 8 is ~18 syllables today; with my
recommendation ~15. The estate norm for that sentence is 13–14 (French *Je vais essayer
d'expliquer ce que je veux dire*; Finnish *Mä aion yrittää selittää mitä mä tarkoitan*). My
change closes about half the gap. The rest of the length sits in *chun* and in *céard atá i
gceist agam*, not in the word for "try". **If seed 8 still feels long after this, the remaining
problem is a different one and I should not pretend I have solved it.**

## 1. Where "try" appears, and how often

**22 of the 668 English sentences ask for "try".** It first appears at **seed 2** — the second
thing a learner ever says. Then seeds **6, 7, 8**, then a long gap to seed 50.

After that: seeds 50, 102, 103, 140, 146, 159, 195, 205, 213, 222, 226, 236, 372, 407, 491,
541, 579, 638. Front-loaded, then steady — it never goes away, but it is never dense again
after the opening.

**The senses, and whether they want the same word.** Fourteen are the plain progressive —
*I'm trying to…*, *we're not trying to…*, *what she was trying to create*. No argument about
those. Eight are not progressive, and this is where the course has been stuck:

> **seed 7** — I want to try as hard as I can today
> **seed 8** — I'm going to try to explain what I mean
> **seed 146** — nothing seems to be working since we tried to fix it
> **seed 236** — I know someone who said that she was going to try to help
> **seed 407** — shouldn't we try to set a good example?
> **seed 491** — I love the way you try to help
> **seed 541** — it's a good idea to try and breathe slowly
> **seed 579** — we've often tried

Seed **579** is the sharp one: *"we've often tried"* has nothing after it. There is no "trying
to do" anything. Any solution that only handles *trying to* fails this sentence.

**They do not all want the same grammatical shape — but they all want the same WORD.** That is
what unlocks this, and section 3 is where it comes from.

## 2. How the evidence was gathered — and why the numbers can be trusted

The Connemara authority is Ó Curnáin's *The Irish of Iorras Aithneach*, four volumes, 686 pages
of recorded speech from Connemara speakers born between 1852 and 1980.

**These volumes cannot be searched normally.** They are largely phonetic transcription in a
custom font, and ordinary text search declares them unreadable and returns **zero for words
that are demonstrably on the page**. A false zero here reads as "Connemara does not have this
word" and has already come close to becoming a ruling. Every count below was made with a reader
built for it, and every single run was calibrated first against three words of known frequency
— they came back at 121, 521 and 3,133, exactly as they should. **The zeros in this document
are real zeros.**

---

## 3. Why `iarraidh` wins

**Ó Curnáin lists "Trying" as a meaning of this exact word.** His volume IV glossary, verbatim:

> **iarraidh**, f. 1. Request, plea… **2. VN, Trying.** … 3. Blow, stroke.

And his index says the same: *"iarraidh, f, request, **trying**, turn, blow."*

This is the point that settles it. It is not inference from a dictionary of standard Irish. It
is the Connemara authority, describing this dialect, listing **trying** as a sense of the word
the course already teaches.

**And here is the single most useful line in the whole corpus.** Under the entry for the
dialect's other "try" word, Ó Curnáin adds a note about who uses it:

> *"(Used by younger generation for **ag iarraidh** of older generation)"*

That is a lexicographer stating, flatly, that **`ag iarraidh` is how the older generation of
Connemara said "try"**, and that younger speakers have replaced it. `ag iarraidh` is not a
workaround, and it is not a compromise. **It is the traditional Connemara word for trying.**

**In recorded speech**, with Ó Curnáin's own English attached:

> *"ag iarraidh a bheith ag obair dhen bhuíochas"* — **"trying to work despite it all"**
>
> *"chuir sé amach a bhutún ag iarraidh rud a dhéanamh is chinn sé air"* — he tried his utmost
> and **failed** at it. You cannot fail at *wanting* something; this one can only be "try".
>
> *"bhí mé ag iarraidh fuarú an súp"* — **I was trying to cool the soup**

Note the shape of that last one — *ag iarraidh* followed straight by a plain verb, no
scaffolding. That is exactly seed 2's *Tá mé ag iarraidh foghlaim*.

**The counts.**

| form | in recorded Connemara |
|---|---|
| **ag iarraidh** | **173** |
| iarracht, as a free word in running speech | **0** |
| **iarracht a dhéanamh** | **0** — a categorical zero |
| tabhairt faoi, in the "attempt" sense | **0** |

**And Ó Dónaill files "want" and "try" as one sense, not two.** Under the verb *iarr*, sense 2
is headed **"Attempt"** — and inside that single sense he gives both readings:

> *Bhí siad ag iarraidh a bheith ag magadh fúinn* — **they were trying to make fun of us**
> *Níl mé ag iarraidh éirí go fóill* — **I don't want to get up yet**

**Finally, the eight hard sentences are already solved — by SSi's own released Irish course.**
It is built on these same 668 English sentences, and it uses this one word for **20 of the 22**,
including every awkward frame:

> **seed 236** — *…she was going to try to help*
> → *…go raibh sí **chun iarraidh** cabhrú*
>
> **seed 407** — *shouldn't we try to set a good example?*
> → *nach cheart dúinn **iarraidh** dea-shampla a thabhairt?*
>
> **seed 541** — *it's a good idea to try and breathe slowly*
> → *is smaoineamh maith é **iarraidh** análú go mall*
>
> **seed 579** — *we've often tried*
> → *Tá muid tar éis **iarraidh** go minic*
>
> **seed 146** — *…since we tried to fix it*
> → *…ó **d'iarr** muid é a dheisiú*

Seed 579 was the one with nothing after it. The bare word handles it. And seed 236 is the
identical construction to our seed 8 — **"going to try to" → *chun iarraidh*** — so the form I
propose for seed 8 is not invented. It is already in a released Irish course, in the same
sentence shape.

**A note on what NOT to copy.** SSi's Ulster course uses *ag iarracht* throughout
(*tá mé ag iarracht smaoineamh*). That is a real Ulster form. It is not a Connemara one, and
this course was right to kill it. Finding it alive in Ulster explains where it came from and is
a good reason to keep it out of here.

## 4. The candidates that lose — including the one that nearly won

**`iarracht a dhéanamh`** — correct standard Irish; Ó Dónaill gives it. In Connemara it is a
**hard zero**. Not rare — absent. Every apparent hit was checked one by one: most are compound
words from a single elicitation exercise about prefixes, two are a quotation from a *Munster*
poet printed in the front matter, seven are a grammar test using the word to demonstrate
something else entirely, and the one spontaneous example was contradicted by the speaker's own
husband and then withdrawn by the speaker. There is no *iarracht* entry in the Connemara
glossary at all. **A course cannot honestly claim this is Connemara.** Five syllables. Its only
virtue is that it is unmistakable.

**`ag triail`** — already banned, and the ban is right. Ó Curnáin's index has *triail* as the
verb of *triall*, **"journey, travel"** — a completely separate word — and every single
occurrence in the corpus is that journey verb. Learners who read it as "trialling" were reading
it correctly.

**`féach le`** — the standard idiom, and de Bhaldraithe gives *féach lena chríochnú anocht* for
"try and finish it tonight". **It is a museum piece.** In the entire four volumes there are
**three** running-speech instances, and all three are from speakers born between 1869 and 1889.
None from anyone born later. None in the glossary, which lists no "try" sense for the word at
all. And Ó Curnáin says so himself: the by-form carrying the sense is *"now obsolescent"* and
unfamiliar to a speaker born in 1921. On top of that it would collide: the course has 24
sentences coming that need "see" (*feiceáil*) and 8 that need "look". **A course could honestly
call *féach le* an archaism attested in the dialect. It could not call it how Connemara says
"try".**

**`tabhairt faoi`** — the dictionary gives it, and an earlier pass counted 8 hits. Those 8 were
contamination — confession-going and cart-bringing. **The attempt sense is zero.** It also
fails the *no bare frames* rule: "give" plus "about", recombined into something a learner could
never guess.

### `traíáil` — the case against my own recommendation

This deserves more than a line, because **on the dialect rail alone it beats my answer.**

*Traíáil* is Connemara's own word for "try", borrowed from English long enough ago that Ó
Curnáin classes it as fully naturalised. It is not a glossary curiosity. It is **common running
speech from at least twelve distinct speakers born between 1897 and 1980 — every generation in
the corpus** — with a complete set of verb endings:

> *"**Traíálthaidh** mé é sin a dhéanamh"* — **"I'll try to do that."** The exact frame we need.
> *"chuaigh sé ag **traíáil** leis an dinnéar a ghoid"* — he went trying to steal the dinner
> *"**tabhair traíáil** dhom"* — give me a try
> *"**Traíáil** siad é"* — Ó Curnáin's own gloss: **"they tried him"**

Two syllables. The shortest option on the table. Genuinely, unarguably Connemara.

**I am recommending against it, and here is the honest reasoning.** Ó Curnáin's own index
writes it as *traíáil (**triail**)* — it is the banned word wearing a different spelling, and
adopting it reopens a ruling you closed after learners objected. And it would mean teaching an
English speaker, in their first hour of an Irish course, that the Irish for "try" is *try*.

**But the strongest reason is the one from section 3**: Ó Curnáin tells us *traíáil* is what
the **younger** generation uses **in place of *ag iarraidh***. So this is not "the living word
versus a bookish one". It is **the traditional Connemara word versus its recent English
replacement** — and choosing the traditional one is the same instinct that produced your ban in
the first place. That is a coherent position rather than a squeamish one.

**It is still a judgement, and it is yours to reverse.** If you say the dialect rail wins here
too, it is one seed's work and I will apply it without arguing.

### Two things nobody had found

**`oipinne`** — a native Connemara attempt-noun, in the glossary with a full entry, and nobody
in this project has ever mentioned it:

> *Rinne mé oipinne é a shábháilt* — **I made an attempt to save it**
> *Thug mé oipinne ach níor éirigh liom* — **I made an attempt but I didn't succeed**

This is precisely the construction *iarracht a dhéanamh* was supposed to supply and does not —
natively, in running speech. **I am not recommending it**: every example of this sense comes
from a single speaker, Ó Curnáin's definition carries "sudden" so it may be narrower than a
plain "try", and it is not in Ó Dónaill, so a learner who goes looking will find nothing. But
it is real, it is Connemara, and it should be on the record.

**`síl`** — the index lists it as *"think, attempt"*, so it does carry "try" here. Ó Curnáin
says outright that *traíáil* is the more common vehicle for that sense and marks his own
examples rare. Not a candidate, but worth knowing.

## 5. The collision check — run before recommending, not after

**Against the specific banned word.** *Iarraidh* has nothing to do with *triail* or *traíáil*.
Different root, different sound, no overlap. The ban is untouched and stays.

**Against `iarracht`.** This is the collision that has worried everyone, and my recommendation
**removes it** rather than managing it. *Iarraidh* and *iarracht* are one syllable apart and
were shaping up to be a genuine minimal pair the learner would have to be drilled through.
Under this decision *iarracht* is not in the course at all, so **the pair never exists**. This
is the single biggest thing the decision buys, and it is the thing you were worried about.

**Against "want".** The deliberate one, so let me set it out squarely. The learner will say the
same Irish for *I want* and *I'm trying*:

> **seed 1** — I want to speak Irish with you now → *Tá mé **ag iarraidh** Gaeilge a labhairt leat anois*
> **seed 2** — I'm trying to learn → *Tá mé **ag iarraidh** foghlaim*

**This passes the rule.** One known prompt, one target — always. *I want* has exactly one
answer. *I'm trying* has exactly one answer. They happen to be the same answer, which is
allowed and is what the language does. What is banned is one prompt with two answers, and this
is the opposite of that.

**And it passes the learner test, which is the harder one.** A learner is asked to produce
Irish from English. Shown *"I'm trying to learn"*, they produce *Tá mé ag iarraidh foghlaim* —
and they cannot get it wrong, because there is nothing else to reach for. **The current course
is the one that fails this test**: a learner shown *"try"* today has three answers available
and no way to choose between them.

**The cost, stated fairly.** Some learners will meet seed 2 and feel they have been handed the
same words twice. That is the trust risk you ruled on before. Three things answer it: it is
true; it is printed in Ó Dónaill under one heading; and Ó Curnáin lists **Trying** as a sense of
this very word in this very dialect. A learner who goes and checks finds we are right — which
is exactly what could not be said for *ag triail*. **The presentation should say it out loud
and confidently at seed 2: in Connemara Irish, wanting and trying are the same word.**

I should also be straight that the ambiguity is real and cannot be defined away. Ó Curnáin
glosses *ag iarraidh* as "want" more often than as "try". **A course can honestly say
*ag iarraidh* is Connemara for "trying to". It cannot honestly say it means only that.** In
practice this costs the learner nothing, because they are always producing Irish from English,
never guessing English from Irish.

**Against look / see / watch.** No collision — *iarraidh* is nowhere near *feiceáil* or
*breathnú*. This is exactly where *féach le* would have failed.

**One build note.** *Iarraidh* on its own sits inside *tá mé ag iarraidh*, which the course
already teaches. The course builder permits this — it treats a small piece appearing inside an
already-taught bigger piece as a valid pattern — but it will flag it, and whoever applies this
should expect the flag and not read it as a refusal.

## 6. What actually changes

**The blast radius is tiny, and it is free.** This course has **36 of 668 sentences translated,
47 building blocks, 368 practice phrases, and zero audio clips**. Nothing that changes here can
silence a learner, because there is no sound to lose.

**Seed 8 is the only sentence that has to change.**

> **now:** Tá mé chun **iarracht a dhéanamh** a mhíniú céard atá i gceist agam *(~18 syllables)*
> **becomes:** Tá mé chun **iarraidh** a mhíniú céard atá i gceist agam *(~15 syllables)*

**Seed 2 changes nothing.** *Tá mé ag iarraidh foghlaim* is already right.
**Seed 6 changes nothing.** *Tá mé ag iarraidh cuimhneamh ar fhocal* is already right.

**Seed 7 keeps its Irish and loses its claim to the word "try".**

> **stays as it is:** Tá mé ag iarraidh **mo dhícheall a dhéanamh** inniu

*Dícheall* means **utmost**, not try. *Tá mé ar mo dhícheall* is "I'm doing my best". It takes
no verb after it, so it could never render "try to do X" even if we wanted it to — which is
exactly why it is not competing for the same job. The released standard Irish course renders
this sentence identically, a useful independent vote. **It should be recorded in the course's
word register as an idiom, not as a way of saying "try"**, so nobody building later seeds
reaches for it when they mean "try".

**The building blocks.** One removed — *to try → iarracht a dhéanamh*. One added —
*to try → iarraidh*. The blocks at seeds 1, 2 and 7 are untouched.

**The English glosses, so no prompt is ambiguous:**

| English prompt | Irish | never glossed as |
|---|---|---|
| I want / he wants / I don't want | tá mé ag iarraidh / tá sé ag iarraidh / níl mé ag iarraidh | — |
| I'm trying / he's trying / I'm not trying | tá mé ag iarraidh / tá sé ag iarraidh / níl mé ag iarraidh | — |
| **to try** | **iarraidh** | not "to make an attempt" |
| to try as hard as I can | mo dhícheall a dhéanamh | **not** "to try" |

The rule a builder can hold in their head: **"trying" with an ‑ing is *ag iarraidh*. Bare "to
try" or "tried" is *iarraidh*. Nothing else is ever glossed "try".**

**What this unblocks.** Two sentences have been sitting untranslatable because the old form
could not express them — *"I want to try…"* and *"I'm going to try…"* both produced nonsense
when the only sanctioned form was a progressive. Under one word with a bare form, both work,
and so do all eight of the awkward sentences in section 1. **The 45-seed quarantine and the
whole contrast-drilling plan built around keeping *iarraidh* and *iarracht* apart become
unnecessary — not satisfied, unnecessary.** There is no longer a pair to keep apart.

## 7. Confidence, and what I could not settle

**On the word itself: high.** Ó Curnáin's Connemara glossary lists **Trying** as a sense of
*iarraidh*; he states directly that *ag iarraidh* is the older generation's word for it; Ó
Dónaill files want and try under one heading; and a released Irish course independently reaches
the same answer in 20 of the same 22 sentences. Four independent sources agreeing is as good as
this gets without a speaker.

**On seed 8's exact wording: moderate.** *Chun iarraidh* is attested — the released course uses
it at seed 236 for the same construction — but not in the Connemara recordings specifically. It
is standard Irish a Connemara speaker would accept, rather than something I have heard a
Connemara speaker say.

**Gaps I am not papering over:**

- **No native Connemara speaker has seen this.** Everything here is dictionaries, a 686-page
  corpus and three SSi courses. That is the largest gap and it is unchanged.
- **`traíáil` is a deliberate refusal, not an oversight.** The dialect's living word for "try"
  exists, it is well attested across every generation, and I am recommending against it. Section
  4 makes the case against my own answer as strongly as I can. You may reverse me; it is one seed.
- **Seed 7's helper verb is a separate open question.** Connemara says the word as *deoicheall*
  and the glossary's idiom is *tá mo dhícheall **tugtha** agam* — with *tabhair*, not *déan*.
  There is one recorded instance of *ag déanamh mo mhíle dícheall*, so both exist. **This does
  not affect the "try" decision** and I have not ruled on it, but somebody should.
- **`chun` itself is under separate review** and looks likely to change — the recordings have
  zero instances of *tá mé chun* meaning "going to". That affects seed 8's opening words, not
  its word for "try". **If *chun* changes, seed 8 gets shorter again, which is the direction
  you want.**
- **Something else was editing this course while I read it.** Seed 8's word order changed
  between two of my own queries tonight. Nothing I recommend depends on the order, but whoever
  applies this should re-read seed 8 first rather than trusting the wording quoted here.
- **One quotation in circulation could not be verified.** A sentence previously cited as
  Connemara evidence for *ag iarraidh* meaning "trying" — *"Bhí muid ag iarraidh finiseáil
  stuf"* — could not be found in these four volumes. The three quoted in section 3 were each
  located and checked. The unverified one should stop being repeated.
- **I did not read the 368 practice phrases one by one** for stray uses of the retired form.
  That belongs to the applying job and, at 36 translated sentences, it is small.
