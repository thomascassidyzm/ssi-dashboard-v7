# The Connemara word for "try" — decided

**Course: Connemara Irish for English speakers. Draft. No learner can reach any of it.
No audio exists for this course at all — not one clip — so nothing in this plan costs money.**

---

## The decision, in one line

**The course teaches ONE word for "try": `iarraidh`.**

It is the word the course already teaches at seed 1 for "want". A learner meets it in their
first minute and never has to learn a second word for trying.

| what the learner sees | what they say | syllables |
|---|---|---|
| trying | **ag iarraidh** | 3 |
| to try / tried | **iarraidh** | 2 |

That is the whole system. It is the same shape as every other verb this course already
teaches — *foghlaim* / *ag foghlaim*, *caint* / *ag caint*, *stopadh* / *ag stopadh*. The
learner is not learning a new pattern. They are learning one more word that behaves exactly
like the ones they already have.

**`iarracht a dhéanamh` goes. `mo dhícheall a dhéanamh` stays but stops being a "try" word.
`ag triail` and `traíáil` stay banned.**

## Kai — you were right, but not for the reason you thought

You said *iarracht a dhéanamh* is too long for a word a beginner meets in the first hour and
then uses constantly. **The length is real. The "uses constantly" is not — and that turns out
to be the more useful half.**

Here is what a learner actually meets in the first ten sentences of this course:

| seed | English | Irish now | which "try" word |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | I want to speak Irish with you now | Tá mé **ag iarraidh** Gaeilge a labhairt leat anois | — (this is "want") |
| 2 | I'm trying to learn | Tá mé **ag iarraidh** foghlaim | **ag iarraidh** |
| 6 | I'm trying to remember a word | Tá mé **ag iarraidh** cuimhneamh ar fhocal | **ag iarraidh** |
| 7 | I want to try as hard as I can today | Tá mé ag iarraidh **mo dhícheall a dhéanamh** inniu | **mo dhícheall a dhéanamh** |
| 8 | I'm going to try to explain what I mean | Tá mé chun **iarracht a dhéanamh** a mhíniú céard atá i gceist agam | **iarracht a dhéanamh** |

**Four of the first eight sentences ask for "try". Three different Irish answers.** That is the
defect. The long one is only one of the three, and it appears exactly once in the first hour.

So the fix is not "find a shorter word". The fix is **stop having three words**. And once you
ask which one of the three should be the survivor, the answer stops being a matter of taste:
it is the one that is already there, already short, and already what Connemara says.

**One thing I have to put on the record, because you asked to be corrected rather than
agreed with.** Shortening the *word* barely shortens the *sentence*. Seed 8 is ~18 syllables
today; with my recommendation it is ~15. The estate norm for that sentence is 13–14 (French
*Je vais essayer d'expliquer ce que je veux dire*, Finnish *Mä aion yrittää selittää mitä mä
tarkoitan*). My change closes about half the gap. The rest of the length is in *chun* and in
*céard atá i gceist agam*, not in the word for "try". **If seed 8 still feels long after this,
the remaining problem is a different one and I should not pretend I have solved it.**

## 1. Where "try" appears, and how often

**22 of the 668 English sentences ask for "try".** It first appears at **seed 2** — the second
thing a learner ever says. Then seeds **6, 7, 8**, and then a long gap to seed 50.

After that it recurs at seeds 50, 102, 103, 140, 146, 159, 195, 205, 213, 222, 226, 236, 372,
407, 491, 541, 579 and 638. So it is front-loaded and then steady — it never goes away, but it
is never dense again after the opening.

**The senses, and whether they want the same word.**

Fourteen of the 22 are the plain progressive — *I'm trying to…*, *we're not trying to…*,
*what she was trying to create*. These all want the same thing and there is no argument
about them.

Eight are not progressive, and this is where the course has been stuck:

> **seed 8** — I'm going to try to explain what I mean
> **seed 146** — nothing seems to be working since we tried to fix it
> **seed 236** — I know someone who said that she was going to try to help
> **seed 407** — shouldn't we try to set a good example?
> **seed 491** — I love the way you try to help
> **seed 541** — it's a good idea to try and breathe slowly
> **seed 579** — we've often tried
> **seed 7** — I want to try as hard as I can today

Seed **579** is the sharp one: *"we've often tried"* has nothing after it. There is no
"trying to do" anything. Any solution that only handles *trying to* fails this sentence.

**They do not all want the same form — but they all want the same WORD.** That is the finding
that unlocks this, and section 3 is where it comes from.

## 2. The candidates, and why four of them lose

**`iarracht a dhéanamh`** — correct standard Irish, and Ó Dónaill gives it. But in 686 pages of
recorded Connemara speech it appears **zero** times as a way of saying "try". Five syllables.
Its only real virtue is that it is unmistakable.

**`ag triail`** — already banned, and the ban is right. In this dialect Ó Curnáin records
*triail* as belonging to *triall*, "to journey, to head for". Ó Dónaill's own English for it is
*test, trial, try on, try a case in court*. Learners who read it as "trialling" were reading it
correctly.

**`traíáil`** — this is Connemara's own word, borrowed straight from English "try". Ó Curnáin's
glossary lists it: *traíáil, v. Try*, and *traíáil, f. Attempt, chance at, go*. Two syllables,
the shortest option on the table, and genuinely the dialect's. **But it is the banned word
wearing a different spelling** — the glossary itself writes it as *traíáil (triail)*. Teaching
it would reopen a ruling you closed, to teach a beginner that the Irish for "try" is "try".
**I am not recommending it, and I want to be honest that this is the one place where I am
letting a previous ruling of yours outrank the dialect rail.** If you tell me the dialect rail
wins here too, it is a one-seed change and I will say so plainly rather than argue.

**`féachaint le`** — the standard idiom, three syllables, and de Bhaldraithe gives
*féach lena chríochnú anocht* for "try and finish it tonight". **It loses on collision, which
is the test you set.** The course has 24 sentences coming that need "see" (*feiceáil*) and 8
that need "look". *Féachaint* and *feiceáil* are near-twins to the ear. Buying a two-syllable
saving by putting a near-homophone of "see" into a course that says "see" 24 times is a bad
trade.

**`tabhairt faoi`** — two syllables, and the dictionary gives it. It loses on the *no bare
frames* rule: it is "give" plus "about", two words the learner will already know, recombined
into something they could never guess. Ó Curnáin has it once.

## 3. Why `iarraidh` wins — the evidence

**It is what Connemara actually says.** In Ó Curnáin's four volumes:

| form | hits in recorded Connemara |
|---|---|
| **ag iarraidh** | **173** |
| iarracht (any use at all) | 38, and none of it running speech |
| iarracht a dhéanamh | **0** |
| tabhairt faoi | 1 |

*(These counts are from a Python reader, not grep. The volumes are phonetic transcription in a
custom font and grep calls them binary and returns zero for words that are demonstrably there.
The reader was calibrated first on three known-present words and hit all three exactly.)*

And it is used for **trying**, not just wanting, in the recordings themselves:

> *"Bhí muid ag iarraidh finiseáil stuf"* — **we were trying to finish stuff**
> *"bhí mé ag iarraidh fuarú an súp"* — **I was trying to cool the soup**
> *"Bhídís ag iarraidh fanacht ó shean-chosáin"* — **they used to be trying to keep away from the old paths**

Note the shape of that second one: *ag iarraidh* followed straight by a plain verb, no
scaffolding. That is exactly seed 2's *Tá mé ag iarraidh foghlaim*.

**Ó Dónaill files "want" and "try" as one sense, not two.** Under the verb *iarr*, sense 2 is
headed **"Attempt"** — and inside that single sense he gives both readings:

> *Bhí siad ag iarraidh a bheith ag magadh fúinn* — **they were trying to make fun of us**
> *Níl mé ag iarraidh éirí go fóill* — **I don't want to get up yet**

That is not a workaround we are inventing. It is the language, printed in the standard
dictionary, with want and try under one heading.

**And here is the piece that settles the eight hard sentences.** SSi already has a released
Irish course built on these same 668 English sentences. I looked at what it does with all 22.
**It uses this one word for 20 of them** — and crucially, it uses it in every awkward frame:

> **seed 236** — *I know someone who said that she was going to try to help*
> → *Tá aithne agam ar dhuine a dúirt go raibh sí **chun iarraidh** cabhrú*
>
> **seed 407** — *shouldn't we try to set a good example?*
> → *nach cheart dúinn **iarraidh** dea-shampla a thabhairt?*
>
> **seed 541** — *it's a good idea to try and breathe slowly*
> → *is smaoineamh maith é **iarraidh** análú go mall*
>
> **seed 579** — *we've often tried*
> → *Tá muid tar éis **iarraidh** go minic*
>
> **seed 146** — *nothing seems to be working since we tried to fix it*
> → *Ní cosúil go bhfuil aon rud ag obair ó **d'iarr** muid é a dheisiú*

Seed 579 is the one I said was sharp — *"we've often tried"*, with nothing after it. The bare
word handles it. And seed 236 is the identical construction to our seed 8: **"going to try to"
→ *chun iarraidh***. So the form I am proposing for seed 8 is not something I made up. It is
already in a released Irish course, in the same sentence shape.

**A note on what NOT to copy.** SSi's Ulster Irish course uses *ag iarracht* throughout
(*tá mé ag iarracht smaoineamh*). That is a genuine Ulster form. It is not a Connemara one, and
this course was right to kill it. Its presence in the Ulster course explains where it came from
and is a good reason to keep it out of this one.

## 4. The collision check — I ran this before recommending, not after

**Against the specific banned word.** *Iarraidh* has nothing to do with *triail* or *traíáil*.
Different root, different sound, no overlap. The ban is untouched and stays.

**Against `iarracht`.** This is the collision that has worried everyone, and my recommendation
**removes it** rather than managing it. *Iarraidh* and *iarracht* are one syllable apart and
were shaping up to be a genuine minimal pair the learner would have to be drilled through.
Under this decision *iarracht* is not in the course at all, so the pair never exists. **This is
the single biggest thing the decision buys, and it is the thing you were worried about.**

**Against "want".** This is the deliberate one, so let me set it out squarely. The learner will
say the same Irish for *I want* and *I'm trying*:

> **seed 1** — I want to speak Irish with you now → *Tá mé **ag iarraidh** Gaeilge a labhairt leat anois*
> **seed 2** — I'm trying to learn → *Tá mé **ag iarraidh** foghlaim*

**This passes the rule.** One known prompt, one target — always. *I want* has exactly one
answer. *I'm trying* has exactly one answer. They happen to be the same answer, which is
allowed and is what the language does. What is banned is one prompt with two answers, and this
is the opposite of that.

**And it passes the learner test, which is the harder one.** A learner is asked to produce
Irish from English. Shown *"I'm trying to learn"*, they produce *Tá mé ag iarraidh foghlaim* —
and they cannot get it wrong, because there is nothing else to reach for. **The current
course is the one that fails this test**: a learner shown *"try"* today has three answers
available and no way to choose.

**The cost, stated fairly.** Some learners will meet seed 2 and feel they have been given the
same words twice. That is the trust risk you ruled on before. The mitigation is that it is
true, it is what Ó Dónaill prints, and a learner who goes and checks will find we are right —
which is exactly what could not be said for *ag triail*. The presentation should say it out
loud and confidently at seed 2: *in Irish, wanting and trying are the same word.*

**Against look / see / watch.** No collision — *iarraidh* is nowhere near *feiceáil* or
*breathnaigh*. This is precisely where *féachaint le* would have failed, with 24 "see"
sentences and 8 "look" sentences still to come.

**One build note.** *Iarraidh* on its own sits inside *tá mé ag iarraidh*, which the course
already teaches. The course builder allows this — it treats a small piece appearing inside an
already-taught bigger piece as a valid pattern — but it will flag it, and whoever applies this
should expect the flag and not read it as a refusal.

## 5. What actually changes

**The blast radius is tiny, and it is free.** This course has 36 of 668 sentences translated,
47 building blocks, 368 practice phrases, and **zero audio clips**. Nothing that changes here
can silence a learner, because there is no sound to lose.

**Seed 8 is the only sentence that has to change.**

> **now:** Tá mé chun **iarracht a dhéanamh** a mhíniú céard atá i gceist agam *(~18 syllables)*
> **becomes:** Tá mé chun **iarraidh** a mhíniú céard atá i gceist agam *(~15 syllables)*

**Seed 2 changes nothing.** *Tá mé ag iarraidh foghlaim* is already right.
**Seed 6 changes nothing.** *Tá mé ag iarraidh cuimhneamh ar fhocal* is already right.

**Seed 7 keeps its Irish and loses its claim to the word "try".**

> **stays as it is:** Tá mé ag iarraidh **mo dhícheall a dhéanamh** inniu

This is not a "try" word and should never have been counted as one — it is the ordinary idiom
*to do my best*, and it is good Connemara: Ó Curnáin has *ag déanamh mo mhíle dícheall* from a
recorded speaker. The released standard Irish course renders this exact sentence
identically, which is a useful independent vote. **It should be recorded in the course's word
register as an idiom, not as a way of saying "try"**, so that nobody building later seeds
reaches for it when they mean "try".

**The building blocks.** One is removed — *to try → iarracht a dhéanamh*. One is added —
*to try → iarraidh*. The blocks at seeds 1, 2 and 7 are untouched.

**The English glosses, so no prompt is ambiguous:**

| English prompt | Irish | never glossed as |
|---|---|---|
| I want / he wants / I don't want | tá mé ag iarraidh / tá sé ag iarraidh / níl mé ag iarraidh | — |
| I'm trying / he's trying / I'm not trying | tá mé ag iarraidh / tá sé ag iarraidh / níl mé ag iarraidh | — |
| **to try** | **iarraidh** | not "to make an attempt" |
| to try as hard as I can | mo dhícheall a dhéanamh | **not** "to try" |

The rule a builder can hold in their head: **"trying" with an -ing is *ag iarraidh*. Bare "to
try" or "tried" is *iarraidh*. Nothing else is ever glossed "try".**

**What this unblocks.** Two sentences have been sitting untranslatable because the old form
could not express them — *"I want to try…"* and *"I'm going to try…"* both produced nonsense
when the only sanctioned form was a progressive. Under one word with a bare form, both work,
and so do all eight of the awkward sentences in section 1. **The 45-seed quarantine and the
whole contrast-drilling plan built around keeping *iarraidh* and *iarracht* apart become
unnecessary — not satisfied, unnecessary.** There is no longer a pair to keep apart.

## 6. Confidence, and what I could not settle

**On the word itself: high.** 173 attestations in recorded Connemara against zero for the
form it replaces; Ó Dónaill filing want and try under one heading; and a released Irish course
independently reaching the same answer in 20 of the same 22 sentences. Three independent
sources agreeing is as good as this gets without a speaker.

**On seed 8's exact wording: moderate.** *Chun iarraidh* is attested — the released course uses
it at seed 236 for the same construction — but not in the Connemara recordings specifically.
It is standard Irish that a Connemara speaker would accept, rather than something I have heard
a Connemara speaker say.

**Gaps I am not papering over:**

- **No native Connemara speaker has seen this.** Everything here is dictionaries, a 686-page
  corpus, and three SSi courses. That is the largest gap and it is unchanged.
- **`traíáil` is a real, deliberate refusal, not an oversight.** The dialect's own word for
  "try" exists and I am recommending against it because it is your banned word respelled. You
  may reverse me. It is one seed.
- **`chun` itself is under separate review** and looks likely to change — the recordings have
  zero instances of *tá mé chun* as a way of saying "going to". That affects seed 8's opening
  words, not its word for "try", and it is somebody else's decision. **If *chun* changes, seed
  8 gets shorter again, which is the direction you want.**
- **Something else was editing this course while I read it.** Seed 8's word order changed
  between two of my own queries tonight. Nothing I recommend depends on the order, but whoever
  applies this should re-read seed 8 first rather than trusting the wording I have quoted.
- **I did not check the practice phrases sentence by sentence** for stray uses of the retired
  form; that count is part of the applying job, and with 368 phrases and 36 translated
  sentences it is small.
