# The Irish word for "try" — decided, per dialect

**Three draft courses. No learner can reach any of them. There is no audio on any of them —
not one clip on Connemara, not one on Ulster, none on Kerry — so nothing in this plan costs
money.**

---

## The decision

**All three dialects use the same word — but each in its own shape. That is not a compromise;
it is what each dialect's own evidence says, checked separately.**

| dialect | trying | to try / tried | syllables |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Connemara** | **ag iarraidh** | **iarraidh** | 3 / 2 |
| **Kerry / Munster** | **d'iarraidh** | **iarraidh** | 3 / 2 |
| **Donegal / Ulster** | **not `ag iarracht` — that has to go** | see §5 | — |

The word is *iarraidh* — the verbal noun of *iarr*. In Connemara it takes *ag*; in Kerry it
takes the *d'* form that Kerry actually writes and says. **Same word, two dialect shapes, and
in both it behaves like every other verb the courses already teach** — *foghlaim* /
*ag foghlaim*, *caint* / *ag caint*. Nobody learns a new pattern.

**`iarracht a dhéanamh` goes from Connemara. `ag iarracht` goes from Ulster and should never
have been there. `mo dhícheall a dhéanamh` stays but stops counting as a "try" word.
`ag triail` and `traíáil` stay banned.**

## Kai — on "too long", you were right, but not for the reason you thought

You said *iarracht a dhéanamh* is too long for a word a beginner meets in the first hour and
then uses constantly. **The length is real. The "uses constantly" is not** — and that is the
more useful half.

Here is what a Connemara learner meets in the first ten sentences:

| seed | English | Irish | which "try" word |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | I want to speak Irish with you now | Tá mé **ag iarraidh** Gaeilge a labhairt leat anois | — (this is "want") |
| 2 | I'm trying to learn | Tá mé **ag iarraidh** foghlaim | **ag iarraidh** |
| 6 | I'm trying to remember a word | Tá mé **ag iarraidh** cuimhneamh ar fhocal | **ag iarraidh** |
| 7 | I want to try as hard as I can today | Tá mé ag iarraidh **mo dhícheall a dhéanamh** inniu | **mo dhícheall a dhéanamh** |
| 8 | I'm going to try to explain what I mean | Tá mé chun **iarracht a dhéanamh** a mhíniú céard atá i gceist agam | **iarracht a dhéanamh** |

**Four of the first eight sentences ask for "try". Three different Irish answers.** That is the
defect. The long one is one of the three and appears **once** in the first hour.

So the fix is not a shorter word. It is **having one word instead of three**.

**And the correction you asked for.** Shortening the word barely shortens the sentence. Seed 8
is ~18 syllables; with my recommendation ~15. The estate norm for that sentence is 13–14
(French *Je vais essayer d'expliquer ce que je veux dire*). **I close about half the gap. The
rest sits in *chun* and *céard atá i gceist agam*, not in the word for "try", and I am not going
to pretend I have solved it.**

## 2. How the evidence was gathered, and why the zeros can be trusted

These corpora cannot be searched normally. The Connemara volumes are phonetic transcription in
a custom font; ordinary text search calls them unreadable and **returns zero for words that are
plainly on the page**. A false zero reads as "this dialect does not have this word" and has come
close to becoming a ruling more than once tonight.

Every count below was calibrated first against words of known frequency, and every run passed.
**I got caught by a false zero myself and it is worth showing**, because it nearly cost Kerry
the right answer:

> I first measured `ag iarraidh` in Kerry and got **zero** in native Blasket prose. That looked
> like a clean dialect finding. It was a spelling artefact — **Munster writes the same word
> `d'iarraidh`**, and it is there **41 times**.

That is why §4 exists at all.

## 3. Connemara — `ag iarraidh`

**Ó Curnáin's Connemara glossary lists "Trying" as a meaning of this exact word:**

> **iarraidh**, f. 1. Request, plea… **2. VN, Trying.** … 3. Blow, stroke.

His index says the same: *"iarraidh, f, request, **trying**, turn, blow."*

**And the single most useful line in the corpus** — his note on the dialect's other "try" word:

> *"(Used by younger generation for **ag iarraidh** of older generation)"*

That is a lexicographer stating flatly that **`ag iarraidh` is how the older generation of
Connemara said "try"**. It is not a workaround. It is the traditional Connemara word.

**In recorded speech**, with his own English attached:

> *"ag iarraidh a bheith ag obair dhen bhuíochas"* — **"trying to work despite it all"**
>
> *"chuir sé amach a bhutún ag iarraidh rud a dhéanamh is chinn sé air"* — he tried his utmost
> and **failed** at it. You cannot fail at *wanting* something; this can only be "try".
>
> *"bhí mé ag iarraidh fuarú an súp"* — **I was trying to cool the soup**

That last one is *ag iarraidh* followed straight by a plain verb — exactly seed 2's *Tá mé ag
iarraidh foghlaim*.

| form | in recorded Connemara |
|---|---|
| **ag iarraidh** | **173** |
| iarracht, as a free word in running speech | **0** |
| **iarracht a dhéanamh** | **0** — categorical |
| tabhairt faoi, in the "attempt" sense | **0** |

**The bare form handles the awkward eight.** SSi's released Irish course, built on these same
668 sentences, uses this one word for **20 of the 22**:

> **seed 579** — *we've often tried* → *Tá muid tar éis **iarraidh** go minic*
> **seed 407** — *shouldn't we try to set a good example?* → *nach cheart dúinn **iarraidh** dea-shampla a thabhairt?*
> **seed 236** — *…she was going to try to help* → *…go raibh sí **chun iarraidh** cabhrú*
> **seed 146** — *…since we tried to fix it* → *…ó **d'iarr** muid é a dheisiú*

Seed 579 was the one with nothing after it. The bare word does it.

### A datum that argues against your `ag triail` ban — reported because it exists

A measurement came back claiming the legacy Irish translation memory is **62% `ag triail`**
across the try-sentence matches, and calling that historical evidence against your ban. **That
is true of that narrow slice and misleading about the corpus.** I went and counted the whole
33MB file myself:

| form, across the entire legacy Irish translation memory | count |
|---|---|
| **ag iarraidh** | **5,790** |
| ag triail | 272 |
| iarracht (all uses) | 271 |
| iarracht a dhéanamh | 91 |

**`ag iarraidh` outnumbers `ag triail` by more than twenty to one.** So the honest reading is
two-sided: among the forms that mean *only* "try", `ag triail` does beat `iarracht a dhéanamh`
about three to one — **your ban did cost something, and that is worth knowing** — but the form
this document recommends dwarfs both by an order of magnitude. **It does not change the ruling**,
and the Connemara-specific reason stands on its own: in this dialect Ó Curnáin's *triail* is the
verb of *triall*, "journey, travel", and every occurrence in the corpus is that word.

**And the strongest confirmation arrived while I was writing this.** The Connemara build
translated 14 more "try" sentences tonight, with no knowledge of this ruling, and **chose
*ag iarraidh* for 13 of them.** Including this one, which now carries both senses in a single
sentence, in the same words:

> **seed 222** — *he's trying to tell me what he wants*
> → ***Tá sé ag iarraidh inseacht dom céard atá sé ag iarraidh***

That sentence is the whole argument in one line, and the course produced it by itself.

## 4. Kerry / Munster — `d'iarraidh`, and it is *cleaner* here than in Connemara

**Kerry uses the same word, in its own shape.** Native Blasket prose has it **41 times**, and
the sense is unmistakably *trying*:

> *"d'iarraidh iad a chur isteach ar an dtráigh"* — **trying to drive them in onto the strand**
> *"ag rámhaidheacht d'iarraidh teacht suas léi"* — **rowing, trying to catch up with her**
> *"d'iarraidh fanúint anáirde air"* — **trying to stay up on it**
> *"d'iarraidh a bheith le h-ais liom"* — **trying to be alongside me**

**And a graded beginners' course in West Munster Irish teaches it in exactly these words.** Its
vocabulary list:

> *iarraidh — asking, to ask; **trying, to try***

and its glossary spells out the dialect shape: *"**a d'iarraidh (= ag iarraidh)** asking"*.

**Here is the part that makes Kerry the easiest of the three.** Kerry does **not** use this word
for "want" — Kerry says *tá … uaim*, which appears 37 times in the same Blasket prose and 14
times in the beginners' course. **So in Munster there is no want/try overlap at all.** The one
real cost of this decision in Connemara simply does not arise in Kerry.

*Iarracht* exists in Munster too, in the same minor role as in Connemara — a discrete attempt,
and with **tabhair**, not *déan*: *"thugas **iarracht ar** mheon na ndaoine … a chur síos"* ("I
made an attempt to set down the disposition of the people"). That is the frame for the rare
non-progressive cases if one is ever wanted.

**`féach le` is not the Munster answer, and the number that suggested it was a trap.** Ó Sé's
grammar appears to contain 17 instances. **All 17 are his own cross-reference idiom** —
*"Féach leis 819–821"*, meaning "see also". His book is a grammar written in Irish, so his prose
is not dialect evidence. **The real count of *féach le* meaning "try to" in Munster is zero.**

**One thing I have not decided, because it is not mine.** Whether the Kerry course writes
*d'iarraidh* or the standard *ag iarraidh* is an orthography question, and these courses have a
standing rail to use standard spelling so the voices pronounce it correctly. **The word is
settled; the spelling belongs with that rail.**

## 5. Donegal / Ulster — the negative is certain, the positive is a gap

**`ag iarracht` is a coinage. It should come out of the Ulster course.** I am confident of this,
and here is the whole basis:

- **Zero** across all four volumes of the Connemara corpus, 686 pages, calibrated.
- **Zero** across every Munster corpus on this machine — Ó Sé, the Blasket prose, the beginners'
  course — calibrated.
- **Ó Dónaill lists *iarracht* as a noun only — I checked the entry myself rather than taking
  it second-hand.** It is a feminine noun; every frame he gives is a light-verb frame —
  *iarracht a dhéanamh*, *iarracht a thabhairt ar*, *iarracht a shárú* — and **nowhere in the
  entry does *ag iarracht* appear before a verb**.
- **The same dictionary puts "attempt" on the other word.** *Iarraidh* is listed as **the verbal
  noun of *iarr***, and its sense 3 is **"Attempt"**, with the example *"An rud a bhí sé **ag
  iarraidh** a dhéanamh — what he was trying to do."* So the dictionary that refuses *ag
  iarracht* hands the same job to *ag iarraidh*, in those words.
- **The grammar forbids it.** *Ag* + a plain noun does not make a progressive in Irish; that
  needs a verbal noun. *Iarracht* has no verbal-noun function. The verbal noun of *iarr* is
  *iarraidh* — which is precisely the word this whole document is about.
- It entered the course on a ruling **Kai never made**, and the Donegal translators objected on
  these grounds at the time. **They were right.**

**And your googling argument does not rescue it.** That reasoning protects real dialect forms,
which have discussion attached for a learner to find. A learner who searches *ag iarracht* finds
nothing, because there is nothing — which is the opposite of the reassurance the argument was
designed to give.

**What Donegal should use instead: I do not yet know, and I am not going to guess.** Donegal is
the one dialect with no searchable corpus on this machine — the single monograph available
transcribes speech phonetically and cannot be searched for Irish words. A worker (**#592**) is
out now on dictionaries and on the hunt for real Donegal text. **Until it reports, the honest
position is: the wrong answer is identified and removable; the right answer is unevidenced.**

**My expectation, flagged as expectation and not as evidence:** *iarraidh* is likely the Ulster
answer too, since it is the verbal noun of the ordinary verb and is not dialect-specific — and
the translators' own proposal, *ag déanamh iarrachta*, is at least grammatical where *ag
iarracht* is not. **Do not build on that paragraph.** It is a hunch, and it is labelled as one.

## 6. The collision check, per dialect

**Against the banned word.** *Iarraidh* has nothing to do with *triail* or *traíáil* — different
root, different sound, no overlap, in any dialect. The ban is untouched.

**Against `iarracht`.** In Connemara this was the collision everyone feared: *iarraidh* and
*iarracht* are one syllable apart and were shaping up to be a minimal pair needing deliberate
drilling. **This decision removes it rather than managing it** — *iarracht* leaves the course,
so the pair never exists. That is the single biggest thing the decision buys, and it is the
thing you asked about.

**Against "want".** In **Kerry, no collision at all** — want is *tá … uaim*. In **Connemara it
is deliberate**, so let me put it squarely:

> **seed 1** — I want to speak Irish with you now → *Tá mé **ag iarraidh** Gaeilge a labhairt leat anois*
> **seed 2** — I'm trying to learn → *Tá mé **ag iarraidh** foghlaim*

**This passes the rule.** One known prompt, one target — always. *I want* has exactly one
answer; *I'm trying* has exactly one answer. They are the same answer, which is allowed and is
what the language does. What is banned is one prompt with **two** answers — the opposite.

**It passes the learner test, which is harder.** A learner produces Irish from English. Shown
*"I'm trying to learn"* they produce *Tá mé ag iarraidh foghlaim* and cannot get it wrong,
because there is nothing else to reach for. **The current course is the one that fails**: shown
*"try"* today, a learner has three answers and no way to choose.

**The cost, fairly.** Some learners will feel they were handed the same words twice. That is
the trust risk you ruled on before. Three things answer it: it is true; Ó Dónaill files want and
try under one heading; and Ó Curnáin lists **Trying** as a sense of this word in this dialect. A
learner who checks finds we are right — which could never be said of *ag triail*. **The
presentation should say it out loud at seed 2: in Connemara, wanting and trying are the same
word.** I should also be straight that Ó Curnáin glosses it "want" more often than "try". **A
course can honestly say *ag iarraidh* is Connemara for "trying to". It cannot say it means only
that** — and in practice that costs the learner nothing, because they always produce Irish from
English, never the reverse.

**Against look / see / watch.** No collision — *iarraidh* is nowhere near *feiceáil* or
*breathnú*. This is exactly where *féach le* would have failed, with 24 "see" sentences coming.

**One build note.** *Iarraidh* alone sits inside *tá mé ag iarraidh*, already taught. The builder
permits this — a small piece inside an already-taught bigger one is a valid pattern — but it
will flag it, and whoever applies this should expect the flag and not read it as a refusal.

## 7. What changes, and what it costs

**Nothing costs money. All three courses have zero audio rows — I checked each one.**

### Connemara — 3 sentences, 1 building block, 13 practice phrases

The build has advanced to 300 translated sentences and chose *ag iarraidh* on its own for
almost everything, so **only three sentences still carry the retired form**:

> **seed 8** — Tá mé chun **iarracht a dhéanamh** a mhíniú céard atá i gceist agam
> → *Tá mé chun **iarraidh** a mhíniú céard atá i gceist agam* *(~18 → ~15 syllables)*
>
> **seed 146** — …ó **rinne muid iarracht** é a dheisiú → …ó **d'iarr muid** é a dheisiú
>
> **seed 236** — …go raibh sí **ag goil a dhéanamh iarracht** cabhrú → the same construction
> with *iarraidh*. **Handle this one with care** — the course has moved "going to" to *ag goil
> a*, and *ag goil a iarraidh* could be misread as "going to ask". It may want rephrasing rather
> than substitution, and I am flagging it rather than pretending it is mechanical.

Behind those: **one building block** (*to try → iarracht a dhéanamh*, replaced by *to try →
iarraidh*) and **13 practice phrases** — 12 under seed 8, one under seed 25 (*"are you going to
try"*). **All 695 phrases were counted, not sampled. Nothing else in the course carries it.**

Seeds 2, 6 and 7 change nothing.

**Seed 7 keeps its Irish and loses its claim to the word "try".** *Dícheall* means **utmost**,
not try — *tá mé ar mo dhícheall* is "I'm doing my best" — and it takes no verb after it, so it
could never render "try to do X" even if we wanted. It is good Connemara: Ó Curnáin has *ag
déanamh mo mhíle dícheall* from a recorded speaker. **Record it in the word register as an
idiom, not as a way of saying "try"**, so nobody building later seeds reaches for it.

### Ulster — 22 sentences, no blocks, no phrases, no audio

The course is fully translated but **not yet decomposed**, so the coined form sits in **22 seed
translations and nowhere else** — zero building blocks, zero practice phrases, zero clips.
**This is the cheapest possible moment to fix it**, and it gets dearer the moment that course is
decomposed.

### Kerry — nothing built yet

**Zero of 668 translated.** The decision costs nothing and simply lands in the register before
the build starts. This is the ideal case.

### The glosses, so no prompt is ambiguous

| English prompt | Connemara | Kerry |
|---|---|---|
| I want / he wants / I don't want | tá mé ag iarraidh … | tá … uaim |
| I'm trying / he's trying / I'm not trying | tá mé ag iarraidh … | táim d'iarraidh … |
| **to try / tried** | **iarraidh** | **iarraidh** |
| to try as hard as I can | mo dhícheall a dhéanamh | *(not yet built)* |

The rule a builder can hold in their head: **"trying" with an ‑ing takes the *ag*/*d'* form.
Bare "to try" or "tried" is *iarraidh*. Nothing else is ever glossed "try".**

**What this unblocks.** Two Connemara sentences have been untranslatable because a
progressive-only form could not express *"I want to try…"* or *"I'm going to try…"*. Both work
now. **And the 45-seed quarantine and the whole *iarraidh*/*iarracht* contrast-drilling plan
become unnecessary — not satisfied, unnecessary.** There is no pair left to keep apart.

## 8. Confidence, and the gaps

**Connemara: high.** Ó Curnáin's own glossary lists **Trying** as a sense of the word; he states
directly that *ag iarraidh* is the older generation's form; Ó Dónaill files want and try under
one heading; a released Irish course reaches the same answer in 20 of the same 22 sentences; and
the live build independently chose it for 13 of 14 sentences tonight.

**Kerry: high.** 41 attestations in native Blasket prose in the trying sense, plus a graded
Munster beginners' course glossing the word as *"trying, to try"* in its own vocabulary list.
Stronger than I expected to be able to say.

**Ulster, negative: high.** *ag iarracht* is unattested in two calibrated corpora, absent from
the dictionary, grammatically impossible, and entered on a ruling that was never made.

**Ulster, positive: I do not have it.** That is the headline gap.

**The rest, stated plainly:**

- **No native speaker of any of the three dialects has seen this.** Everything is dictionaries,
  corpora and existing courses. Largest gap, unchanged.
- **`traíáil` is a deliberate refusal, not an oversight.** Connemara's own living word for "try"
  is well attested across every generation — *"Traíálthaidh mé é sin a dhéanamh"* — and I am
  recommending against it because Ó Curnáin's index writes it *traíáil (**triail**)*: it is your
  banned word respelled, and it is the *younger* generation's replacement for *ag iarraidh*. So
  the choice is traditional-versus-English-borrowing, not living-versus-bookish. **You may
  reverse me; it is one seed.**
- **I found `oipinne`**, a native Connemara attempt-noun nobody here had named — *"Thug mé
  oipinne ach níor éirigh liom"*. One speaker only, so not recommended, but on the record.
- **Seed 236 is the one substitution I would not do mechanically** (above).
- **Seed 7's helper verb is a separate open question** — Connemara says *deoicheall*, and the
  glossary idiom is *tá mo dhícheall **tugtha** agam*, with *tabhair* not *déan*. It does not
  affect this decision.
- **`chun` is under separate review**; zero instances of *tá mé chun* meaning "going to" in the
  recordings. If it changes, seed 8 gets shorter still — your direction.
- **The Connemara course is being edited live.** It went from 36 to 300 translated sentences
  while I worked, and seed 8's word order changed between two of my own queries. **Whoever
  applies this must re-measure first rather than trusting my counts**, which were true when
  taken.
- **One quotation in circulation could not be verified** — *"Bhí muid ag iarraidh finiseáil
  stuf"*, previously cited as Connemara evidence, is not in these four volumes. The ones quoted
  here were each located and checked. That one should stop being repeated.
