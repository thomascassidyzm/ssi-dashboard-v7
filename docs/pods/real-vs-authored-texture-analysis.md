# Real vs Authored — a texture analysis of pod-0.5 against pod-1

*2026-07-16. Discussion instrument for the founder/co-founder conversation on pod-1's
flesh. The founder's verdict on the pod-1 move canon: "not right… something off…
probably because it's not actually the sorts of things humans would say." This document
measures what that instinct is detecting, names the failure mode, and lays the sourcing
fork. It is NOT rewritten content and contains no recommendation.*

**Texts compared.** `pod-1-english-canonical.md` — 16 scenes, 236 turns, 2,451 words,
model-authored to the founders' 16-move skeleton. `pod05-aran-raw-2026-07-16.txt` — 17
turns, 1,011 words, written by Aran from 75 hours of real conversations. All counts
below are surface counts on those two files, normalised per 1,000 words where sizes
differ; marker counts include some conjunctive uses ("so", "right"), so treat the
*shape* of each distribution as the finding, not the third decimal place.

---

## 1. The forensics — where the two texts part company as language

### 1.1 Turn economy: ping-pong vs floor-holding

| | pod-1 (authored) | pod-0.5 (Aran) |
|---|---|---|
| Turns | 236 | 17 |
| Words per turn — mean | 10.4 | 59.5 |
| Words per turn — SD | 3.6 | 50.2 |
| Coefficient of variation | **0.34** | **0.84** |
| Shortest / longest turn | 2 / 27 words | 7 / 183 words |
| Sentences per turn | 2.0 | 4.5 |

Pod-1 is metronomic. Across 236 turns nobody ever holds the floor for more than 27
words; the modal turn is 9–13 words; speakers alternate strictly, one thought per
serve. It is table tennis with no rallies.

Aran's dialogue has a 26× spread between its shortest and longest turn. The Friend
asks a seven-word question — "Do you speak a lot of languages?" — and the learner
answers with 152 words. Then the Friend fires four questions in one 16-word burst.
Real conversation is wildly asymmetric in who has the floor and for how long; the
asymmetry IS the conversation (one person is practising, one is hosting). Pod-1's
uniformity means no turn ever costs more than one breath — and no turn is ever an
event.

### 1.2 Discourse markers: an idiolect vs an inventory

The naive prediction — real speech has more "well"s and "anyway"s — is false. Pod-1 is
*saturated* with discourse markers. The difference is distributional:

| per 1,000 words | pod-1 | pod-0.5 |
|---|---|---|
| really | 6.5 | 9.9 |
| so | 4.1 | 7.9 |
| well | 2.4 | 6.9 |
| oh | 5.7 | 1.0 |
| honestly | 3.7 | **0** |
| actually | 2.9 | **0** |
| anyway | 2.0 | **0** |
| right | 4.1 | **0** |
| exactly | 2.4 | **0** |
| you know | 2.9 | **0** |
| by the way / to be honest / of course… | 0.8–2.0 each | ~0 |

Aran uses a **narrow personal palette, heavily**: "really" ten times, "well" seven,
"I think" three, and almost nothing else. That is what an idiolect looks like — real
speakers repeat their own tics, because repetition costs a speaker nothing.

Pod-1 spreads **fifteen-plus marker types evenly** across the text, each at 2–6 per
thousand, no type dominant, every scene getting its ration of "honestly", "actually",
"oh". That is what an *inventory* looks like — an author avoiding repetition because
repetition reads as bad writing. And it is shared: pod-1 has 27 named characters and
they all draw from the same inventory in the same proportions. Swap any of Grace's
lines with Laura's or Priya's and nothing breaks. Aran's two voices can't be swapped —
one asks short and hosts, one monologues and apologises for it.

Placement differs too. Pod-1's markers are turn-initial and turn-final ornaments
("Honestly? It wasn't really my thing." / "Fair enough."). Aran's sit at points of
real cognitive load, mid-struggle:

> "That's a good question. The truth is, I'm not sure. It's not easy to think of
> something to ask! So, let me think. How about your family? Yes, tell me about your
> family."

Five sentences to produce one question — a live recording of thinking. Pod-1 never
spends five sentences getting to a question, because its author already knew the
question.

### 1.3 How questions get answered

Pod-1 answers are complete, on-topic, and arrive in the very next turn, usually with a
bonus detail and a return question:

> Amy: "How was your week, anyway?"
> Joe: "Busy, but good. We finally finished the kitchen… so we can stop eating in the
> front room."

96 questions in pod-1; nearly every one gets its second pair-part serviced immediately
and fully. Grice would weep with joy.

Aran's answers are **partial, hedged, and self-revising**:

> "Do you understand what I'm saying at the moment?"
> "I understand a lot of it, I think. Maybe I understand most of it, when you speak
> slowly. If I don't know how to answer, I'll ask you to say it again more slowly."

The claim is made, weakened, re-scoped, and then side-stepped into a contingency plan.
Or the answer opens with an apology and delivers vagueness:

> "I'm not sure I know enough words to explain this very well, but I've just read a
> book about a complicated murder mystery, and I've just seen a film about… a young
> man discovers something unusual in the countryside."

Asked *what was interesting about it*, the answer is "I kept wanting to know what was
going to happen next" — which is a description of every story ever. Real memory under
pressure is approximate. Pod-1's memory is photographic: "Forty-two, Harbour Road."
"Saturday the twenty-third." "Bennett! That's it — Bennett." "A chihuahua, in a little
coat."

And Aran **over-answers**. "Where do you come from, and why are you learning?" yields
116 words that also cover five years of residence history, a friend's language,
politeness doctrine, the family's holiday nerves, and restaurant-ordering duties
nobody asked about. Real answers leak surplus biography; authored answers deliver
exactly the requested payload.

### 1.4 Information asymmetry: real questions vs fed setups

In Aran's dialogue the Friend genuinely knows nothing about the learner, so every
question is a real question — including the machine-gun burst that stacks four at
once, two of them the same question:

> "Are you married? Do you have children? What do you do for work? What's your job?"
> "So many questions! You'll need to slow down to give me time to think."

Overload happens, and gets called out. Nobody in pod-1 ever asks a redundant question
or gets told off for pace, because every pod-1 speaker is the other's **news-midwife**
— they arrive pre-briefed on exactly what setup to feed:

> Paul: "And how did it go on Friday, in the end? The interview."
> Grace: "I got it! I start in two weeks."

Paul carries Grace's interview calendar in his head so that her news can be born on
cue. Katie's "And you? You said you had news too" tees up Ryan's bad-news segment with
the same midwifery. Real interlocutors don't know what you need to be asked; they ask
the wrong thing, or too much, or nothing, and your news has to fight its way in.

### 1.5 Specificity texture: worldview leaks vs stage props

Both texts contain specifics, but they are different species.

Pod-1's specifics are **props** — proper nouns and numbers manufactured to be drilled:
Sara from the old office, Leeds, Maya's party, Harbour Road, three-fifty for a small
coffee, twenty-five minutes at the bus stop, the chihuahua. Each exists to service the
scene's move ("Could you say that again, a bit slower? I wrote fourteen") and none
reveals a person.

Aran's specifics are **worldview leaks** — evaluative, self-exposing, and unprompted:

> "But mostly I'm like everyone else, I have to do too much work and I don't have
> enough time off, or enough money to do all the things that I'd enjoy. Maybe
> capitalism isn't the best way to organise societies."

> "I prefer to be cold, because at least you can put on an extra sweater or a coat or
> something."

> "But I'm very lazy, so I always like to find the easiest way to learn a new
> language. … I get bored very quickly."

> "I love them, of course, even when they spend all my money."

Nobody asked for a position on economic systems in a chat about hobbies. That is
precisely the point: real people cannot describe their week without accidentally
disclosing their politics, their laziness, their thermostat preferences. Pod-1's
characters disclose nothing that the scene doesn't require; their only self-criticism
is charming and pre-redeemed ("it was completely my fault" — setting up "the wrong bus
won").

### 1.6 Topic drift: associative slide vs licensed pivots

Pod-1's topics change only at **signposted junctions**: "Oh — before I forget." /
"Actually… while you're here." / "Enough about me, though —" / "Are you still running,
by the way?" Every transition files a flight plan.

Aran drifts **inside turns, associatively, without permission**: interests → no time
since the children → reading needs a quiet house → travel is expensive → too much work
→ not enough money → capitalism. Or: our city → moving out → husband's commute → the
children need trees → city heat → "I really don't like it when it's hot" → the sweater
theory of temperature. The chain is glued by "although / because / but / so", each
clause suggested by the last, and the turn ends somewhere its opening never predicted.
Real topic movement is a slide, not a junction.

### 1.7 Syntax: accretion vs architecture

Aran's sentences accrete — paratactic run-ons that add clauses while the thought is
still forming, with **redundant self-restatement** left in:

> "I work in education - I'm not a teacher any more, although I used to be, but now I
> write content for courses."

"Not any more" and "used to be" say the same thing twice; real speech is redundant
because speech is cheaper than planning. Pod-1 never restates: every sentence adds
exactly one new piece of information, and many are *balanced* — architected antitheses
no jaw produces at speed:

> "You're not asking, I'm offering."
> "Funny how the mistakes make the best stories."
> "See — the rain brings out the best in you."
> "Funny old week — the best and worst news in one day."

That is epigram density of roughly one per scene. Humans manage about one per dinner
party, and feel pleased with themselves afterwards.

The disfluency signature confirms it: pod-1 carries **40 em-dashes** — scripted
interruptions, performed hesitation ("Wait — say that again. An hour, on the first
day?") — disfluency as a *prop*, typographically applied. Aran has one dash in the
whole text; his disfluency is structural (restarts, re-scoping, "let me think"), not
punctuational.

### 1.8 Affect: enthusiasm economy

Pod-1: 21 exclamation marks and a steady diet of superlative assessments — "That's the
best thing I've heard all week", "You've made my week, Ben", "That's brilliant",
"That's amazing, Katie". Everyone is delighted with everyone, at volume, in every
scene. Aran: 3 exclamation marks in 1,011 words, and his strongest assessment is
"That's always really interesting." Real warmth is mostly mild; authored warmth is
graded for the back row.

### 1.9 Scene shape: buttons vs running out of road

All 16 pod-1 scenes end on a **button** — a callback, a mini-joke, a bow: "Deal. It's
so good to see you, Paul." / "Bennett, and a small dog in a coat." / "An experience.
Perfect. That's exactly what I'll say." Sitcom scene grammar: arc opened, complicated,
resolved, tied. Aran's conversation just stops where the energy runs out — the final
line is "I like books or films with interesting characters in them", which resolves
nothing and calls back to nothing. Two turns earlier the Friend had already flagged
fatigue: "I'm not finding it easy to think of any more questions!" Real conversations
have visible battery levels; pod-1's speakers have infinite conversational stamina.

### 1.10 Menu structures: a conversation you'll have vs conversations you'll watch

Aran writes side-by-side alternatives inside one turn — "Yes, I'm married. No, I'm not
married." / "We don't have children. We've got three children, two girls and a boy." —
a structure no fictional scene could contain, because a *character* can't be both. It
is only possible because pod-0.5's speaker is **the learner themselves**: the dialogue
offers the answer-space, not a person's answers, and the founder's menu-lines ruling
(all variants shipped, all drilled) leans directly into that. Pod-1 instead invents 27
fixed people with fixed facts. The deep difference: pod-0.5 is a conversation the
learner will *have*; pod-1 is conversations the learner will *overhear*. Ownership vs
observation is a texture difference all of its own — the "I" of pod-0.5 has an
off-balance, effortful, first-person quality that no third-person ensemble can supply.

## 2. The failure mode, named: maximal typicality

Pod-1 is not badly written. Line by line it is competent — arguably slick. The defect
is that **every line is the most probable line**: the modal realisation of its
conversational move, drawn from the centre of the distribution of what dialogue looks
like. The interview is landed, the celebration is proposed, the disagreement resolves
to "agree to disagree" — which is not just a typical outcome but the *named* typical
outcome. Adjacency pairs always complete, repairs always succeed, references always
resolve, arcs always close. It is the grammar of conversation with a perfect score.

Real speech is never at the mode, because it is sampled from **one person's**
conditional distribution — always off-centre: a lopsided marker palette, hobby-horses
(capitalism, sweaters), unbalanced floor time, questions that misfire, threads left
dangling. Aran's text is idiosyncratic in a dozen measurable ways at once, and the
idiosyncrasies are *correlated* — they all belong to the same two people.

Typicality is invisible in one line and lethal at length. One modal turn reads fine;
236 consecutive modal turns produce a texture with no grain, and the reader's ear —
without being able to say why — registers that nothing ever snags. That is the
founder's "something off": not an error anywhere, which is itself the error. Perfect
smoothness is the uncanny valley of dialogue.

Two connections to the founders' own doctrine, both earned by the data:

- **Agents converge; aliveness is human.** The marker table in §1.2 is convergence made
  visible: 27 authored characters sharing one evenly-spread inventory versus one real
  author's peaked, repetitive idiolect. A model (or a careful human author writing
  "good dialogue") regresses every voice to the ensemble mean. Divergence — the
  off-centre sample — is exactly what a lived source supplies for free and an authored
  process has to fake.
- **Least action — but note whose.** Authored dialogue minimises the *listener's*
  action: everything is complete, resolved, effortless to follow. Real speech
  minimises the *speaker's* action: redundancy, vagueness, drift, and recycled tics
  are all production-side energy savings, with the tidying-up cost pushed onto the
  listener. The gradient points the opposite way. This matters pedagogically: learners
  are training to converse with humans, whose speech is speaker-optimised. A diet of
  purely listener-optimised dialogue trains comprehension of a language nobody
  actually speaks.

**One structural honesty, before blaming the craft.** The pod-1 frame ruling asks for
one move drilled six-to-ten times inside one continuous natural conversation. That
drill density is itself a typicality engine: ten clean instances of "tell me about it"
in fifteen turns forces each instance towards the canonical form, and no real
five-minute conversation repeats one move at that density. Some of pod-1's weirdness
is the constraint, not the author — which means the sourcing options in §4 all inherit
this tension, and it deserves its own minute in the discussion: does the drill density
bend, or must the flesh absorb it?

**And one fairness note.** Pod-1's best moments are the ones that behave like Aran:
Amy's "Honestly… I don't know yet. Ask me next week" (a deferred answer), Joe's "Don't
ask." (a refused one), Owen's "Now you're making it worse." (uncooperative,
character-revealing). The canon is not uniformly dead — it flickers wherever a line
escapes the mode. Those flickers are what the rubric below is built to detect and
demand.

## 3. The Realness Rubric

Twelve checkable properties that separate Aran-grade dialogue from authored dialogue.
Each is stated as a check a QA pass can run on any future pod content, source-blind,
with a pod-0.5 pass and a pod-1 fail as calibration. Checks R1 and R2 are
**distributional** — they cannot be satisfied by sprinkling texture-words onto an
authored draft, which is deliberate: they keep any hybrid process honest.

| # | Property | The check | pod-0.5 passes | pod-1 fails |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **R1** | **Turn-length variance** | Words-per-turn CV ≥ ~0.6; at least one genuine floor-holding turn (≥4× the median) and one minimal turn. | CV 0.84; 7-word question against a 183-word answer. | CV 0.34; longest of 236 turns is 27 words — nobody ever holds the floor. |
| **R2** | **Marker idiolect, not inventory** | Few marker types, heavily reused, per speaker; peaked distribution. Fail: a wide palette spread evenly, shared across all characters. | "really" ×10, "well" ×7; zero "actually / anyway / honestly". | 15+ types at 2–6 per 1,000 each; every character draws the same ration. |
| **R3** | **Redundant self-restatement** | At least occasional saying-the-same-thing-twice left in; speech is cheaper than planning. | "I'm not a teacher any more, although I used to be, but now I write content." | Every sentence adds exactly one new fact; zero restatement in 236 turns. |
| **R4** | **Imperfect answers** | A meaningful share of questions answered partially, hedged, deferred, or obliquely — not serviced completely in the next turn. | "I understand a lot of it, I think. Maybe I understand most of it, when you speak slowly." | "How was your week?" → "Busy, but good. We finally finished the kitchen…" — complete, on-topic, plus bonus detail. |
| **R5** | **Question overload happens** | Somewhere, questions stack, repeat, or misfire — and may get called out. | "Are you married? Do you have children? What do you do for work? What's your job?" → "So many questions!" | One well-formed question per turn, each individually serviced; nobody ever mis-asks. |
| **R6** | **Unprompted worldview leak** | At least one stance nobody asked for — evaluative, self-exposing, tangent to the scene's business. | "Maybe capitalism isn't the best way to organise societies." / "I prefer to be cold, because at least you can put on an extra sweater." | Opinions appear only where the scene's move requires them, and resolve to "agree to disagree". |
| **R7** | **Drift inside turns** | Topic movement by associative chaining mid-turn, ending somewhere the turn's opening didn't predict — not only signposted pivots between turns. | Interests → no time → children → money → capitalism, one turn. | All pivots licensed and marked: "Oh — before I forget." / "Actually… while you're here." |
| **R8** | **Vagueness in the right places** | Recall of content is approximate ("a book about a complicated murder mystery"); precision reserved for self-facts ("my son is fifteen"). Fail: crisp prop-specifics everywhere. | "A young man discovers something unusual in the countryside." | "Forty-two, Harbour Road." / "Bennett! A chihuahua, in a little coat." |
| **R9** | **No buttons** | Conversations end where the energy runs out, not on a callback, bow, or mini-joke. Visible fatigue is a plus. | "I'm not finding it easy to think of any more questions!" …ends on "I like books or films with interesting characters in them." | All 16 scenes end on a button: "See — the rain brings out the best in you." |
| **R10** | **Epigram ceiling** | At most ~1 balanced aphorism per conversation; humans don't talk in antithesis. | Zero epigrams in 1,011 words. | "You're not asking, I'm offering." / "Funny how the mistakes make the best stories." — roughly one per scene. |
| **R11** | **Enthusiasm economy** | Exclamations and superlative assessments are scarce; warmth is mostly mild. | 3 exclamation marks / 1,011 words; strongest praise: "That's always really interesting." | 21 exclamations; "That's the best thing I've heard all week." / "You've made my week." |
| **R12** | **Disfluency is structural, not typographic** | Hesitation shows as restarts, re-scoping, "let me think" — not as em-dash-performed interruptions. | "The truth is, I'm not sure. … So, let me think." One dash in the whole text. | 40 em-dashes; "Wait — say that again." — hesitation as punctuation prop. |

**Using it as a gate.** Score a candidate text against all twelve; Aran's raw text
passes 12/12, the pod-1 canon roughly 1/12 (it scrapes R5's cousin in the odd deferred
answer). A working gate for future pod content might demand a strong majority
including *both* distributional checks — but where the bar sits is a taste call for
the founders, not this document. The rubric also works at line level: it is exactly
the instrument that identifies "Ask me next week" and "Don't ask." as pod-1's living
tissue.

One caution, prompted by the a/b/c/d sweep history: a rubric this legible invites
optimisation. Dialogue written *to* the rubric would be a new species of artificial —
which is why R1/R2 are distributional (unfakeable by touch-up) and why §4's options
differ mainly in how much rubric-chasing they expose themselves to.

## 4. The fork: three ways to source pod-1's flesh

The 16-move skeleton stands — it came from the founders' own thinking and nothing in
this analysis touches it. The question is where the *language* that fleshes each move
comes from. Three options, costs and honesty for each, no recommendation.

### Option A — Aran-method: real exchanges per move, machine does structure only

Aran (or another human with real conversational hours behind them) produces the raw
dialogue for each of the 16 moves the same way pod-0.5 was produced — written from
lived conversation, not invented. The machine's role stops at structure: S-LEGO
segmentation, ellipsis baking at C=12, canon tables, variant rows, seeding.

- **Cost.** Founder-adjacent human time — the scarcest resource in the company. The
  honest unit is not "75 hours of conversation"; Aran didn't transcribe those hours,
  he wrote *from the memory of them*. Pod-0.5's ~1,000 words presumably took hours,
  not weeks — but pod-1 needs 16 moves' worth, and several (gossip, disagreement,
  persuasion, banter) are genuinely two-sided in a way pod-0.5's
  learner-monologue-plus-host shape is not. Writing both sides of a real-feeling
  argument from memory is harder than writing one's own biography. Doesn't scale to
  many pods or many known-languages without more Arans.
- **Honesty.** The output arrives in Aran's idiolect — which is exactly what makes it
  pass R2, and also means every pod speaks like Aran. A feature for realness, a
  constraint on variety; whether that constraint matters at pod-1 scale is a taste
  call. Also: pod-0.5's evidence is one data point from one author on the one move
  (first-conversation biography) that most suits the written-from-memory method. And
  the drill-density tension from §2 lands here undiluted — even lived language must
  repeat each move six-to-ten times per scene, which no memory of a real conversation
  will supply unaided.

### Option B — Hybrid: authored drafts, gated by the rubric after a lived-dialogue edit pass by Aran

The model drafts against the skeleton (cheap, fast, already done once); Aran runs an
edit pass whose brief is not polish but *de-typicalisation* — breaking the ping-pong
into floor-holding, replacing prop-specifics with worldview leaks, cutting buttons and
epigrams, letting answers fail; the result must pass the §3 rubric to ship.

- **Cost.** Cheapest in human hours — editing beats authoring, and the machine absorbs
  all structural work. Most of pod-1's existing investment survives.
- **Honesty.** The failure mode is known in advance: texture-sprinkling. An edit pass
  that adds "well"s and dashes will pass the shallow checks and fail R1/R2, which
  can't be faked without genuinely restructuring turns — so the gate holds *if the
  edit pass is licensed to restructure*, merging ping-pong exchanges into monologues
  and deleting whole turns, not just re-flavouring lines. That is a heavier edit than
  "edit pass" usually implies; on some scenes it may approach rewriting, at which
  point Option B quietly becomes Option A with extra steps. The second risk is
  rubric-chasing: dialogue optimised to twelve checks is a new kind of artificial. The
  rubric mitigates but cannot eliminate this — a human ear (Aran's) stays the final
  gate, which is why his name is in the option.

### Option C — Harvest: mine real learner/tutor conversation transcripts

Where real conversations between learners and tutors or community members exist on
record — SSi live lessons, tutor sessions, community calls — mine them for real
instances of each of the 16 moves and build each scene's flesh from harvested
language, lightly cleaned.

- **Cost.** Highest and least certain. No transcript corpus exists in this repo today
  — step one is discovery (do recordings exist? whose?), then consent and
  anonymisation (real names, real biography, real employers are in there), then
  transcription, then the mining itself. Real speech is messy on tape: overlaps,
  half-audible runs, references to off-tape context. And coverage is not guaranteed —
  nobody may perform a clean, excerptable "What's the Word" or "You're Joking!" in the
  hours that happen to be recorded; the rarest moves will be exactly the ones missing.
- **Honesty.** The realness ceiling is the highest of the three — both sides genuinely
  real, multiple idiolects, zero authorship — and it is the only option that produces
  texture *evidence* rather than texture *recall*. But harvested English (or Welsh)
  texture must then survive translation into every target language and the C=12
  S-LEGO machinery, and it will fight that machinery harder than written-from-memory
  text does: the placement analysis showed even Aran's deliberately clean prose had
  56% of sentences over the C=12 ceiling. Verbatim real speech will be worse, and
  every repair chips at the very texture that justified the harvest. There is also a
  quiet category risk: transcripts of *lessons* are institutional talk, not social
  conversation — a tutor scaffolding a learner is its own genre, and it may teach the
  16 social moves in classroom register.

### What each option is a bet on

Not a recommendation — a framing for the discussion. Option A bets that Aran's method
generalises beyond the one move it has been tested on, and that his time is
purchasable at pod scale. Option B bets that realness can be *edited in* under a
rubric that resists faking, without the edit pass silently becoming authorship.
Option C bets that recordings exist, that consent is gettable, and that real texture
survives the pipeline that must eat it. All three inherit the drill-density tension
named in §2, and that tension is upstream of the choice: it is worth the founders
deciding whether six-to-ten drilled repetitions per scene is negotiable *before*
choosing who writes the words.
