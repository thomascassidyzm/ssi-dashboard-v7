# Should inheritance be taxed heavily? (script)

*How to read it:* a **/** marks where the next speaker starts talking over the line. A line ending in **—** is cut off there. Short back-channels like *(Dean: mm)* sit in brackets where they land. [Watch it as a timed replay](https://watson-1.tail4968cb.ts.net/evidence/pod3-group-chat/inherit.html).

*Hugh has just told the table that under the new rules on farms, when he dies his son Will is looking at an inheritance-tax bill that means selling about a third of the land. Jess said, before she could stop herself: 'Good.' Question on the table: should inheritance be taxed heavily, farms and family homes included?*

**HUGH** Good. Well, that's refreshingly clear. Which third would you like, Jess? (Danny: ha) Because the only bit anyone'll pay proper money for is the river meadow, and that goes to a hedge fund in Surrey.

**JESS** I can't afford the meadow, Hugh, I can't afford the bathroom I've got. And yeah, I'd have put it nicer if I'd had a second to think. But you've just told me the only buyer is a hedge fund in Surrey, and somehow that's an argument against the tax and not against the hedge fund. I pay a thousand and fifty a month to a man who got four houses off his mum. Nobody's ever asked him which third he'd like to give up.

**DANNY** Jess, I'm afraid your landlord almost certainly paid. Houses never had relief, his mum's estate will have handed over forty per cent above the threshold, and I'd bet he sold a fifth one to do it. It's Hugh's acres that went through untouched. I had a client, a retired dentist from Cheltenham, who bought two hundred acres he'd never once stood on, purely so his children wouldn't pay a penny. He asked me whether he had to own a wellington boot. So the tax you're cheering isn't new, Jess. It's the end of the loophole Hugh was sharing with the dentist.

**HUGH** Your dentist bought the farm next to mine, I suspect. Paid four times what it'll ever earn in beef. Which is why Will's bill is so large. He's being taxed on the dentist's price.

**RHI** So sell the /dentist—

**FEMI** Not the river meadow you don't. That was under about a metre in February 2020, give or take twenty centimetres. Whoever's paying proper money for it is buying /a seasonal lake.

**RHI** Even better. Sell the dentist the lake.

**SUNNY** While you're all flogging lakes, I'll tell you what this tax looks like from my side of the desk. Last month a son brings his dad in, eighty-four, lovely man, heart's going. And when Dad's in the loo, the son asks me, very quietly, whether I think he'll make it to April. Not because he loves him. Because of the gift they'd signed over and the date it counts from. I've had that question three times since the budget. So tax it or don't, Hugh, but don't tell me it's about land. It's about who's counting the days.

**JESS** Fine, Danny, I'll give you my landlord, he probably paid. Doesn't make me like him. But Sunny, you want to know who else counts the days? My nan had dementia, care home in Headingley, fourteen hundred a week. Her house went in about three years, every brick of it (Sunny: mm), and nobody at that table asked my mum which third she'd like to keep. That's a hundred per cent tax on getting the wrong illness. So when Will loses a third of four hundred acres, I'm sorry, Hugh, I can't find the violin.

**HUGH** I didn't ask for a violin. And your nan's house is the real scandal, I'll give you that freely. I voted for the people who left it there. Twice.

**RHI** Hang on, before we all weep or cheer. My accountant sat me down last month about the firm, same rules for me. Twenty per cent on anything over the million, and you can pay it over ten years, no interest. Ten years, Hugh. I've got lads on my books paying off a van over five at nine per cent. So right, who actually pays, and when? Not Will, not on the day. Will pays it out of what the land earns. And if the land can't find two per cent /a year—

**FEMI** Two per cent of what, though? Of the dentist's valuation, not of what the field actually makes. Those are different numbers. My back garden's worth a fortune on paper and it grew four courgettes. (Rhi: ha)

**HUGH** Femi's right, and it's older than the dentist. My grandfather died in 1961, I was nine. Estate duty, the old sort, no relief worth the name. They sold Pentre, the top farm, eighty acres, to the Forestry Commission, who put the whole lot under Sitka spruce, and my father didn't mention it for thirty years. And here's the awkward bit, Jess. We survived. Rather comfortably. Which proves either that the tax works, or that nobody minds what it costs until it's their name on the auction poster.

**DANNY** The second. In my experience, always the second.

**SUNNY** No, it's not always the second, Danny, that's too easy. My mum and dad had a corner shop in Whitmore Reans, newspapers, paraffin, Mom on the till till she was seventy-one. When Dad died there was no tax, there was nothing to tax, and the one thing they'd made sure of was that none of us would ever have to stand behind that counter. That was the inheritance. Getting out. So I'm asking properly, Hugh. Not about the third. Does Will actually want the other two, or is he just too decent to say?

**HUGH** He wants it. More than I did at his age, frankly. I was meant to be in London ruining something else. That's the bit nobody taxes, Sunny: the /one who stays.

**JESS** Some of us would love /to stay—

**RHI** And some of us stay and nobody gets misty about it. Cai wants the firm, eight vans and a unit on the trading estate. Nobody calls him the one who stays, Hugh. They call him the plumber.

**JESS** I'd settle for being the one who rents.

**DANNY** Rent from whom, Jess? That's the question.

**FEMI** From the council, Danny. My mum rented the same flat in Peckham for twenty-six years. Nobody inherited it, nobody sold it to Surrey. You've both skipped the third option.

**SUNNY** Third option got sold, Femi. Half this town's council houses went in the eighties, and I've got the people who bought them on my list now, leaving them to their kids.

**DANNY** Then they fight over /it—

**HUGH** Ah. Where the real inheritance tax gets paid.

**DANNY** Thank you, Hugh, you've stolen my line. Three brothers, ex-council semi on the Weston estate, bought in 1984 for eleven thousand, worth two-sixty now. No tax due, nowhere near the threshold. They spent forty-one thousand on solicitors arguing about who did their mother's shopping, and two of those invoices were mine. The one who did the shopping got nothing extra. The one with the better barrister got the house. So I'll drop the devil's advocate, Jess. Tax it harder. Not because it's fair. Because the Treasury is a cheaper and far more polite heir than a brother.

**FEMI** Hang on, you've merged two things there, Danny. The tax doesn't stop the fight, it just shrinks the prize. Your brothers were scrapping over two-sixty with nothing due. Lower the threshold, take a big bite, and they're scrapping over a hundred and sixty with the same barristers and the same shopping list. After February 2020 I did a survey on a bungalow down Mill Lane, two sisters, inherited it a month before the water came in. Value dropped by about a third, surveyor wouldn't commit closer than that. Did they stop fighting? Did they hell. Smaller pile, same row. (Danny: mm) It was never about the size of the pile.

**JESS** Right, so if it was never about the size of the pile, Femi, nobody can complain when the Treasury takes most /of it—

**FEMI** Whoa. Most of it? I never said that.

**RHI** You didn't have to say it, Femi. You drew her the drawing and she's read it. Bit late to stamp 'not to scale' across the corner now.

**SUNNY** Leave him be, Rhi, he's allowed to be frightened of his own drawing. But I'll tell you where I've ended up, because I walked in tonight thinking tax the farms and leave people's houses alone. It's Jess's nan that's shifted me. If the state takes every brick off a woman because her brain went, and nothing off the next woman because it was her heart and it was quick, then the diagnosis is setting the tax rate. I'd rather a proper bite out of everybody's house, mine included. My two would get less. They'll cope. I won't, obviously, but that's rather the point of it.

**JESS** No, you're right, Sunny, and I hate that you're right, because I've got to own up to something. Tom's mum has a three-bed in Ilkley. Paid off. And when she had that fall last spring, I sat on the end of our bed and did sums on my phone. Deposit, stamp duty, whether we'd finally get out of the damp. I'm not proud of it. So I'm not cheering from outside, Hugh. I'm the son in Sunny's surgery asking about April. I just want everyone else's mum taxed first. (Rhi: ha)

**HUGH** So when the Ilkley three-bed comes with a bill, Jess, do you sell it to pay? Or do you find, rather suddenly, that Tom's always loved that house?

**JESS** Oh, he'd discover a lifelong passion for Ilkley by teatime. That's my whole point, Hugh. Nobody should get to set the rate while they're sat on the end of the bed with a calculator. Not Tom, /not me—

**RHI** Then who, Jess? Some bloke in Whitehall with no mum? Everybody's on the end of a bed. And it's not only the kids doing sums. Tuesday I'm in a terrace up in Knighton quoting for a heat pump, lovely woman, eighty-odd, living in the back kitchen in two cardigans with the front rooms shut off. Eleven grand after the grant and she says no, because that's coming out of what the grandkids get. She's freezing to keep the house worth more for them. So go on, put your big bite on that house, and tell me she turns the heating on.

**SUNNY** No, Rhi, you've got that backwards. She's freezing now, with no tax on that house at all. Nothing's stopping her except she's decided it already belongs to the grandkids and she's only the caretaker. I see her every January, or her cousin anyway. Chest that won't clear, a fall on the back step because the hall's like a fridge, and then a daughter on the phone asking me why nobody did more. If the state's taking a slice whatever she does, maybe she finally spends her /own money—

**RHI** Maybe. Forty-odd of her I've quoted this year, Sunny, three said yes, and not one of them mentioned tax. You go and tell her it's her money. I've tried. She puts the kettle on.
