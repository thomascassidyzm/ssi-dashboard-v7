*Toby has just said he's thinking of dropping his Spanish module because 'the earbuds translate everything now anyway'. Ana is horrified. Question on the table: is it still worth learning a language when your phone can translate?*

**ANA** Perdona? You're dropping what?

**TOBY** I mean— it's just one module, Mum.

**DEAN** Which module's that then, butt?

**TOBY** Spanish. Just the, like, language one.

**GAZ** Now, hang about, because I had this. Germany, this was, Osnabrück, eighty-nine, no, ninety-one, it was Paderborn by then, and we had a lad in the workshop, Kenny something, wouldn't learn a word. Not one. Said everyone speaks English, what's the point. And he's right, to be fair, mostly they did. But there was this old boy ran the bakery by the gate, every morning, and the rest of us, we'd have a go, terrible German, and he'd be giving us the end bits, the, what d'you call it, the crusts, extra rolls, all sorts. Kenny never got so much as a nod (Dean: ha). Two years. I'm not saying that's the whole argument, but I'll tell you what, it's not nothing, is it.

**MAGGIE** Well, the baker is rather the point, I'd venture, though not for the reason Gareth thinks. It isn't really about the rolls. After the Conquest the clerks wrote in Latin, the court spoke French, and the people who actually ran things, the reeves, the stewards, the ones who knew where the eels were, were the ones who could move between all three. The translating was never the hard part. It was the, what's the word, the being trusted. And I'm not at all sure a pair of earbuds gets you the crusts, Toby.

**DEAN** Crusts. Lovely. What about the eels, mun?

**TOBY** Okay but isn't that kind of romanticising it? Like, trust as a, um, currency. That's basically Foucault, language as power, so if the earbuds /flatten that—

**PRIYA** No but— we use the translate apps in A&E basically every shift, and they're fine right up until someone says chest pain and it comes out as, like, box hurts (Gaz: oh God).

**ANA** Box hurts. You see? And your abuela, Toby, she's going to talk to you at Christmas through a little machine in your ear? Hombre, no. No way.

**DEAN** I had that, box hurts. Well, not that. Job in Townhill last winter, old Polish lady, combi's packed in, she's got no English, I've got no Polish, so out comes the phone, tidy. (Priya: oh no) Turns out it's come out something like I'm going to kill the gas, or, I dunno, kill her, the way she took it. Her grandson's come running down the stairs thinking I'm robbing her. And funny was most of him. The subjunctive tables are dull, I grant you, dreadfully dull, but that's where you learn to wish and hedge and be politely rude, all the things a grandmother notices. And it isn't Foucault, Toby, not really. Nobody in the twelfth century needed Foucault to tell them the man who spoke French got the land.

**TOBY** I did say basically Foucault.

**PRIYA** Okay but the five years thing, Toby, the data's actually really clear on this, there's a study, I think JAMA, I think twenty-nineteen, where they ran discharge instructions through the app and something like eight percent came out clinically wrong, and for some languages it was way worse, like, a third. Do the module.

**TOBY** It's— okay, it's kind of both? Like, it is relevant, there's a whole week on Nozick, the veil of ignorance guy, which is literally my dissertation area, sort of, potentially. At Abuela's I can do the words, I can do pass the bread, but she says something and the whole table laughs and I'm like two seconds behind, and I just laugh anyway so nobody notices. **ANA** Ay, Toby. Why you never told me this?

**DEAN** Go easy, Professor, he's had a week.

**MAGGIE** I am going easy. Toby, two seconds behind is simply what learning a language feels like, for years and years. Hugh was two seconds behind in Italian until the day he died.

**MAGGIE** Every summer. Thirty-one of them. He said, oh, how did he put it, that two seconds behind with people you love beats being punctual with nobody at all.
