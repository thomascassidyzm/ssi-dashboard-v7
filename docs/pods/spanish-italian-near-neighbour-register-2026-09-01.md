# The Spanish/Italian near-neighbour register — the Bologna instrument

**Authored 2026-09-01, consolidated across both Italian pair overlays** — the Method Pod
(`method-pod-italian-pair-overlay-2026-09-01.md`, over
`method-pod-chapters-2026-08-30.md`) and the learning flagship
(`learning-flagship-italian-pair-overlay-2026-09-01.md`). **DRAFT-FOR-ARAN**, like everything
Italian in this programme.

**What this is for.** Tom and Aran sit a cold three-way Zoom with an Italian speaker as the
pre-test; the live Bologna event is the post-test. Both speakers carry a strong Spanish shadow,
so **errors are predicted to cluster at Spanish/Italian near-neighbour selection points** — and
this register is the flagged list of every such point in the two scripts, one row per point,
with **where it occurs** and **what the predicted interference error actually is**. Every claim
in the source chapters' own Near-neighbour lines was re-verified during the overlay pass, not
copied; §6 records the two that were sharpened. References: **M**n = Method Pod chapter n,
**F**n = flagship chapter n.

**Classes** (the established taxonomy, extended where the material demanded):
**anchor** — the Spanish shape matches; ground deliberately held ·
**trap / false friend** — the neighbour exists and is wrong ·
**absence** — Spanish has no neighbour; the learner reaches and finds nothing ·
**systemic remap** — a high-frequency frame built on different machinery ·
**one-moving-part** — a familiar Spanish idiom with a single new element, the most surgical kind.

---

## 0. Where the errors will cluster — the pre-test's predictions, cold

Ranked by expected error volume in a spoken hour:

1. **The clitic system (§1.1–1.3).** *ne*, *ci*, the fused clusters, participle agreement.
   Spanish has none of the first two and fuses differently. Predicted errors: **omission of
   *ne/ci*** (*"necessito tre"* for *me ne servono tre*; *"non sono giovedì"* for *giovedì non
   ci sono*), **\*se lo** for *glielo*, and **no agreement** (*"l'ho perso"* for *l'ho persa*).
   This is where most raw errors will fall — and they are mostly intelligible errors.
2. **The mood flip after opinion verbs (§1.7).** *penso che* + subjunctive where *pienso que*
   takes the indicative. Predicted error: **indicative after penso/credo che** — frequent,
   systematic, invisible to the speaker.
3. **essere/estar and the auxiliaries (§1.5–1.6).** Predicted errors: **\*sto d'accordo**,
   **\*sta arrabbiata**-avoidance panic, and **haber-shaped compound pasts** (*"ha passata
   l'ora"*, *"si ha messo"*).
4. **The false friends (§2).** Lower volume, **highest cost per error** — *battuta, mutuo,
   macchina, fiero, incidente, negozio, fatica, educazione, argomento, tema, bravo* each flip a
   sentence's meaning rather than its grammar. The pre-test should listen for exactly these.
5. **The small-system traps (§1.9–1.14).** *fa* postposed for ago, *finché non*, *non…più*
   order, *qualche* + singular, *di*-linkage after verbs. Steady drizzle, easily coded.

The **anchors** (§4) are the control group: points where Spanish actively helps, so an error
THERE would falsify the interference story rather than confirm it.

## 3. The absences — the learner reaches and finds nothing

Grouped by what the reach feels like; each with its site.

## 5. The one-moving-part points — the most surgical

The learner owns the Spanish idiom exactly; ONE element moves. These are the pre-test's
scalpels — each error is a single phoneme-to-morpheme event, trivially codable:

| the familiar idiom | the one moving part | where |
