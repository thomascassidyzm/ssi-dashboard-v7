# Does each course pick one way and stick to it?

**49 courses scanned for optional features that a language can express two ways.** Survey only — nothing was changed.

---

## The short answer

**It is a few bad batches, not a widespread habit.** Of 49 courses checked across a dozen features, **43 are already consistent** — often to a degree worth naming: Spanish drops the subject pronoun in 6,340 out of 6,340 first-person opportunities, Lebanese Arabic in 154 out of 154 lessons, French never once contradicts itself inside a lesson across 1,886 checked pairs.

Six courses have a real problem, and **four of the six are one authoring batch each** — a day when the convention changed mid-course and nobody noticed. Those are cheap: re-touch the batch, not the course.

**Two are not batches, and those are the ones that need a decision.** Egyptian Arabic drills the opposite of what it teaches, everywhere, in every batch. `eng_for_deu` contradicts itself inside a single day's work.

There is also a finding that is **not a defect and matters more than most of them**: the pipeline can change convention deliberately and keep it perfectly clean. German switches from informal to formal address in a block at the end of each course — 0 disagreements in 1,757 pairs. Japanese does the same for polite forms — 0 in 223. Korean does it for honorifics. When a register switch is *intended*, it comes out spotless. That is what the broken courses should look like.

---

## What needs a decision

**1. Egyptian Arabic — the lesson teaches one thing, the drill teaches the opposite.**
The legos drop the subject pronoun (89% of them). The phrases put it back (54% explicit). A learner is shown `عارف` and then drilled on `أنا عارف`. Disagreement runs **425 of 2,296 pairs — 18.5%**, ten times its MSA and Lebanese siblings. It is elevated in *every* seed range and *every* build date, so there is no batch to roll back. This is the one course needing a full pass.

**2. Portuguese, both courses — one late batch changed the rule.**
Portugal: phrases written 2026-02-10 are 1% explicit; the next day's batch is 37%. Brazil is starker — 5% explicit in March, **53% in the 2026-07-15 batch**, which is also where the seed numbers jump from 1–13% to 33–75%. Recommendation for both: standardise on dropped, re-touch the late batch.

**3. Brazilian Portuguese first-person plural has no majority to defer to.** It sits at 53%/47%. Every other recommendation here says "go with what the course already mostly does." This one has no answer — it needs a human to pick.

**4. Korean for Hindi speakers uses two different honorific registers, one day apart.** Seeds 14–527 (written 27–28 July) use the informal-polite form; seeds 642–653 (written 29 July) use the formal-deferential one. Recommendation: bring the later batch down to match — it agrees with the earlier batch, with *both* sibling Korean courses at the identical seed numbers for identical content, and with the course default.

**5. Seed 87 across all 19 English courses — intentional or not?** "they are people" is expanded in essentially every English-target course, while every neighbouring "they're" lesson is contracted. Same seed, same choice, in courses built months apart. That repetition says template decision, not drift — but it does contradict the pattern used everywhere else in the same course. Worth one word from Kai.

## What was checked and came back empty

Worth recording so nobody pays to look twice:

- **Arabic two-way spellings** — none. What looks like variant spellings is diacritics present in some rows and absent in others: a rendering artefact, not a choice.
- **German dropped final `-e`** (`ich hab` vs `ich habe`) — 487 instances, every one the full form. The feature is never exercised, so it cannot be inconsistent.
- **Chinese measure words and the `了` particle**, four courses — 0 real disagreements.
- **French `il y a` vs `y a`** — a non-finding once a detection bug was fixed.

## Where this survey stops

Honest limits, so the numbers aren't read as covering more than they do:

- **The Japanese topic particle was not tested at all.** A trustworthy test needs a dictionary or morphology table, which was ruled out. It remains an open candidate.
- **French `tu` vs `vous` was deliberately not scored.** It looks like variation at 78%/22%, but the outliers are grammatically forced plurals and deliberate honorific address. Separating true free variation from forced choice was not done — those counts must not be read as a defect.
- **`on`/`nous` could not be measured** in the Japanese- and Chinese-side French courses: no reliable anchor exists on those known sides. Nothing was substituted for it.
- **German past-tense choice was a spot-check of ~40 lessons, not a census.** The pattern found is normal verb-by-verb selection; closing it properly needs a verb-by-verb sweep that was not run.
- **Korean detection used four unambiguous honorific endings only.** Single-syllable markers were rejected because they collide with ordinary words — "again", "start", "time", "office", "mistake", "fact". Rarer honorific forms would be missed.
- **Chinese possessive `的` and the `是...的` frame** — one was spot-checked at 7 instances, the other never reached.
- **One Chinese-target course is thin** (1–12 instances per feature), so its clear verdict carries less weight than its siblings.
- **The calibration example did not reproduce.** The Korean lesson named in the original brief is now fully consistent live. The live analogue was found elsewhere in the same course and is reported above. Live rows were used throughout; no document was treated as authority.

One thing outside this scan worth a separate look: an Egyptian Arabic drill was found containing Lebanese forms. That is dialect mixing, not an optional-feature inconsistency, so it was not measured here.

## How this was measured

Two detectors, both conservative, both eyeballed before anything was counted.

Where the known side is English, the English subject pronoun anchors the question: the lesson says "I found", so does the target say it with the pronoun or without? Where the known side is Japanese, Chinese, Hindi or Tamil — languages that drop pronouns themselves — that anchor is useless, so a second method compares each lesson's own wording against its own drills directly.

**Counts are bucketed by grammatical person, and that turned out to be essential.** Portuguese third person is 98% explicit and Spanish third person 59% — because the verb alone doesn't say who did it. Merging persons into one number would have reported both languages as wildly inconsistent when they are doing exactly the right thing. Only first person, and informal "you", are genuinely optional.

Several false trails were caught and discarded before they became findings: unaccented `tu` and `el` in Spanish are the possessive and the article, not pronouns (≈240 phantom findings avoided); Austrian `des` is a demonstrative, not a genitive; `il n'y a pas` is not a dropped `y a`; "have to" never contracts to "I've to"; and across several languages, bare-stem drilling fragments look like disagreements but are not sentences.
