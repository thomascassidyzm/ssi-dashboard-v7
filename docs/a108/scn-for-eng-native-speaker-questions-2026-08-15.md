# scn_for_eng — questions for a Sicilian speaker

**2026-08-15 · everything below is written in plain English. You do not need to know anything
about how our system works to answer these. Where there is a choice, the candidate sentences are
written out so you can simply pick one — or write a better one.**

Nobody who built this course speaks Sicilian. The 668 Sicilian sentences already existed; what we
did was cut the first 30 of them into teachable pieces. Every question below is a place where the
cutting forced a decision that only a speaker can actually make.

**BLOCKING** means the course currently teaches a guess, and a learner will be repeating it.

---

## A. "his name" and "her name" are the same words — is that right? **BLOCKING**

Seed 20 is *"You want to learn **his** name quickly"* → `voi 'mparari **lu sò** nomu prestu`
Seed 21 is *"Why are you learning **her** name?"* → `pirchì stai 'mparannu **lu sò** nomu?`

Identical Sicilian, two different English words. We have assumed Sicilian simply does not mark
whether the owner is a man or a woman here, so we taught `lu sò` once (at seed 20, glossed "his")
and did **not** teach a second card for "her".

1. Is `lu sò nomu` genuinely both "his name" and "her name"?
2. If a Sicilian speaker wanted to be explicit that it is *her* name, what would they say?
3. Right now a learner is told `lu sò` means "his". When seed 21 asks them for "her name", they
   have never been told that is the same word. **Should we introduce it as "his/her" from the
   start?** That is our recommendation, but it is your call.

## B. `pirchì` is doing two jobs — "why" and "because" **BLOCKING**

Seed 21: *"**Why** are you learning her name?"* → `**pirchì** stai 'mparannu lu sò nomu?`
Seed 22: *"**Because** I want to meet people who speak Sicilian."* → `**pirchì** vogghiu canusciri…`

Across all 668 sentences we count `pirchì` 20 times and `picchì` 2 times, used for both meanings.

1. Is one Sicilian word really both "why" and "because"?
2. If so, is that true in every context, or would a speaker say something different for "because"
   in some sentences?
3. The course currently teaches `pirchì` **only as "why"**, then uses it as "because" one seed
   later without ever saying so. That gap is real and we would like to close it — how should we
   word it?

---

## C. `aju a` is doing two jobs — "I'm going to" and "I have to" **BLOCKING**

Seed 5: *"**I'm going to** practise speaking…"* → `**aju a** fari pràttica parrannu…`
Seed 25: *"…before **I have to** go"* → `…prima ca **aju a** jiri`

1. Is `aju a` genuinely both "I'm going to (do something)" and "I have to (do something)"?
2. If a speaker wanted to be clear they *must* do something rather than merely *will*, is there a
   different way to say it?
3. We taught `aju a` as "I'm going to". Is that the better of the two to lead with?

## D. Where do the little words `mi`, `ti`, `ni` go? **BLOCKING**

The existing sentences put them in two different places, and we could not tell which is the rule
and which is a variation:

- **Stuck on the end of the verb:** `arricurdàrimi` (remember), `aiutàrimi` (help me),
  `sèntirimi` (feel), `pigghiàrimi` (take), `spiàriti` (ask you), `vìdirini` (meet)
- **In front of the verb:** `**mi** pozzu arricurdari` — "I can remember" (seeds 10, 56, 57, 113)

Seed 24 has *both* patterns available and uses the stuck-on-the-end one: `nun aju a putiri
arricurdàrimi`.

1. Are **both** of these correct Sicilian, or is one of them wrong?
2. If both are correct, is one more natural in speech?
3. Would a speaker ever say `pozzu arricurdàrimi` (ending) instead of `mi pozzu arricurdari`
   (front)? We avoided producing that combination because we could not tell.

This matters more than it looks: until it is settled, we are avoiding whole classes of sentence,
which is why the "remember" material is thinner than it should be.

## E. "to meet" is two different verbs — did we split them correctly?

Seed 18: *"We want **to meet** at six o'clock"* → `vulemu **vìdirini** a li sei`
Seed 22: *"I want **to meet** people who speak Sicilian"* → `vogghiu **canusciri** genti…`

We assumed `vìdirini` is *meeting up with someone you already know* and `canusciri` is *getting to
know someone new*. To keep them apart for the learner we taught the second one only inside the
larger chunk "to meet people".

1. Is that distinction right?
2. Is `canusciri` closer to "to get to know" or "to meet"? If the former, we would rather say so
   plainly to the learner.

## F. Is `fari tuttu chiḍḍu ca pozzu` really "to try as hard as I can"?

Seed 7: *"I want **to try as hard as I can** today"* → `vogghiu **fari tuttu chiḍḍu ca pozzu** oggi`

Word for word this looks to us like "to do everything that I can". We taught it as one whole
phrase rather than splitting it, because "try" → `fari` on its own looked wrong.

1. Does `fari tuttu chiḍḍu ca pozzu` sound natural to you for "I'll try as hard as I can"?
2. Is there a more idiomatic Sicilian way to say it?

## G. Two spellings of "to help" — which one should the course teach?

Across the 668 sentences, "help" is spelled two ways:

- `ajut-` — **18 seeds** (62, 63, 74, 142, 168, 171, 172, 176, 203, 204, 344, 448, 491, 564, 605, 645, 654, 660)
- `aiut-` — **8 seeds** (25, 212, 226, 229, 231, 236, 246, 249)

**Seed 25 is in the part we have already built**, so the course currently teaches the *minority*
spelling `aiutàrimi`. We did not change it, because changing your sentences without a speaker's
say-so is not ours to do.

1. Are `ajutari` and `aiutari` both correct, or is one of them a mistake?
2. If we should standardise, which one wins?

*(If the answer is "use `ajut-`", the fix is small and confined: one LEGO and its practice
phrases at seed 25.)*

## J. One thing we could not check at all

Every Sicilian sentence in the built material is either copied from your original 668 or
assembled from pieces of them. That means it is **grammatically consistent with your sentences** —
but consistency is not correctness. If the original translation made the same mistake everywhere,
our checks cannot see it, because nothing contradicts it.

So: **is there anything in the 668 sentences themselves that a Sicilian speaker would not say?**
That question is not answerable by us at all, and it is the one that matters most.
