# Yoruba: our answers, for a Yoruba speaker to correct

**Please disagree with us.** Everything below is our best guess. Nobody on our team speaks Yoruba, so every answer here was reasoned out from the Yoruba we already have written down, not from knowing the language. We have written each answer so that you can see exactly *why* we think it, which means you can tell us exactly *where* we went wrong.

You do not need to know anything about how our software works. Every question is about the language.

**How to answer:** anything is fine — write on this page, send a voice note, or talk it through with whoever gave it to you. "That's fine" is a complete answer. So is "no, nobody says that."

---

## What we are building, in one paragraph

Two courses share one set of 668 Yoruba sentences. One course teaches Yoruba to English speakers; the other teaches Welsh to Yoruba speakers, using those same Yoruba sentences as the prompts. So one Yoruba ruling from you fixes both.

The courses teach in **small pieces that snap together**. We teach a piece like *"I want"* and a piece like *"to speak"*, and the learner joins them up to make sentences we never taught. Learners only ever meet pieces they have already been given — so an answer that is correct Yoruba but needs a word we have not taught yet is a problem for us, and we will say so where it matters.

---

## Two things about how we wrote the Yoruba here

**Tone marks.** We use standard Yoruba spelling with all three tones written: high tone with an acute accent (**á**), low tone with a grave accent (**à**), mid tone left unmarked (**a**). We use the dotted letters **ẹ, ọ, ṣ**, and **ń / ǹ** for the nasal. So *kọ́* and *kọ* and *kọ̀* are three different words to us, not one word written carelessly. If any tone mark below is wrong, that is exactly the kind of thing we need told.

**How to read our confidence.**

| **Medium** | We lean this way and can defend it, but this is a normal place for a speaker to say "no". |

# Part 1 — The two hardest ones

These two are not about vocabulary. They are places where Yoruba and Welsh cut the world differently, and they affect hundreds of sentences, so we have given them the most room.

---

## A. Negation — does Yoruba change when the sentence is about the past?

### The problem

In Welsh, "we don't want" and "we didn't want" are built differently — *dan ni ddim isio* versus *doedden ni ddim isio*. In our Yoruba, both come out **exactly the same**:

> "We don't want to interrupt the story."
> **A kò fẹ́ láti dá ìtàn náà dúró**

> "We didn't want to let anyone hear the truth."
> **A kò fẹ́ láti jẹ́ kí ẹnikẹ́ni gbọ́ òótọ́**

Same opening — *A kò fẹ́* — for present and for past. This happens right through: *"I wasn't thinking"* is **Mi ò ronú**, *"I don't want"* is **mi ò fẹ́**, and nothing in the Yoruba marks which one is which.

### Our answer

**We believe this is correct Yoruba and not a mistake.** We think a negated Yoruba verb simply does not carry a past/present distinction — the time comes from context or from a word like "yesterday" — and that Yoruba draws its line in a *different* place from Welsh: not past versus present, but **future versus everything else**.

Our evidence is that our own Yoruba already behaves that way consistently. Where the English is about the future, the Yoruba does change, to **kò ní**:

> "No I'm sure it will be okay." → **Rárá mo dájú pé kò ní jẹ́ ìṣòro**
> "Even if he wanted to he wouldn't be able to." → **Pàápàá bí ó bá fẹ́ kò ní lè**

And for "is not / was not" before a noun, it uses **kì í ṣe**:

> "That isn't what I'm trying to say." → **Ìyẹn kì í ṣe ohun tí mo ń gbìyànjú láti sọ**

So we count three shapes — plain **kò**, future **kò ní**, and **kì í ṣe** before a noun — and past versus present is not one of the things they separate.

**Confidence: high** that Yoruba does not split negation into past and present the way Welsh does. **Medium** on our claim that **kò ní** is specifically the future one rather than a "will not / would not" that also does other work.

**What we rejected:** we considered that the past sentences might simply be badly translated and ought to carry some past marker. We rejected that because it happens in dozens of sentences written at different times, not in a stray few, and because we could not find any Yoruba past-tense marker anywhere in our 668 sentences.

**Could a speaker reasonably disagree?** Yes, on one specific point. If you tell us there *is* a natural way to mark the past under negation that we have simply never used, that changes a lot for us.

### The two specific things we want from you

**A1.** When you hear **A kò fẹ́ láti jẹ́ kí ẹnikẹ́ni gbọ́ òótọ́** with no other clue, do you hear "we don't want" or "we didn't want", or genuinely either? If a speaker needed to force the past reading, what would they add — a word like *nígbà náà* ("then / at that time"), or something else?

**A2.** Is **kò ní** really the "will not / would not" form? Give us the plain negative future of *"I will speak Yoruba"* in your own words.

### One extra thing we noticed and are unsure about

Four of our sentences put **ń** straight after the negative:

> "I'm not trying to finish as quickly as possible." → **Mi ò ń gbìyànjú láti parí ní kíákíá tó bá ṣeé ṣe**
> "We're not trying to hear many more words." → **A kò ń gbìyànjú láti gbọ́ ọ̀pọ̀lọpọ̀ ọ̀rọ̀ síi**
> "…why it's not working" → **…kí ló dé tí kò ń ṣiṣẹ́**

**A3. Is *kò ń* something people say?** We have read that some speakers reject it and would say plainly **kò ṣiṣẹ́**, and we have also heard it is ordinary in everyday speech. **UNRESOLVED — we are not picking a side.** Only four sentences are affected, so it is cheap to change either way. Please just tell us which you would say.

## B. "When" — the question word and the joining word

### The problem

Welsh uses two different words: *pryd* for the question *"When?"*, and *pan* for the joining word in *"when I get there"*. We wanted to know whether Yoruba merges them.

### Our answer

**Yoruba distinguishes them too, and our sentences already do it consistently and, we believe, correctly.** We do not think there is a problem here at all.

We think **nígbà** is not really a "when" word — it is closer to *"at the time"*, and what follows it does the work:

- **Nígbà wo ni…?** for a question — *"at which time…?"*
  > "When did you start to learn?" → **Nígbà wo ni o bẹ̀rẹ̀ sí kọ́?**
  > "When do you want to start?" → **Nígbà wo ni o fẹ́ bẹ̀rẹ̀?**

- **nígbà tí…** for the joining word — *"at the time that…"*
  > "He doesn't want to be quiet when other people are here." → **Kò fẹ́ láti dakẹ́ nígbà tí àwọn ẹlòmíràn wà níbí**
  > "She was very kind when she saw me feeling nervous." → **Ó ṣe oníyọ̀ọ́nú gan-an nígbà tí ó rí mi tí ẹ̀rù ń bà mí**

Across all 668 sentences, **every** question uses *nígbà wo* and **every** joining "when" uses *nígbà tí*. Not one crossover.

**Confidence: high.**

**What we rejected:** an earlier automatic scan of ours flagged *nígbà* as a word that "means two different things". We now think that scan was simply looking at too small a piece — it was reading *nígbà* on its own, when the real units are *nígbà wo* and *nígbà tí*.

**Could a speaker reasonably disagree?** We do not think so on the main point, but see B2.

### The three things we want from you

**B1.** Confirm that **Nígbà wo ni…?** and **nígbà tí…** are the natural everyday forms, and that we have not picked a stiff or bookish way of saying either.

**B2. This is the part we are least sure of.** We spotted a third kind of "when" that is neither a plain question nor a plain joining word — where "when" introduces something you *don't know*:

> "I'm not sure when I'll be ready." → **Mi ò dájú nígbà tí mo máa ṣetán**
> "They couldn't find out when the film started." → **Wọ́n kò lè mọ̀ nígbà tí fíìmù náà bẹ̀rẹ̀**

Here "when I'll be ready" is the *thing* you're not sure of, not a time at which something happened. Our suspicion is that this should not be **nígbà tí** ("at the time that") but **ìgbà tí** ("the time that"), without the *ní*.

**Confidence: low.** This is a fine distinction and we may have invented it. **Are these two sentences right as they stand, or should they lose the *n*?** Only two sentences are affected.

**B3.** We also noticed our sentences sometimes put **bá** into the "when" clause and sometimes not:

> "It's interesting when you understand enough words." → **nígbà tí o bá mọ̀ àwọn ọ̀rọ̀ tó tó**  *(with bá)*
> "I feel better than I felt when we were in the pub." → **nígbà tí a wà ní agó ọtí**  *(no bá)*

Our reading is that **bá** goes in when the sentence means "whenever / any time that" or is about something not yet real, and stays out when it is one specific thing that actually happened. We think our sentences follow that rule correctly, which is why we are raising it as a compliment rather than a complaint.

**Confidence: medium.** Please confirm the rule, because we will need to teach it.

## 1. Does this say "I'm not sure" or "I don't think"?

We want to teach: **"I'm not sure if I can remember the whole sentence."**
What we have written: **Mi ò rò pé mo lè rántí gbogbo gbólóhùn náà**

### Our answer

**We think this is wrong, and we think it says "I don't think I can remember…", which is a stronger and more definite claim.** We recommend changing it.

This is not a guess from the shape of the words alone. Our own Yoruba uses **Mi ò rò pé** for "I don't think" in more than a dozen other sentences:

> "No I don't think it's very important." → **Rárá mi ò rò pé ó ṣe pàtàkì púpọ̀**
> "I don't think that she can open the door." → **Mi ò rò pé ó lè ṣí ìlẹ̀kùn**

And it uses **completely different words** for "I'm not sure" elsewhere:

> "I'm not sure when I'll be ready." → **Mi ò dájú nígbà tí mo máa ṣetán**
> "But I'm not sure if it's true." → **Ṣùgbọ́n mi ò dá mi lójú bóyá ó jẹ́ òótọ́**
> "Nobody was sure how to answer the question." → **Kò sí ẹni tí ó dá lójú bí a ṣe máa dáhùn ìbéèrè náà**

So the same set of sentences uses *rò pé* for thinking and *dájú / dá…lójú* for being sure — except in three places, where "I'm not sure" was written as *Mi ò rò pé*. We read that as three slips, not as a Yoruba fact.

**Confidence: high** that these three are wrong. **Medium** on exactly what should replace them.

**Our proposed replacement:** **Kò dá mi lójú pé mo lè rántí gbogbo gbólóhùn náà** — roughly "it is not clear to me that I can remember the whole sentence."

**What we rejected, and why we are only medium:** the obvious move was to copy the form already in our sentences, **Mi ò dá mi lójú**. We did not, because it has *mi* twice — once as the subject and once as the object — and we suspect the natural form puts the fact in the subject slot: **Kò dá mi lójú**. But we may be wrong, and if **Mi ò dá mi lójú** is what people actually say, we would rather have that.

**Could a speaker reasonably disagree?** On the replacement, easily. On whether *Mi ò rò pé* means "I don't think", we would be surprised.

**What we want from you:**
- **1a.** Confirm *Mi ò rò pé* = "I don't think", not "I'm not sure".
- **1b.** Give us the plain, ordinary way to say **"I'm not sure if I can remember the whole sentence."** Whatever you say, we will use.
- **1c.** Is **Mi ò dá mi lójú** natural, or should it be **Kò dá mi lójú**?

*(This affects three sentences directly, and it is also the very first negative sentence a learner ever meets — so it matters more than three sentences' worth.)*

## 2. Is *kọ́* "learn" and *kọ* "practise"?

We teach **kọ́** for **"learn"** and **kọ** for **"practise"** — the same letters, different tone.

### Our answer

**We are confident *kọ́* is "learn". We do not believe *kọ* means "practise", and we think this one needs replacing.**

Our doubt: we can find *kọ* used elsewhere in our own sentences for **"write"** —

> "He wanted to write a letter to his friend last week." → **Ó fẹ́ kọ lẹ́tà sí ọ̀rẹ́ rẹ̀ ní ọ̀sẹ̀ tó kọjá**

— and we cannot find any support for it meaning "practise". It is used that way in four of our sentences, always as **kọ sísọ** for "practise speaking", so it was clearly a deliberate choice by whoever wrote them; we just do not think it is right.

**Confidence: high** on *kọ́* = learn. **Medium** that *kọ* = practise is wrong — deliberate and repeated is not nothing, and we may be missing a real usage.

**What we rejected:** we considered that this might be *kọ́* with a dropped tone mark, giving "learn speaking", which at least means something. We think that is more likely than "practise", but it is still not what the English says.

**What we want from you:**
- **2a.** Is the tone right on **kọ́** = learn?
- **2b.** What is the ordinary way to say **"to practise speaking"** and **"I'm going to practise speaking with someone else"**? We have **Mo máa kọ sísọ pẹ̀lú ẹlòmíràn**.
- **2c.** Whatever word you give us, does it take an object — can we say "practise speaking Yoruba" with it? (See question 5.)

## 3. When do you need *láti*?

We teach **Mo fẹ́** for "I want" and **láti sọ** for "to speak", so a learner can join them: **Mo fẹ́ láti sọ**.

### Our answer

**We now think *láti* after *fẹ́* is probably wrong, and that plain *Mo fẹ́ sọ* is the natural form.** This is a change of mind on our part, and it is uncomfortable, because *Mo fẹ́ láti sọ* is the **first sentence of the course**.

What changed our mind is counting. Across all 668 sentences, *fẹ́* is followed by *láti* about 22 times, and by a bare verb with no *láti* about five times as often:

> "She wants to find out what the answer is." → **Ó fẹ́ mọ ohun tí ìdáhùn náà jẹ́**
> "When do you want to start?" → **Nígbà wo ni o fẹ́ bẹ̀rẹ̀?**
> "You want to learn his name quickly." → **O fẹ́ kọ́ orúkọ rẹ̀ yárayára**
> "Do you want to go to a restaurant tonight?" → **Ṣé o fẹ́ lọ sí ilé oúnjẹ lálẹ́ yìí?**

Our reading: *láti* is a "in order to" word and belongs after verbs like *gbìyànjú* ("try") and *nílò* ("need"), but *fẹ́* takes the verb straight. Where *Mo fẹ́ láti…* appears, we suspect it is following English "want **to**".

**Confidence: medium.** The count is one-sided, but we cannot rule out that both are fine and one is just more formal.

**Could a speaker reasonably disagree?** Very much so, and this is one where we would especially like to be told if we are overreacting.

**What we want from you:**
- **3a.** Which would you say: **Mo fẹ́ sọ èdè Yorùbá** or **Mo fẹ́ láti sọ èdè Yorùbá**?
- **3b.** If both are possible, is one of them more formal, more written, or more emphatic?
- **3c.** Is there a rule for when *láti* is needed at all? We would love a short answer we can teach.

## 15. Is *búrúkú* the right word for "ugly"?

Three sentences use it:

> "The church is ugly." → **Ṣọ́ọ̀sì náà búrúkú**
> "I think the small church is very ugly." → **Mo rò pé ṣọ́ọ̀sì kékeré náà búrúkú púpọ̀**

### Our answer

**We think this is wrong, on two counts.**

First, meaning: we believe **búrúkú** is about being *bad* or *wicked* — a bad person, bad luck — not about being unpleasant to look at.

Second, and we are less sure here: we think **búrúkú** normally sits *in front of* what it describes, attached to a noun (*ènìyàn búrúkú*, "a bad person"), and that using it as the whole of "the church **is** ugly" may not be how it works.

**Our proposal:** say it the other way round — **Ṣọ́ọ̀sì náà kò lẹ́wà** ("the church is not beautiful"), and for "very ugly", **kò lẹ́wà rárá** ("not beautiful at all").

**Confidence: medium-high** that *búrúkú* is the wrong word. **Low** on our replacement.

**What we rejected:** leaving it, on the grounds that three consistent uses looked deliberate. We think it is one translator's habit repeated three times rather than three independent decisions.

**A note for us, not for you:** turning "ugly" into "not beautiful" changes the shape of the sentence, which matters for how we teach it. So if there is a straightforward single word for ugly, that is much better for us than a negative phrase — please say so if there is one.

**What we want from you:**
- **15a.** Does *búrúkú* cover "ugly to look at"?
- **15b.** How would you say **"the church is ugly"**? A single word if there is one.

## 13. Should "Sunday" be an English word?

Four sentences write the day as **ọjọ́ Sunday**:

> "I didn't do much on Sunday." → **Mi ò ṣe púpọ̀ ní ọjọ́ Sunday**

### Our answer

**We think this is an oversight, not a decision, and we recommend the Yoruba name.**

The reason is not a general view about loanwords — it is that **every other day of the week in these sentences already uses its Yoruba name**:

| Day | What our sentences use |
|---|---|
| Monday | **ọjọ́ ajé** |
| Tuesday | **ọjọ́ Ìṣẹ́gun** |
| Wednesday | **ọjọ́rú** |
| Thursday | **ọjọ́bọ̀** |
| Friday | **ọjọ́ Ẹtì** |
| Saturday | **ọjọ́ Àbámẹ́ta** |
| **Sunday** | **ọjọ́ Sunday** ← the only English one |

Six days in Yoruba, one in English, in a set of sentences written by the same hand. We read that as a gap rather than a policy.

**Our proposal:** **ọjọ́ Àìkú**.

**Confidence: high** that the inconsistency is accidental. **Medium** on *Àìkú* being the form you would use rather than some other version of the name.

**What we rejected:** the argument that English day names are genuinely common in everyday spoken Yoruba — which we accept is true. We rejected it here only because the other six days would then also be in English, and they are not.

**What we want from you:**
- **13a.** Confirm **ọjọ́ Àìkú**, or give us the form you would say.
- **13b.** Separately, and this is a real question about how the course should feel: given that people **do** say "Sunday" in conversation, should a course teach the Yoruba name anyway? We will follow your view.

## 14. Does this add a "they" that isn't in the English?

We want: **"So that I don't get left behind."**
We have: **Kí wọ́n má bàa fi mí sílẹ̀**

### Our answer

**We think the *wọ́n* here is not really "they", and that this is fine as it stands.**

Our reading: Yoruba does not have an easy way to say "get left behind" without saying who does the leaving, and **wọ́n** is doing the job that English does with a vague "they" or "people" — not pointing at any particular group.

**Confidence: medium.**

**An alternative we would accept happily:** turn it round so nobody is doing anything to you — something on the lines of **Kí n má bàa kù sẹ́yìn** ("so that I don't remain behind"). We like this better because it matches the English more closely, but we are **low** confidence that it is natural.

**What we want from you:** which of the two would you say, or is there a third way?

## 4. Where does *gidigidi* ("hard") go?

> "I want to try as hard as I can today." → **Mo fẹ́ láti gbìyànjú gidigidi bí mo ṣe lè lónìí**
> "We must work hard to learn a lot of new words." → **A gbọdọ̀ ṣiṣẹ́ gidigidi láti kọ́ ọ̀pọ̀ àwọn ọ̀rọ̀ tuntun**

### Our answer

**The position is right — straight after the verb — but we suspect the word is not quite "hard".**

On position we are **high** confidence: all three uses in our sentences put it immediately after the verb, and it reads consistently.

On the word we are **medium**: our understanding is that *gidigidi* means "greatly / very much / intensely", which is close to "hard" but not the same as the effort sense in "work hard". We have a feeling there is a more specific word for putting effort in.

**What we want from you:**
- **4a.** Is **ṣiṣẹ́ gidigidi** how you would say "work hard", or is there a better word?
- **4b.** Is **gbìyànjú gidigidi bí mo ṣe lè** ("try as hard as I can") natural, or is it word-for-word English? This one we are particularly suspicious of.

## 6. Is *tó bá ṣeé ṣe* a phrase that can stand alone?

The full phrase **lóòrèkóòrè tó bá ṣeé ṣe** means "as often as possible". We split it so the learner can reuse **tó bá ṣeé ṣe** ("as possible") elsewhere.

### Our answer

**Yes — and our own sentences already prove it, which is why this is our most confident answer on the page.** *tó bá ṣeé ṣe* attaches to four different things across the corpus:

> "as soon as you can" → **ní kíákíá tó bá ṣeé ṣe**
> "as quickly as possible" → **ní kíákíá tó bá ṣeé ṣe**
> "as much as possible" → **iye tí ó pọ̀ tó bá ṣeé ṣe**
> "for as long as possible" → **A yẹ kí a dakẹ́ tó bá ṣeé ṣe**

That last one attaches it straight to a verb with no adverb in between, which is the strongest evidence that it is a free-standing piece and not glued to *lóòrèkóòrè*.

**Confidence: high.**

**What we want from you:** just a sanity check that all four of those sound like real Yoruba.

## 7. Where does *díẹ̀* ("a little") go?

We put it after the thing it describes: **èdè Yorùbá díẹ̀** ("a little Yoruba").

### Our answer

**Yes, after — high confidence.** It is used that way in 28 sentences with no exceptions: *àkókò díẹ̀* (a little time), *ọ̀rọ̀ díẹ̀* (a few words), *ìṣẹ́jú díẹ̀* (a few minutes), *ìgbà díẹ̀* (a while).

**But we want you to look at one sentence in particular**, because it looks garbled to us:

> "Could you say that again a little more slowly?" → **Ṣé o lè sọ ìyẹn lẹ́ẹ̀kan síi díẹ̀ síi díẹ̀díẹ̀?**

That has *díẹ̀* twice and *síi* twice. **We think this one is wrong. Confidence: medium.** How would you say it?

## 8. "Speak Yoruba" vs "say something *in* Yoruba" — is *ní* needed?

> "I want to speak Yoruba" → **sọ èdè Yorùbá** (no *ní*)
> "how to say something in Yoruba" → **sọ nǹkan ní èdè Yorùbá** (with *ní*)

### Our answer

**We think this is a real difference and both are right.** Our reading: in the first, the language is the *thing being spoken* — it is the object of the verb. In the second, the thing being said is *something*, and the language is the medium, which needs *ní* ("in").

**Confidence: medium-high.** It matches how the same distinction works in English and Welsh, which is not proof of anything about Yoruba, and that is why we are not saying high.

**What we want from you:** confirm, and tell us whether **sọ nǹkan ní èdè Yorùbá** is the natural phrasing or whether you would put it differently.

## 9. "How to speak" and "how to say" — and whose "how"?

We use **bí mo ṣe máa sọ** for both "how to speak" and "how to say".

### Our answer — two parts, and the second is the important one.

**On "speak" versus "say": we think one Yoruba word covers both, and that is fine.** *sọ* appears in about 97 of our sentences and covers speaking a language and saying a thing. Welsh needs two verbs; Yoruba does not. **Confidence: medium-high.**

**On the hidden "I": you were right to be suspicious, and we now think we are teaching this under a misleading label.**

The frame is **bí [somebody] ṣe máa [verb]**, and the pronoun in the middle really does change. Our own sentences do it:

> "There's no point worrying about how **you're** going to pay." → **…bí **o** ṣe máa san owó**
> "Nobody was sure how to answer the question." → **…bí **a** ṣe máa dáhùn ìbéèrè náà**

So **bí mo ṣe máa sọ** means "how **I** will speak", not a general "how to speak". We are labelling it wrongly for learners, and we intend to fix that on our side by teaching the pronoun as a piece that can be swapped.

**Confidence: high** that the pronoun is a changeable slot.

**What we want from you:**
- **9a.** Confirm that *sọ* really does cover both "speak" and "say".
- **9b.** Confirm the frame is **bí [pronoun] ṣe máa [verb]** and that swapping *mo* for *o* / *a* / *wọ́n* is all you have to do.
- **9c.** Is **máa** required in that frame, or can it be left out?

## 10. Is *sí* stuck to the end?

We teach **ohun tí mo túmọ̀ sí** for "what I mean".

### Our answer

**We think *sí* belongs to the verb — the verb is *túmọ̀ sí*, not *túmọ̀* — and it lands at the end here only because of the shape of this particular phrase.** Our own sentences show it in the middle when there is something after it:

> "They could mean that they want you to ask." → **Wọ́n lè túmọ̀ sí pé wọ́n fẹ́ kí o béèrè**
> "But that doesn't mean we can't change it." → **Ṣùgbọ́n èyí ò túmọ̀ sí pé a ò lè yí i padà**

So *sí* is not "stuck at the end"; it follows *túmọ̀* directly, and in *ohun tí mo túmọ̀ sí* there is simply nothing after it.

**Confidence: high** on that.

**But we have a separate doubt about the whole phrase.** We suspect **túmọ̀ sí** leans towards "signify / translate as", and that "what I mean" in the sense of "what I'm getting at" might be said another way entirely.

**Confidence: medium** on that doubt.

**What we want from you:** how would you say **"I'm going to try to explain what I mean"**?

## 12. Should the course be familiar or respectful?

### First, a correction to our own earlier note

We previously told people this course speaks to the listener in the familiar form all the way through. **That was wrong, and we are glad we checked.** The later material already switches to the respectful form:

> "Do you want, sir?" → **Ṣé ẹ fẹ́, ọ̀gá?**
> "What do you think, madam?" → **Kí ni ẹ rò, ìyá àgbà?**
> "I can help you, madam." → **Mo lè ràn yín lọ́wọ́, ìyá àgbà**

So there is already a respectful section, using **ẹ** and **yín** with *ọ̀gá* and *ìyá àgbà*. There is also a "you all" section using **gbogbo yín**.

### Our answer

**We think this is the right structure — familiar first, respectful introduced later as its own thing — and our question to you is only about the timing and the wording.**

**Confidence: medium** on the structure being right. This is a judgement call about the course, and we would genuinely rather it were yours than ours.

**What we want from you:**
- **12a.** For adults learning Yoruba, often to speak with family and with elders, is familiar-first-then-respectful the right order? Or should respectful come much earlier, given how quickly a learner might need it?
- **12b.** Are **ọ̀gá** and **ìyá àgbà** the right ways to address a man and a woman respectfully in the situations a learner will meet? *ìyá àgbà* looks to us like it might specifically mean an older woman or a grandmother rather than a general polite "madam" — is that right?
- **12c.** Is **ẹ ṣé** the ordinary polite "thank you"?

## 16. The word for "know" is written two different ways

Most of our sentences write "know" as **mọ̀**, with the low-tone mark:

> "I don't know those people." → **Mi ò mọ̀ àwọn ènìyàn wọ̀nyẹn**

But nine of them write it as **mọ**, with no tone mark at all — and eight of those nine are clustered together in one block of sentences:

> "I know a young man who wants to work with you." → **Mo mọ ọkùnrin ọ̀dọ́ kan tí ó fẹ́ ṣiṣẹ́ pẹ̀lú rẹ**
> "I don't need to know everything." → **Mi ò nílò láti mọ gbogbo nǹkan**

And one sentence uses **both**, in the same breath:

> "I know a young woman who knows your sister." → **Mo mọ obìnrin ọ̀dọ́ kan tí ó mọ̀ arábìnrin rẹ**

### Our answer

**We think the nine without the mark are simply missing a tone mark, and should all be *mọ̀*.** The clustering is what convinces us — they look like one batch written in a hurry rather than nine separate decisions.

**Confidence: medium-high.**

**Why this matters more than nine sentences:** we had been telling ourselves that the tone marks in this material were all present and correct. Nine sentences say "present" and "correct" are not the same thing, and that changes how carefully we need someone to read the rest.

**What we want from you:**
- **16a.** Is "to know" **mọ̀**? Should all nine be corrected?
- **16b.** Or is there a real distinction between *mọ* and *mọ̀* that we are blind to?

## 17. Does Yoruba distinguish knowing a fact from knowing a person?

Welsh has two separate verbs — *gwybod* for facts, *nabod* for people. Our Yoruba uses **mọ̀** for both:

> "I don't know why you think that it's so good." → **Mi ò mọ̀ ìdí tí o fi rò pé ó dára tó bẹ́ẹ̀**  *(a fact)*
> "Do you know my sister's friend?" → **Ṣé o mọ̀ ọ̀rẹ́ arábìnrin mi?**  *(a person)*

### Our answer

**We believe Yoruba uses one verb for both, and that this is simply a distinction Yoruba does not make.**

**Confidence: medium-high.**

**Why we are asking anyway:** this is a genuine headache for the Welsh-for-Yoruba course, because a Yoruba speaker will see the same word *mọ̀* and have to produce two different Welsh verbs. If you tell us Yoruba *does* have a way to signal "know a person" specifically, that would help us enormously.

**What we want from you:** is there any difference at all — a different verb, or a different way of putting it — between knowing a fact and knowing a person?

# Part 5 — What we could not answer, and what we did not check

We would rather be blunt about the edges of this than let it look more complete than it is.

**Nobody involved in producing this document speaks Yoruba.** Every answer above is inference from the written sentences plus general knowledge. The confidence ratings are our honest estimate of our own reliability, not a measurement of anything.

**We have only looked at the written words.** We have not heard any of these sentences spoken. Anything that depends on how a thing *sounds* — rhythm, emphasis, whether a phrase is stiff or warm — is completely outside what we can judge.

**We have not checked the tone marks sentence by sentence.** We found the nine "know" cases (question 16) because we happened to be counting that word. There are 668 sentences and we have looked closely at perhaps 150. **We cannot tell you the rest are right.** A speaker reading the whole set for tone marks alone would be worth more than everything above.

**Three questions are genuinely unresolved and we have not picked a side:**

- Whether **kò ń** is something people say (question A3)
- Whether **nígbà tí** or **ìgbà tí** belongs in "I'm not sure when I'll be ready" (question B2)

**One question we cannot ask you at all**, because it is not about Yoruba: the Welsh-for-Yoruba course leaves the name of the Welsh language in English, as **èdè Welsh**, in fourteen sentences. Whether Welsh should have a Yoruba name, and what it should be, is worth asking — but it is a different kind of question and we did not want to bury it here.
