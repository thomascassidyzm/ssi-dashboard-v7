# Questions for our Yoruba speakers

Thank you for helping with this. We are building a Yoruba course for English speakers, and we have hit a set of questions that **only a Yoruba speaker can answer**. Nobody on our team speaks Yoruba, so rather than guess, we have written them down.

You do not need to know anything about how our software works. Every question below is just about the language.

**How to answer:** anything is fine — write on this page, send a voice note, or talk it through with whoever passes it on. "That's fine" is a complete answer. So is "no, nobody says that."

A note on how the course works, because it explains why some questions look odd: learners are taught in **small pieces that snap together**. We teach a piece like *"I want"* and a piece like *"to speak"*, and the learner joins them up to make new sentences we never explicitly taught. That is why we care so much about whether a piece still works when it is moved somewhere new.

## Part 1 — Sentences we think may be wrong

### 1. Does this sentence say "I'm not sure" or "I don't think"?

We want to teach: **"I'm not sure if I can remember the whole sentence"**

What we have written is: **Mi ò rò pé mo lè rántí gbogbo gbólóhùn náà**

Our worry: we think this actually means **"I don't *think* I can remember the whole sentence"** — which is a stronger, more definite statement than "I'm not sure".

- Are those the same thing in Yoruba, or different?
- If they are different, **how would you say "I'm not sure if..."?**

*(This one matters a lot — the same phrase is used in several places.)*

### 2. Is *kọ́* "learn" and *kọ* "practise"?

We are teaching two words that look almost identical but have different tone marks:

- **kọ́** — we are using this for **"learn"**
- **kọ** — we are using this for **"practise"**

Have we got those the right way round? Is the tone mark on each one correct?

## Part 2 — Do these pieces still work when we move them?

### 3. When do you need *láti*?

We teach **Mo fẹ́** for *"I want"*, and separately **láti sọ** for *"to speak"*, so a learner can join them into **Mo fẹ́ láti sọ** (*"I want to speak"*).

But elsewhere we have sentences like **Ó fẹ́ mọ** (*"he wants to know"*) with **no** *láti*.

- Is *láti* always allowed after *fẹ́*, or does it depend on the verb that follows?
- Would **Mo fẹ́ sọ** also be correct, or does it have to be **Mo fẹ́ láti sọ**?

### 4. Where does *gidigidi* ("hard") go in a sentence?

We are teaching **gidigidi** for *"hard"* (as in "trying hard"). Using it, we have built sentences like:

> **Mo ń gbìyànjú gidigidi láti sọ èdè Yorùbá**
> *(we mean: "I'm trying hard to speak Yoruba")*

- Is *gidigidi* in the right place there, or should it come at the end?
- Would you say it differently?

### 5. Can *kọ sísọ* take an object?

We teach **kọ sísọ** for *"to practise speaking"*. We have then built:

> **kọ sísọ èdè Yorùbá** — *"to practise speaking Yoruba"*

- Does that work, or does it need to be said another way?

### 6. Is *tó bá ṣeé ṣe* a phrase that can stand alone?

The full phrase **lóòrèkóòrè tó bá ṣeé ṣe** means *"as often as possible"*.

We have split it into two pieces so the learner can reuse them: **lóòrèkóòrè** (*"often"*) and **tó bá ṣeé ṣe** (*"as possible"*).

- Does **tó bá ṣeé ṣe** make sense on its own, or does it only work stuck to *lóòrèkóòrè*?

### 7. Where does *díẹ̀* ("a little") go?

We teach **díẹ̀** for *"a little"*, and we put it after the thing it describes: **èdè Yorùbá díẹ̀** (*"a little Yoruba"*).

- Is that the right position?
- We have also built **mo lè rántí èdè Yorùbá díẹ̀** (*"I can remember a little Yoruba"*). Does that sound natural?

## Part 3 — Things that look inconsistent to us

### 8. "Speak Yoruba" vs "say something *in* Yoruba" — is *ní* needed?

We have two sentences that treat this differently:

- **Mo fẹ́ láti sọ èdè Yorùbá** — *"I want to speak Yoruba"* (no *ní*)
- **bí mo ṣe máa sọ nǹkan ní èdè Yorùbá** — *"how to say something in Yoruba"* (with *ní*)

- Is that a real difference in Yoruba, or is one of them wrong?

### 9. "How to speak" and "how to say" — same words?

We have used **bí mo ṣe máa sọ** for *both* **"how to speak"** and **"how to say"**.

- Is that right — does the same Yoruba cover both?
- Also: this phrase contains **mo** ("I"), so it seems to literally mean *"how **I** speak"*. If a learner wanted to say *"how **you** speak"*, would this phrase change? We may be teaching it under a misleading English label.

### 10. Is *sí* stuck to the end?

We teach **ohun tí mo túmọ̀ sí** for *"what I mean"*.

- Does the **sí** have to sit at the very end of that phrase, or can it move?
- If a learner wanted to say something longer, would the *sí* still go last?

### 11. Does this sentence work?

> **Mi ò rò pé mo sọ èdè Yorùbá**

We built this to mean something like *"I don't think I speak Yoruba"*.

- Is that a sentence a person would actually say?

## Part 4 — A choice about politeness

### 12. Should the course be familiar or respectful?

Right now the whole course speaks to the listener in the **familiar** form — using **o** and **rẹ** for "you" — all the way through.

We know Yoruba also has a respectful form (**ẹ**) used with elders and people you don't know well.

- For adults learning Yoruba, often to talk to family and to elders, is familiar-throughout the right choice?
- Or should some of the course teach the respectful form, and if so, roughly when?

*(There is no wrong answer here — this is a judgement call about the course, and we would rather it were yours than ours.)*

## Part 5 — Three more, from a sweep of the rest of the course

These come from later material we have not built yet, but you are the right people to settle them and we would rather ask once.

### 13. Should "Sunday" be an English word?

Four sentences use the English word **Sunday** inside otherwise-Yoruba sentences, rather than a Yoruba day name:

> **...ní òwúrọ̀ ọjọ́ Sunday** — *"on Sunday morning"*

It is written the same way in all four places, so it looks deliberate rather than a slip, and we know English day names are common in everyday spoken Yoruba.

- Is that what you would say, or should the course teach the Yoruba day name (**Ọjọ́-àìkú**)?
- If people really do say "Sunday" in conversation, should a course still teach the Yoruba name?

*(No other English words appear anywhere in the course except the name "Jane", which is a person's name.)*

### 14. Does this add a "they" that isn't in the English?

We want to say: **"So that I don't get left behind"** — in English, it doesn't say *who* might leave you behind.

What we have is: **Kí wọ́n má bàa fi mí sílẹ̀**

That looks to us like it says **"so that *they* don't leave me behind"**, with a "they" that the English never mentions.

- Does Yoruba need someone named there, so this is just how it must be said?
- Or is there a way to say it without specifying who?

### 15. Is *búrúkú* the right word for "ugly"?

We want: **"I think the small church is very ugly"**

We have used **búrúkú púpọ̀** for *"very ugly"*.

Our doubt: we think **búrúkú** usually means **bad** — a bad person, bad luck — rather than *ugly* in the sense of how something looks.

- Is *búrúkú* right here, or is there a better word for something being ugly to look at?
