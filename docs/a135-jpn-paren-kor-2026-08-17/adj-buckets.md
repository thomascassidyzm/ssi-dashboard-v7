# A-135 — the Japanese known side is carrying the author's grammar notes, and the TTS reads them out

**17 August 2026. Read + judge only.** 1,193 rows in seven Japanese-known courses have a
parenthesis in learner-facing known text, and a second surface the brief did not cover — the
decomposition tiles on the LEGO card — carries 230 more. Below: what each distinct parenthetical
is, whether it may be removed, and the 169 rows where removing it would break the ZUT rail and
so must not be touched.

## The verdict in one paragraph

Two thirds of these parentheses are the author's own working notes — 過去分詞, 不定詞, 接続法,
与格, 分離前綴り, and in Spanish the target verb's own infinitive (`ayudar三人称過去接続法`) —
printed on the learner's card. **494 of them are not merely printed but spoken**, inside the
prompt clip, in a live beta course: the Japanese voice says *iku — futeishi* and the learner is
producing against that. That is bucket A and it is a real defect. But a third of them are
load-bearing — the person markers （私は）（あなたが）（彼らは） that a pro-drop language needs
before a person-inflected target form is determinate — and a blanket strip would delete them.
Worse, **169 rows are held by nothing but their grammar label**: strip 「知っていた（過去）」→wusste
and 「知っていた（過去形）」→kannte together and one Japanese prompt now has two German answers.
That is a manufactured ZUT violation in a live course, and it is the reason this job was worth
adjudicating rather than sedding.

## The decision rule I applied

Two tests, in order. I applied them to every distinct string and I am naming them because the
gray cases turn on which test fires:

1. **Message or form.** CONTENT names part of the *message the learner intends* — who the
   subject is, which sense of a polysemous known word, which register. ANNOTATION names a
   property of the *target form* — its person/tense/mood/case/gender morphology, its part of
   speech, its lemma, its phonological environment, its contraction.
2. **Producibility** (ralph, *Conservative Suppression & Honest Glosses*: "a debut must hand the
   learner a producible intention, never a grammar label"). If acting on the parenthetical
   requires grammar terminology, it is ANNOTATION even when the information it carries is real.

Test 2 is what sends 一人称 to bucket A while （私が） stays in bucket C, even though the two
carry *identical* information. That is the whole shape of the fix: the information is usually
worth keeping and the wording almost never is. Which is why the largest single action here is
not `strip` but `rewrite` — 213 rows where the person label is replaced by the course's own
person marker.

Two places where I differ from the brief's worked examples, with reasons:

- The brief lists 「（君は・丁寧）」 and 「（彼女は・私に）」 as MIXED. I judge both **pure CONTENT**.
  In 「～したいですか（君は・丁寧）」→`gostarias de`, 丁寧 is not the addressee register (the
  addressee is 君, informal `tu`) — it is the softened *request*, which is a message the learner
  intends. In 「送ってくれた（彼女は・私に）」→`enviou-me`, both halves are arguments of the verb:
  subject and indirect object. Neither has a grammar-label half to drop, so there is nothing to
  partially rewrite.
- I do treat register (丁寧, 口語, 改まった) as content, with a caveat: it is metalinguistic in
  *form*, and the method would rather see the register carried by the pronoun choice (君 vs
  あなた) than by a tag. That is an authoring improvement, not a defect, and I did not fold it in.

## The ZUT trap — the part that can sink the fix

I built the post-strip corpus as **the whole course**, not just the affected rows: all 50,843 lego and practice-phrase rows in the seven courses, with the 1,193 carrying their
proposed text. That matters — a stripped prompt collides just as easily with a row that never
had a parenthesis, and grouping only the census rows against each other misses most of it.

- **157 collision groups** in total.
- **100 of them are on the production surface** (legos + build/use phrases). These hold 169 rows.
- 57 exist only among `phrase_role=component` rows. Not a production fork — see the next section.
- Of the 100 production groups, **38 would be manufactured by this fix** and 62 land on a prompt that **already** carries several target forms today.

That second number is the finding I did not expect and it changes the shape of the job.

## The families I held, and why

### 1. Target-internal agreement — no Japanese message difference exists (largest family)

German case, Romance gender and number on articles, possessives and adjectives:
「その（男性形）」→`der` vs 「その（与格）」→`dem`; 「あなたの」→`deine`/`deinem`/`deiner`/`deinen`;
「私の」→`minha`/`meu`/`as minhas`; 「新しい（複数女性形）」→`nuevas` vs 「（単数男性形）」→`nuevo`.
**Japanese cannot express these as a message, because they are not messages** — they are
agreement with a governing noun or a preposition. No rewrite exists, in principle, and I did not
invent one. The real fix is upstream and it is ralph Principle 3: agreement is a
construction-feature, taught in context inside an M-LEGO, never a bare debut with a gloss.
These rows should not exist in their present shape at all; a paren strip is the wrong tool.

### 2. Sense splits wearing a tense label

「知っていた（過去）」→`wusste` vs 「知っていた（過去形）」→`kannte` is `wissen` vs `kennen`;
「～だった（estar過去形）」→`estaba` vs 「（ser過去形）」→`era`; 「知っている（conocer三人称現在形）」
vs 「（saber三人称現在形）」. The label is doing a job the label cannot name honestly. The fix is a
Japanese sense split (acquaintance vs knowing-a-fact; state vs identity) and it is real authoring
against what each seed has already taught, so I propose the direction and not the string.

### 3. Tense/aspect pairs where I believe a rewrite exists but will not assert it

「見た（過去形）」→`sah` vs 「見た（過去分詞）」→`gesehen`, and 「聞いた」/「できた」/「言った」 alike.
The method's own answer is 聞いた vs 聞いていた — a Japanese contrast, not a label. I can see the
shape of it; I cannot verify that the contrasting Japanese form is already taught at that seed,
or that it does not collide with a third row. Held with the direction recorded per row in
`suggested_direction`. This is the family most worth a Japanese-speaking author's afternoon.

### 4. The label-only rows (no gloss to fall back on)

Covered above: 31 rows and tiles where the label *is* the content. Needs a gloss written.

### 5. Corrupt and wrong-language rows

The 3 truncated and 2 English rows. Not this defect.
